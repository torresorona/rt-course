# NotebookLM Source: Initiating & Adjusting Ventilatory Support

This lesson covers how to set up a patient on mechanical ventilation for the first time and how to adjust settings based on their response. It is the most calculation-heavy lesson in Critical Care II, so mastering the formulas and their clinical rationale is essential.

## Mode Selection

When a patient is first intubated, the standard initial mode is Assist/Control, also called A/C or CMV. This is the rest mode — the ventilator assumes all or most of the work of breathing and every breath the patient takes is fully supported. As the patient recovers, they can be transitioned to SIMV, which allows some spontaneous breathing between mandatory breaths. When ready to evaluate for weaning, the patient is placed on CPAP or spontaneous mode to assess their ability to breathe independently.

## Tidal Volume and IBW

The initial tidal volume is 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram of ideal body weight. Ideal body weight is used — not actual body weight — because the lungs are sized to the height of the person, not how much they weigh. Using actual body weight in an obese patient can deliver dangerously large tidal volumes that overdistend the alveoli.

The IBW formula for males is 50 plus 2.3 times the height in inches minus 60. For females it is 45.5 plus 2.3 times the height in inches minus 60. Both formulas are valid for patients at least 4 feet tall.

For a male who is 5 feet 10 inches, that is 70 inches. IBW equals 50 plus 2.3 times the quantity 70 minus 60, which is 50 plus 23, equaling 73 kg. The appropriate tidal volume range is 6 times 73 equals 438 mL up to 8 times 73 equals 584 mL — so a clinician would set approximately 450 to 550 mL.

## ARDS Lung Protective Strategy

In ARDS, the alveoli are fragile and prone to injury from overdistension. When the plateau pressure climbs above 30 cmH₂O, you implement lung-protective ventilation. This means reducing tidal volume to 4 to 8 mL per kg IBW, targeting a plateau pressure less than 28 cmH₂O, and maintaining a driving pressure — which is plateau pressure minus PEEP — less than 15 cmH₂O.

This approach may allow PaCO₂ to rise above 60 mmHg, which is called permissive hypercapnia, as long as the pH stays above 7.15. Similarly, permissive hypoxemia allows PaO₂ to remain around 55 mmHg rather than chasing a normal value at the cost of lung injury.

## Dead Space

Three types of dead space reduce the alveolar tidal volume — the volume actually available for gas exchange.

Mechanical dead space is volume lost in the equipment before reaching the patient's airways. This includes the ventilator circuit, the artificial airway, and any adaptors such as an HME, in-line suction catheter, ETCO₂ monitor, or aerosol delivery device. An important component of mechanical dead space is compressible volume: the circuit expands when pressurized and contracts when pressure is released. An adult circuit loses 3 mL per cmH₂O of pressure. So a patient with a peak pressure of 33 cmH₂O loses 99 mL just to circuit expansion. Most modern ventilators compensate for this automatically.

Anatomic dead space is the volume that reaches the upper airways — trachea and bronchioles — but does not reach the alveolar-capillary level where gas exchange occurs. It is approximately 1 mL per pound of IBW, or about 30 percent of the inhaled tidal volume.

Alveolar dead space is volume that reaches the alveoli but does not participate in gas exchange because those alveoli are not perfused. Causes include pulmonary embolism, emphysema, shock, pulmonary hypertension, and obstruction of pulmonary vessels.

Physiologic dead space is the sum of anatomic and alveolar dead space. It is quantified by the VD/Vt ratio, calculated using the Bohr equation: VD/Vt equals PaCO₂ minus PECO₂ divided by PaCO₂. Normal VD/Vt is 0.30 or 30 percent. During exercise it decreases to 0.20. In significant lung disease it exceeds 0.60 and predicts increased mortality.

A worked example: a 150-pound patient on a Vt of 500 mL with a peak pressure of 33 cmH₂O and 50 mL of alveolar dead space. Mechanical dead space is 99 mL. Anatomic dead space is 150 mL. Alveolar dead space is 50 mL. Total dead space is 299 mL. Alveolar tidal volume is 500 minus 299 equals 201 mL. Only 201 of the 500 mL delivered actually participated in gas exchange.

## Respiratory Rate

The initial respiratory rate is 10 to 20 breaths per minute. Rate is the primary control for PaCO₂. Increasing rate increases minute volume and decreases PaCO₂. Decreasing rate decreases minute volume and increases PaCO₂. Cycle time equals 60 divided by the respiratory rate — at a rate of 10, one mandatory breath is delivered every 6 seconds.

## Peak Inspiratory Flow Rate

PIFR is the speed of gas delivery, measured in L per minute. It is a set parameter in volume control and a variable result in pressure control. The typical setting is 60 L per minute, with a range of 40 to 80 L per minute. PIFR multiplied by I-time equals tidal volume.

Increasing PIFR delivers the set volume faster, shortening I-time. This increases E-time but also increases PIP and worsens gas distribution because gas does not distribute evenly at high flow. Decreasing PIFR lengthens I-time, lowers PIP, and improves gas distribution — but if I-time gets too long, air trapping and auto-PEEP can develop.

## Inspiratory Time and I:E Ratio

Normal I-time for adults is 0.75 to 1.5 seconds. I-time can be calculated from cycle time and the I:E ratio: I-time equals cycle time divided by the sum of the I:E ratio numbers. At a rate of 10 with an I:E of 1:2, cycle time is 6 seconds, and I-time equals 6 divided by 3, which is 2 seconds.

The normal I:E ratio for adults is 1:2 to 1:3. Obstructive disease patients with COPD or asthma need a prolonged expiratory time — ratios like 1:4 to 1:6 give more time for air to exhale, reducing air trapping. ARDS patients may use an inversed ratio of 2:1, where I-time is longer than E-time, to improve alveolar recruitment and oxygenation.

## PEEP

PEEP maintains positive airway pressure at end-exhalation to prevent alveolar collapse. It is indicated for refractory hypoxemia from atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, or pulmonary edema when PaO₂ is less than 50 to 60 mmHg despite FiO₂ over 50 percent. The main purpose is to allow FiO₂ to be reduced to non-toxic levels below 60 percent.

PEEP is contraindicated in untreated tension pneumothorax, head trauma with elevated ICP, hemodynamic instability, and bronchopleural fistula. Its hazards include alveolar overdistension and barotrauma, decreased cardiac output, decreased renal function, and increased ICP.

Optimal PEEP is the lowest level that achieves adequate oxygenation at a non-toxic FiO₂. A lung recruitment maneuver applies sustained high airway pressure — 30 cmH₂O for 30 seconds or 40 cmH₂O for 40 seconds — to open collapsed alveoli, followed by titrating PEEP to keep them open.

## FiO₂ and PEEP Algorithm

When correcting hypoxemia, increase FiO₂ first up to 60 percent. Once FiO₂ is at 60 percent, begin adding or increasing PEEP. Continue increasing PEEP until adverse cardiac effects appear — decreased cardiac output or hypotension. At that point, return to increasing FiO₂ from 60 percent toward 100 percent.

When weaning, reverse the order: reduce FiO₂ to 60 percent first, then decrease PEEP in increments of 2 to 5 cmH₂O.

## Pressure Support

Pressure support is patient-triggered, pressure-targeted, and flow-cycled. The breath ends when inspiratory flow decelerates to 25 percent of peak flow. A low level of 5 to 10 cmH₂O overcomes the resistance of the endotracheal tube and circuit. A high level above 10 cmH₂O supports fatigued muscles during weaning. Automatic Tube Compensation calculates the minimum pressure needed based on ETT size.

## Alarms

The high pressure alarm is set 10 to 15 cmH₂O above the measured PIP. When it is breached, inspiration ends and the remaining volume is not delivered — this is a safety feature to protect the lungs. Causes include secretions, bronchospasm, patient-ventilator asynchrony, and decreasing compliance. The low pressure alarm is set 10 to 15 cmH₂O below PIP and indicates a leak or disconnection.

Apnea backup ventilation activates in spontaneous modes or on low SIMV rates when the patient has been apneic for 10 to 15 seconds. It delivers a backup rate and tidal volume to keep the patient alive while the clinical team responds.

## ABG-Guided Adjustments

After obtaining an ABG, first determine whether the problem is oxygenation or ventilation. For ventilation issues, confirm that the tidal volume is correct for the patient's IBW. If Vt is correct and PaCO₂ is wrong, adjust the rate. If Vt is not correct, fix the Vt first.

The PaCO₂ correction formula is: new rate equals current rate times current PaCO₂ divided by desired PaCO₂. Similarly, new Vt equals current Vt times current PaCO₂ divided by desired PaCO₂. The key phrase to remember is "what you have over what you want."

When you need to correct an inappropriate Vt but the PaCO₂ is already normal, calculate the current minute volume first, then divide by the new target Vt to find the new rate that maintains the same minute volume.
