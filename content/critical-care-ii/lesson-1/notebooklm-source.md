This lesson covers when, why, and how we decide to place a patient on mechanical ventilation — the indications, contraindications, goals, and hazards. This is the foundation of everything in Critical Care II.

## Why Do We Ventilate?

Mechanical ventilation is initiated when a patient can no longer maintain adequate ventilation or oxygenation on their own. The general categories are Type I respiratory failure, Type II respiratory failure, impending respiratory failure, and patients with abnormal bedside parameters or physiologic measurements.

## Type I Respiratory Failure — Hypoxemic

Type I respiratory failure is hypoxemic respiratory failure. The defining feature is a PaO₂ less than 60 mmHg with a PaCO₂ that is normal or decreased — meaning the lungs are actually blowing off CO₂ faster than normal, but they still cannot get oxygen in. The P(A-a)O₂ gradient is increased, which tells us the problem is at the alveolar-capillary membrane level rather than just hypoventilation.

The causes of Type I failure revolve around V/Q mismatch and shunt. The normal V/Q ratio is 0.8, calculated as alveolar minute ventilation of 4 liters per minute divided by cardiac output of 5 liters per minute. When V/Q is greater than 0.8, there is more ventilation than perfusion — like the lung apices normally have. When V/Q is less than 0.8, there is less ventilation than perfusion — like the lung bases normally have.

V/Q dead space occurs when the V/Q ratio approaches infinity: there is ventilation but no perfusion. Think of pulmonary embolism, where blood flow to part of the lung is cut off. The alveoli are ventilated but no gas exchange happens because there is no blood passing by. Other causes of dead space include shock, hypovolemia, and excessive PEEP.

Shunt is the opposite. When V/Q equals zero, there is perfusion but no ventilation. Blood flows through the lung but the alveoli are collapsed or filled with fluid, so no gas exchange occurs. This is called refractory hypoxemia because giving more oxygen does not fix it — the blood never contacts any gas. Normal anatomic shunt is 2 to 3 percent from the bronchial and thebesian veins. Pathologic shunt includes intracardiac causes like ASD and VSD, and intrapulmonary causes like atelectasis, pulmonary edema, pneumonia, and ARDS.

Other causes of Type I failure include diffusion impairment from interstitial lung disease or emphysema, decreased inspired oxygen from high altitude (above 10,000 feet), and venous admixture where non-oxygenated shunted blood mixes with re-oxygenated blood in CHF with low cardiac output.

To differentiate the cause of hypoxemia, the P(A-a)O₂ gradient is very useful. An increased gradient points to V/Q mismatch or shunt as the cause. A normal gradient suggests the problem is simply hypoventilation or decreased PiO₂ from altitude.

## Type II Respiratory Failure — Hypercapnic

Type II respiratory failure is hypercapnic: PaCO₂ greater than 50 mmHg. The body is failing to remove CO₂. Hypoxemia is usually also present, but the key distinction is that it responds to supplemental oxygen — you can correct the PaO₂ by giving more oxygen, unlike in a true shunt. The underlying mechanism is decreased alveolar minute volume.

Causes of Type II failure include decreased respiratory drive from drug overdoses, brainstem lesions, CNS diseases like multiple sclerosis or Parkinson's, elevated intracranial pressure, hypothyroidism, and morbid obesity. Carotid endarterectomy can incidentally remove the carotid bodies, which are peripheral chemoreceptors that normally stimulate breathing in response to low oxygen.

Increased CO₂ production can also cause or worsen Type II failure. Fever, agitation, shivering, and hypermetabolism all generate more CO₂ than the lungs can handle. Importantly, metabolism of carbohydrates produces the most CO₂ for the amount of oxygen used — excess caloric intake, especially carbohydrates, can significantly worsen hypercapnia in borderline patients.

Impaired ability to exhale CO₂ from muscle weakness diseases is another mechanism. Guillain-Barré syndrome, Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, and botulism all weaken the respiratory muscles so severely that patients cannot generate the flow needed to exhale CO₂ effectively.

CO₂ exposure itself can cause Type II failure — defective CO₂ scrubbers in anesthesia machines or PFT equipment, caves where CO₂ accumulates, and dry ice exposure.

## Impending Respiratory Failure

Sometimes a patient has not yet crossed the objective thresholds for Type I or Type II failure but is visibly heading there. This is impending respiratory failure, and it is based on physician judgment. Key considerations include patient appearance, degree of dyspnea, the underlying disease process and its typical trajectory, and trends in oxygen saturation and respiratory rate.

## Bedside Ventilatory Parameters

Bedside spirometry and blood gas values provide objective evidence of respiratory failure. For inadequate alveolar ventilation, the thresholds are PaCO₂ greater than 55 mmHg and pH less than 7.20. For inadequate lung expansion assessed with bedside spirometry, look for a spontaneous tidal volume less than 5 mL per kg, vital capacity less than 10 mL per kg, and respiratory rate greater than 35 breaths per minute. For inadequate muscle strength measured with a negative inspiratory force gauge, the threshold is MIP greater than negative 20 cmH₂O — remember that less negative means weaker, so negative 18 is worse than negative 21.

## Physiologic Measurements

Physiologic measurements look at oxygenation and work of breathing. For hypoxemia, the P(A-a)O₂ gradient greater than 300 mmHg and a PaO₂ to FiO₂ ratio less than 200 are significant thresholds. The alveolar oxygen tension formula is PAO₂ equals the quantity of atmospheric pressure minus water vapor pressure times FiO₂, minus PaCO₂ divided by the respiratory quotient. For increased work of breathing, a minute volume greater than 10 liters per minute and a VD/Vt ratio greater than 0.6 indicate the patient is working excessively hard just to maintain borderline gas exchange.

## Contraindications

Mechanical ventilation should not be initiated if there is no indication, if the patient meets criteria for non-invasive ventilation and could be managed that way, if it is contrary to the patient's expressed wishes such as a valid DNR or DNI order, or if it represents medically futile therapy.

## Goals of Mechanical Ventilation

The goals are straightforward and clinically important. We are trying to maintain adequate alveolar ventilation, maintain adequate oxygen delivery, restore acid-base balance, reduce the work of breathing, reduce myocardial workload that was increased by hypoxemia and excessive WOB, permit sedation and neuromuscular blockade when needed, reduce intracranial pressure in appropriate patients, and stabilize the chest wall in trauma patients.

## Hazards of Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is non-physiological. Normally, the respiratory muscles generate negative pressure to draw air in. On a ventilator, positive pressure blows air in against the airways. This reversal creates predictable hazards. Ventilator-induced lung injury comes from over-distension and cyclic opening and closing of alveoli. Barotrauma from excessive pressure can cause pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum. Nosocomial pneumonia, specifically ventilator-associated pneumonia, develops because the endotracheal tube bypasses the upper airway defenses and allows colonized secretions to leak around the cuff into the lungs. Atelectasis, oxygen toxicity from prolonged high FiO₂, and auto-PEEP from air trapping are additional concerns. The positive intrathoracic pressure of mechanical ventilation impedes venous return, which decreases cardiac output. Reduced cardiac output then decreases renal perfusion and urinary output. Finally, patients on mechanical ventilation often receive inadequate nutrition, which impairs their ability to heal and wean.
