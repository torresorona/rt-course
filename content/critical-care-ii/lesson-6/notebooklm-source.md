# NotebookLM Source: Weaning, Discontinuation & Module Review

This lesson covers the final phase of caring for a mechanically ventilated patient — how to prevent complications during their stay on the vent, how to safely get them off the ventilator, and what to do when weaning is impossible. It also serves as a comprehensive review of all Critical Care II content.

## Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia

VAP is pneumonia that develops more than 48 to 72 hours after intubation and mechanical ventilation. It is one of the most serious and preventable complications of mechanical ventilation. The endotracheal tube bypasses the upper airway's natural defenses, allowing colonized secretions to pool above the cuff and leak into the lungs.

VAP prevention is a bundle of simultaneous practices. Hand hygiene is foundational. The head of the bed should be maintained at 30 to 45 degrees at all times to reduce aspiration. Ventilator circuits should not be changed on a routine schedule — AARC evidence shows no benefit to scheduled changes and considerable cost savings with extended use. Change the circuit only when visibly soiled or mechanically malfunctioning. Condensate in the circuit must be drained away from the patient — never toward the patient's airway. HMEs can be used safely for at least 48 hours, and some devices up to one week. Closed suction catheters do not need daily replacement. MDIs are preferred over handheld nebulizers when possible because nebulizers are a recognized VAP risk factor along with manual ventilation and patient transport. Sedation holidays and spontaneous breathing trials should occur daily. When possible, avoid intubation entirely — NIV is always safer. Oral hygiene every 4 hours significantly reduces oropharyngeal bacterial burden and VAP incidence.

When a circuit change is necessary, the procedure includes setting up a BVM first, bypassing the ventilator circuit from the humidifier, replacing the humidifier and filling it with sterile distilled water, silencing alarms, swapping the circuit during the patient's exhalation phase to minimize exposure, checking for leaks, confirming all parameters, assessing the patient, resetting alarms, reassuring the patient, and documenting the change.

## Weaning vs. Liberation

Weaning is the gradual process of decreasing ventilatory support — reducing oxygen, PEEP, and mandatory rate — while allowing the patient to assume more of their own work of breathing. Ventilator discontinuation or liberation is the act of removing the patient from the ventilator entirely. These are conceptually different, and it is sometimes important to separate them: a patient may be liberated from the ventilator but still need a protected artificial airway for secretion management or upper airway edema.

## Weaning Timelines

Most patients — those who are post-operative or had a drug overdose — wean within 72 hours. About 20 to 25 percent require a systematic weaning approach over days. About 5 percent require weeks to wean, particularly COPD and ARDS patients. And 1 percent are ventilator-dependent and unweanable, such as those with cervical spinal cord injuries or advanced neuromuscular disease.

## Weaning Factors

Success depends on four factors. First, ventilatory workload versus capacity — has the underlying disease resolved enough that the patient's respiratory muscles can maintain adequate ventilation? Second, oxygenation status — can the patient maintain adequate PaO₂ on reduced FiO₂ and PEEP? Third, cardiovascular function — is the patient hemodynamically stable? Weaning shifts work back to the patient and increases myocardial demand. Fourth, psychological factors — fear, anxiety, depression, sleep deprivation, and ICU psychosis all impair weaning. ICU psychosis is an acute brain failure from dehydration, hypoxia, infection, and drugs that causes confusion, paranoia, and agitation. Addressing the patient's psychological state — including providing windows, communication tools, and lifting sedation when appropriate — is part of preparing for weaning.

## Weaning Parameters

Before attempting weaning, objective parameters are evaluated. For oxygenation, the target thresholds are FiO₂ less than 40 to 50 percent, PEEP less than 5 to 8 cmH₂O, PaO₂ greater than 60 mmHg, SaO₂ greater than 90 percent, P/F ratio greater than 150 to 200, and P(A-a)O₂ less than 350 mmHg. For ventilation, PaCO₂ less than 50 and pH greater than 7.35. For mechanics, respiratory rate 12 to 30, tidal volume greater than 5 mL/kg, vital capacity greater than 15 mL/kg, static compliance greater than 25 mL/cmH₂O, and RSBI less than 105.

For muscle strength, the MIP must be less than negative 20 cmH₂O — meaning the patient can generate a pressure more negative than negative 20. Remember the sign convention: negative 19 is weaker than negative 21.

P0.1 is the inspiratory pressure measured 100 milliseconds after airway occlusion. It is effort-independent — it does not depend on the patient's cooperation or effort level. A P0.1 greater than 6 cmH₂O indicates high ventilatory drive and predicts difficulty weaning.

It is important not to over-rely on weaning parameters. Patients who meet all parameters are usually ready to be liberated. But waiting for all parameters to be simultaneously perfect can unnecessarily prolong ventilation.

## RSBI — The Rapid Shallow Breathing Index

The RSBI, also called Tobin's Index, is calculated as respiratory rate divided by tidal volume in liters. It captures the pattern of rapid shallow breathing that typically precedes respiratory failure in a patient who cannot sustain spontaneous ventilation.

To measure it, place the patient on CPAP mode with PEEP set to zero and PS set to zero. After one minute, record the spontaneous RR and Vt. Divide RR by Vt in liters. An RSBI less than 105 is a good predictor of weaning success.

Three examples all with a minute volume of 6 L/min: a patient breathing 12 times per minute with 500 mL tidal volume has an RSBI of 24 — strongly predictive of success. A patient breathing 24 times per minute with 250 mL tidal volumes has an RSBI of 96 — borderline but still under 105. A patient breathing 30 times per minute with 200 mL tidal volumes has an RSBI of 150 — predictive of failure, even though the minute volume is normal. The RSBI catches the pattern that minute volume alone misses.

## Weaning Methods

SIMV weaning involves gradually reducing the mandatory rate by 1 to 2 breaths at a time, assessing tolerance with ABG results, vital signs, and patient appearance after each reduction. When the rate reaches 4 to 6 mandatory breaths per minute and the patient is tolerating it, an SBT is performed and extubation is considered. SIMV is the slowest weaning method.

PSV weaning starts with high pressure support set to deliver 6 to 10 mL/kg IBW. Support is gradually reduced until the patient is receiving only 5 to 8 cmH₂O — just enough to overcome the resistance of the ETT and circuit. This method preserves alarms, backup ventilation, monitored data, and the intact circuit throughout.

The SBT assesses the patient's ability to breathe with minimal or no support. It is done multiple times per day, interspersed with full support to rest the muscles. SBTs are extended gradually based on patient response. A successful SBT should prompt consideration for extubation.

## T-Piece vs. CPAP SBT

The T-piece trial removes the patient from the ventilator and places them on humidified oxygen through a T-piece adaptor. It is the oldest method and has the disadvantages of requiring extra equipment, breaking the circuit, and providing no apnea backup or alarms.

The CPAP SBT leaves the patient on the ventilator in CPAP mode with CPAP set to zero and PS set to zero. This method is preferred: no extra equipment is needed, the circuit stays intact, full alarms remain active, and apnea backup is available.

## SBT Failure Criteria

Stop the SBT and return the patient to full support if any of the following occur: sustained respiratory rate greater than 35, SpO₂ less than 90 percent, tachycardia greater than 140 or a 20 percent increase from baseline, bradycardia 20 percent below baseline, systolic hypertension above 180, hypotension below 90, agitation or anxiety, diaphoresis, or changes in mental status. Causes of SBT failure include poor respiratory mechanics, cardiac disease, electrolyte imbalances, anxiety, secretions, aspiration, alkalosis, neuromuscular weakness, sepsis, excessive sedation, inadequate nutrition, opiates, and obesity.

After a failed SBT, reestablish ventilatory support immediately, identify and correct the cause, and allow the patient to rest on full support for several hours before the next attempt. RT-driven weaning protocols improve outcomes when systematically applied.

## Terminal Weaning

When a patient has a catastrophic and irreversible illness, the family in consultation with the physician may decide to discontinue mechanical ventilation. This is called terminal weaning. The focus shifts to patient comfort. Morphine or fentanyl is given for pain and air hunger. Midazolam or lorazepam is given for anxiety. Terminal weaning must be conducted in a humane and compassionate manner, with the patient's dignity and comfort as the priority.

## Brain Death

Brain death occurs from prolonged cardiac arrest, massive stroke, or severe brain infection or tumor. Clinical evaluation by a neurologist or critical care physician assesses for the complete absence of brainstem reflexes: pupillary reflex to light, corneal reflex, gag reflex, and oculovestibular reflex tested with iced water in the ear canal. An EEG may confirm the absence of electrical brain activity.

The apnea test is the definitive test. Before beginning, a compensated ABG must show PaCO₂ between 35 and 40 mmHg and PaO₂ of 200 mmHg. The patient is then removed from the ventilator and 100 percent oxygen is delivered at 4 to 6 L per minute through a catheter placed at the carina. The team watches for any spontaneous chest movement for 8 to 10 minutes. At the end, an ABG is obtained. A positive test — meaning brain death is confirmed — occurs when the PaCO₂ rises to 60 mmHg or 20 mmHg above baseline, and the patient remains apneic throughout. The patient is then returned to full ventilation and the results are documented.
