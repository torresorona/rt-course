# NotebookLM Source: Monitoring, Ventilator Graphics & Patient Transport

This lesson covers three closely related skills: monitoring the patient on the ventilator, reading and interpreting ventilator graphics, and safely transporting a mechanically ventilated patient. These skills are used continuously at the bedside and are especially important in military settings where CCATT missions may require transport in challenging environments.

## Monitored Values

The ventilator displays both settings (what you put in) and measured values (what actually happened). The measured values tell you how the patient is responding.

For pressures, the key values are peak inspiratory pressure, mean airway pressure, plateau pressure, driving pressure, baseline pressure (PEEP), and auto-PEEP. PIP is the highest pressure recorded during inspiration and is affected by PIFR, PEEP, tidal volume, airway resistance, and compliance. Mean airway pressure represents the average pressure over the entire ventilatory cycle — normal is 5 to 10 cmH₂O. Higher MAP indicates poorer oxygenation status and greater risk of adverse cardiac effects from positive pressure. MAP is increased by higher PIP, higher PEEP, longer I-time, higher respiratory rate, decreased compliance, and increased resistance.

Plateau pressure is measured by programming an inspiratory hold. During the hold, gas flow stops, and pressure equilibrates throughout the lung. Because there is no flow, there is no airway resistance contribution — plateau pressure represents only the pressure applied to the small airways and alveoli. It is always lower than PIP and should be kept below 30 cmH₂O — below 28 in ARDS.

Driving pressure equals Pplat minus PEEP. It represents the pressure required to deliver a tidal breath from the PEEP baseline. Current evidence supports maintaining driving pressure below 15 cmH₂O.

Auto-PEEP, also called intrinsic PEEP or occult PEEP, is trapped gas at end-exhalation that creates a hidden positive pressure in the alveoli. It develops when expiratory time is inadequate — from high respiratory rates, low PIFR, or obstructive disease that slows exhalation. Auto-PEEP is detected by an expiratory hold maneuver, or graphically using the flow-time scalar when expiratory flow does not return to zero before the next breath begins. It is treated by addressing the underlying cause — bronchodilators, suctioning, increasing airway size, decreasing respiratory rate, increasing PIFR to shorten I-time, or allowing spontaneous breathing on CPAP.

## Pulmonary Mechanics

Dynamic compliance is tidal volume divided by PIP minus PEEP. Because PIP includes the airway resistance component, dynamic compliance reflects both compliance and resistance. Static compliance is tidal volume divided by Pplat minus PEEP. Because Pplat is measured under no-flow conditions, static compliance reflects only lung and chest wall distensibility. Normal static compliance is 60 to 100 mL per cmH₂O. Low values indicate ARDS, atelectasis, pneumothorax, pulmonary fibrosis, or chest wall stiffness.

To distinguish increased airway resistance from decreased compliance, compare the PIP-Pplat delta. If PIP rises but Pplat stays the same, the delta increases — this is a RAW problem, typically from secretions, bronchospasm, or circuit obstruction. If both PIP and Pplat rise proportionally and the delta stays the same, this is a compliance problem — think atelectasis, ARDS, pneumonia, or pneumothorax.

## Ventilator System Check

A ventilator check is a documented evaluation of the mechanical ventilator and the patient's response to ventilatory support. It must be performed on a scheduled basis — generally every two hours — and additionally before obtaining an ABG, before hemodynamic tests, after any settings change, and after any acute deterioration.

During the check, the clinician verifies airway size and position, checks for and empties circuit condensate, checks the date of the last circuit change, assesses vital signs and breath sounds, documents all settings and measured values, verifies all alarm settings, and assesses patient-ventilator synchrony using graphics.

## Ventilator Graphics

Ventilator graphics display the relationship between pressure, flow, volume, and time as waveforms. There are two types: scalars (timed waveforms) and loops.

The pressure-time scalar is the most commonly used waveform, especially during volume control. It shows PIP, PEEP, and Pplat during an inspiratory hold. The flow-time scalar plots flow on the Y-axis with inspiration above baseline and expiration below. It is excellent for detecting auto-PEEP when expiratory flow does not return to zero, and for assessing bronchodilator response. The volume-time scalar is used to detect leaks — if the tracing does not return to baseline at end-exhalation, volume is escaping the system.

Loops display two variables against each other. The pressure-volume loop plots volume on the Y-axis and pressure on the X-axis. Controlled breaths trace counterclockwise. The shape of the curve reflects compliance — a wider flatter curve means lower compliance. The opening pressure on the P/V curve, typically around 15 cmH₂O, represents the point at which collapsed alveoli begin to open and can be used to guide PEEP settings. The flow-volume loop is used to detect auto-PEEP and small airway obstruction, and it resembles a forced vital capacity loop but is passive and reversed.

Flow starvation occurs when the PIFR is set too low for the patient's inspiratory demand. On the pressure-time scalar it appears as a distinct downward dip during the inspiratory phase — the patient is pulling harder than the ventilator is delivering. The fix is to increase PIFR.

## Patient Transport

Mechanically ventilated patients are transported for intra-hospital diagnostic procedures like CT and MRI, and for inter-hospital transport over varying distances. Ground ambulance is used for trips up to 80 miles. Helicopter is used for 80 to 150 miles. Fixed-wing aircraft is used for distances greater than 150 miles.

Transport should only occur after careful risk-benefit analysis, because nearly two-thirds of transports for diagnostic studies fail to yield results that change patient care. Contraindications include inability to adequately ventilate and oxygenate on a transport ventilator, inability to maintain hemodynamics, inability to monitor appropriately, inability to maintain airway control, and incomplete transport team assembly.

Hazards include hyperventilation during manual ventilation causing respiratory alkalosis, disconnection, accidental extubation, loss of oxygen supply, loss of PEEP, position-related hypotension and hypercarbia, equipment failure, and IV disconnection.

The transport ventilator must have battery backup, be capable of A/C or SIMV, have airway pressure monitoring and audible alarms, include a disconnect and high-pressure release alarm, be capable of delivering PEEP and up to 100 percent FiO₂, and have an anti-asphyxia valve in case of gas supply failure. Tank duration is calculated as tank PSI times tank factor divided by minute volume in L per minute.

The goal of monitoring during transport is to provide the same level of care the patient received in the ICU. Continuous monitoring includes ECG, pulse oximetry, and blood pressure — ideally via arterial line. Periodic monitoring includes non-invasive blood pressure, respiratory rate, and tidal volume if a transport ventilator is used.

The transport team includes an RN, RT, and physician for unstable patients. At least one member must be proficient in airway management and at least one must be an ACLS provider.

## CCATT

The Critical Care Air Transport Team is a military specialized transport team trained to maintain ICU-level care during flight. The team consists of a critical care physician, critical care nurse, and respiratory therapist. Training occurs at Wright-Patterson AFB in Cincinnati, Ohio. The CCATT carries three transport ventilators, infusion pumps, portable suction, a transport monitor and defibrillator, an airway kit, the SMEED equipment mounting platform, and an iStat for bedside blood gas analysis. Aircraft used include the KC-135 and C-130.

Aircraft-specific considerations include incompatible electrical power — not standard 110 V — extreme temperatures, vibration from turboprop engines, G-forces during takeoff and landing, and significant noise that makes communication difficult and may prevent alarm sounds from being heard.

At altitude, atmospheric pressure decreases and gas volume expands per Boyle's Law. This means tidal volumes must be verified with a Wright's spirometer in flight. ETT cuffs expand at altitude, risking tracheal injury, and deflate during descent, risking aspiration and ventilation compromise. Cuff pressure must be checked and adjusted before and after takeoff and landing.
