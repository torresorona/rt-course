# NotebookLM Source: Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV)

This lesson covers non-invasive ventilation — what it is, when to use it, what devices and interfaces are available, how to set it up, and how to troubleshoot problems. NIV is one of the most important tools for preventing intubation in patients with acute respiratory failure.

## What Is NIV?

Non-invasive ventilation provides ventilatory support without a controlled airway — the patient is not intubated. It delivers positive pressure through a mask or similar interface. The umbrella term NIV includes non-invasive positive pressure ventilation, CPAP, and high-flow nasal cannula, though in the clinical context of this course we focus on NPPV and CPAP.

The core advantage of NIV is that it can improve gas exchange and reduce the work of breathing without the risks of intubation — specifically ventilator-associated pneumonia, airway injury, and the complications of sedation and neuromuscular blockade.

## Indications

NIV is indicated for a wide range of acute conditions: hypoxemic respiratory failure, hypercapnic respiratory failure, asthma exacerbation, facilitating weaning in COPD patients, acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, pneumonia, ARDS and ALI, acute respiratory failure in immunocompromised patients who should not be intubated if possible, palliative care, post-operative respiratory failure, prevention of reintubation in high-risk patients, and post-extubation respiratory failure. In the military context, NIV is particularly valuable as a bridge intervention in austere environments before invasive airway management becomes necessary.

## Goals

The goals of NIV in acute care are to improve gas exchange, avoid intubation, and decrease mortality, time on invasive ventilation, length of hospitalization, and ventilator-associated pneumonia.

## Contraindications

NIV is contraindicated when the patient is apneic — they cannot maintain any breathing between support cycles. It is also contraindicated when the patient cannot protect their airway or has a high aspiration risk, when there is hemodynamic or cardiac instability, when the patient will not cooperate, when there are excessive secretions that cannot be cleared, and when the patient cannot use an appropriate interface due to facial burns, trauma, or abnormal anatomy.

## Side Effects

NIV commonly causes nasal irritation and congestion, dry mouth and throat, eye and skin irritation from mask leaks, pressure or pain in the ears, gastric bloating from swallowed air, claustrophobia especially with full-face masks, pressure sores from straps and mask edges, and excessive air leaks particularly in mouth-breathers.

## Interfaces

The choice of interface significantly affects patient comfort, tolerance, and clinical effectiveness. The nasal mask covers only the nose. It is well-tolerated, causes less claustrophobia, allows the patient to communicate and cough, and has lower dead space than full-face options. The major disadvantage is that the mouth must remain closed or air will leak out. Nasal pillows sit at the entrance to the nostrils and are very comfortable but can cause pressure sores at the nares and provide a less effective seal.

The full face mask covers both the nose and mouth. It provides a better seal and can treat respiratory failure more effectively, but it is claustrophobic, poorly tolerated, and carries aspiration risk because the mask cannot be easily removed if the patient vomits. The total face mask covers the entire face. It requires less patient cooperation, provides improved vision, and causes no pressure sores, but comes in fewer sizes and moisture can fog up the interior.

The helmet is a full head enclosure that avoids pressure sores entirely but requires special circuit connections and is less common.

## NIV Device vs. ICU Ventilator

There are key design differences between non-invasive ventilators and ICU invasive ventilators. NIV devices are electrically powered using a blower motor and are microprocessor controlled. They use a single-limb circuit. Most importantly, they have no exhalation valve — they tolerate and rely on a continuous air leak through the mask or a dedicated port for CO₂ to escape. This is the fundamental design feature that makes them suitable for mask ventilation. An ICU ventilator, by contrast, uses a dual-limb circuit, has an active exhalation valve, and does not tolerate leaks — leaks in an ICU ventilator circuit trigger alarms and reduce delivered volume.

## CPAP Mode

CPAP maintains spontaneous breathing at a continuous elevated pressure above atmospheric. It does not add inspiratory support — the patient breathes on their own against the elevated baseline. CPAP recruits collapsed alveoli, reduces the work of breathing, and improves oxygenation similar to how PEEP works in invasive ventilation.

## BiPAP Settings

BiPAP delivers two levels of pressure: IPAP during inspiration and EPAP during expiration. IPAP is similar to pressure support in invasive ventilation — increasing IPAP augments tidal volume and ventilation, which reduces PaCO₂. EPAP is similar to PEEP — increasing EPAP directly improves oxygenation by recruiting alveoli and preventing collapse.

A useful memory aid: IPAP affects ventilation and CO₂; EPAP affects oxygenation and PaO₂.

In spontaneous/timed or S/T mode, a backup respiratory rate is added. If the patient fails to initiate a breath within the set interval, the ventilator delivers a mandatory breath. Settings in S/T mode include IPAP, EPAP, respiratory rate, I-time, and rise. Rise controls how quickly pressure increases from EPAP to IPAP — a higher rise setting delivers the pressure more slowly and may be more comfortable for patients who are very short of breath.

AVAPS, Average Volume-Assured Pressure Support, targets a specific tidal volume. The ventilator automatically adjusts IPAP within a range set by the clinician — a minimum pressure and a maximum pressure — to achieve the target volume. If the patient needs more pressure to reach the target volume, IPAP increases. If less is needed, it decreases.

## Initial Application

When applying NIV, position the patient with the head of the bed elevated greater than 30 degrees. Select the appropriate ventilator and interface. Set the initial settings low or even at zero so the patient is not immediately hit with high pressure when the mask is applied. Hold the mask to the patient's face and allow them to acclimate before securing the straps. Adjust FiO₂ to achieve target oxygenation. Check carefully for air leak, especially around the eyes — this indicates a poor mask seal and will reduce effectiveness. Reassess the patient frequently, especially every 30 minutes during the first couple of hours.

## Troubleshooting

For nasal irritation and dryness, add a humidifier and use nasal moistening sprays. For dry mouth and throat, use a heated humidifier and offer sips of water. For skin irritation, practice strict hygiene — wipe the face and device with a baby wipe before and after use, and wash the face to remove oils. For ear pain, reduce ventilation pressure if clinically possible. For gastric bloating, consider simethicone. For claustrophobia, consult the physician about alternative therapies or anxiolytics. For pressure sores, maintain hygiene and avoid over-tightening straps. For excessive air leaks in mouth-breathers, use a chin strap or switch to a full face mask. For patients who feel the pressure is too high, use the ramp mode, which gradually increases pressure over 5 to 45 minutes to allow the patient to acclimate.
