# NotebookLM Source: Manual Breathing Devices & Pharyngeal Airways

This lesson covers manual breathing devices and pharyngeal airways. A manual breathing device is also called a bag-valve-mask, BVM, artificial manual breathing unit, or AMBU. It is a hand-held device used to provide positive pressure ventilation to patients who are not breathing adequately. Supplemental oxygen should be attached as soon as appropriate and when enough resources are available. The source also refers to this as "bagging" a patient.

Manual ventilation is indicated when spontaneous breathing is absent or is insufficient to support adequate ventilation and gas exchange. It is also used during CPR, as temporary ventilation for mechanically ventilated patients, during transport of patients on a mechanical ventilator, and for manual hyperinflation before or after suctioning.

Manual ventilation has hazards. Positive pressure ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure, which decreases venous return and can decrease cardiac output. Too much pressure can cause barotrauma and damage the airways, lungs, or other organs. Increased airway pressure can cause gastric insufflation, leading to gastric distension and an increased risk of regurgitation and aspiration. Excessive volume can cause volutrauma and may contribute to tension pneumothorax, especially in patients with lung disease.

A self-inflating manual resuscitation bag includes a cushioned mask that fits over the mouth and nose and connects through a one-way valve to a self-inflating compressible bag. It does not need a gas source to operate. With oxygen at 15 L/min, it can deliver 95% to 100% oxygen. Its disadvantage is that it cannot deliver blow-by oxygen. A PEEP valve may be added, especially when a patient has a high ventilator PEEP setting and particularly when PEEP is over 5 cm H2O.

A flow-inflating resuscitation bag is also called an anesthesia bag. It is mainly used in the operating room by anesthesia and with neonates. Peak inspiratory pressure is controlled by flow to the bag, adjustment of the flow control valve, and how hard the bag is squeezed. The neonate receives the same FiO2 as the gas flowing to the bag. The bag should be kept about half-full between breaths. It may include a pressure manometer and a pressure relief port or pop-off valve. Advantages include always delivering 100% oxygen, allowing the clinician to feel lung compliance, and providing blow-by oxygen. Disadvantages include that it only inflates when the gas source is turned on and the opening is sealed, and it requires a more experienced user.

Before manual ventilation, gather the BVM device, cushioned mask, oxygen source and flowmeter, Yankauer suction catheter with vacuum source, nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal airways, and pulse oximeter.

Patient positioning supports ventilation. The head-tilt/chin-lift technique is performed by pressing down on the forehead with one hand while lifting the bony underside of the chin with two or three fingers of the other hand. The head is tilted past neutral to open the airway. If head, neck, or spinal injury is suspected, use the modified jaw-thrust maneuver. The provider positions above the patient's head, places one hand on each side of the head, slides the fingers under the angles of the jaw without moving the head or neck, and thrusts the jaw upward to open the airway.

An OPA or NPA may help ventilation. The mask is placed first at the bridge of the nose and then lowered over the nose, mouth, and chin. Ventilation rate is 10 to 12 breaths per minute, or approximately one bag squeeze every 5 to 6 seconds. The bag should be depressed for a full 1 to 2 seconds and then released. The clinician should not depress the bag aggressively.

For one-person BVM technique, use the EC technique while lifting the patient's jaw up into the mask. Depress the bag about halfway to deliver 400 to 700 mL. Watch for chest rise. Continue smooth ventilations that last about 1 second and cause the chest to just begin to rise. For two-person technique, Provider 1 is at the head of the bed and uses both hands with the EC technique to maintain the mask seal and open airway. Provider 2 depresses the bag about halfway to deliver 400 to 700 mL and provides smooth ventilations that last about 1 second. Both providers watch for chest rise.

Troubleshooting poor ventilation includes checking equipment, positioning, mask seal, and oxygenation or ventilation barriers. The oxygen source should be on, the bag should function properly, and the correct airway should be in place. The clinician may need to repeat the chin lift or jaw thrust. The mask seal may need a one-person or two-person hold, or a better mask fit. Disease process, vomitus, secretions, or difficult anatomy can also impair ventilation.

The oropharyngeal airway, or OPA, is a hard plastic airway designed to prevent the tongue from obstructing the hypopharynx. It is indicated for unconscious patients, to maintain a patent airway when the tongue would otherwise obstruct the oropharynx, and as a bite block for patients who are intubated. It is contraindicated in conscious or semiconscious patients, trauma to the oral cavity or mandible or maxillary skull areas, and foreign body obstruction in the oral cavity or pharynx.

OPA sizing is done by placing the device on the side of the patient's face with the flange even with the mouth. It should measure from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw while following the natural curve of the airway. One insertion technique uses the cross-finger scissor technique to open the mouth, inserts the OPA upside down with the curvature toward the tongue, advances to the uvula, and rotates 180 degrees. Another technique inserts through the side of the mouth while following the curve of the oral cavity.

The nasopharyngeal airway, or NPA, is a soft plastic or rubber airway inserted through the nostril with the tip resting behind the tongue. It creates a groove that allows airflow. It is indicated to assist with bagging, frequent nasotracheal suctioning, restoring airway patency by separating the tongue from the posterior pharyngeal wall, bronchoscopy, and when a conscious patient needs an airway that is better tolerated. Absolute contraindications are trauma to the nasal region and a foreign object blocking the nasal passages. NPAs are generally used in adults.

NPA sizing uses the distance from the patient's earlobe to the tip of the nose. Insertion includes tilting the patient slightly backward, lubricating the airway with a water-soluble agent, positioning it perpendicular to the frontal plane of the face, and advancing it slowly through the inferior meatus of either nostril with the bevel facing the septum. If an obstruction is felt, gentle twisting may help. If resistance continues, a deviated nasal septum is likely; try the other naris or use a smaller-diameter NPA.
