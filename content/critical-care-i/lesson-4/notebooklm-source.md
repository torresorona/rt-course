# NotebookLM Source: Extubation, Airway Emergencies & Patient Positioning

This lesson covers extubation, airway emergencies, and patient positioning. Extubation is removal of an endotracheal tube. The objectives are to identify when and how to extubate or decannulate a patient and to perform extubation.

Extubation is indicated when the conditions that required airway protection with an endotracheal tube or mechanical ventilation are no longer present. The patient must be able to maintain a patent airway and adequate spontaneous ventilation. The patient should have adequate central inspiratory drive, respiratory muscle strength, cough strength to clear secretions, laryngeal function, nutritional status, and clearance of sedative and neuromuscular blocking effects.

Extubation hazards include hypoxemia and hypercapnia, upper airway obstruction from laryngospasm, laryngeal edema, and vocal cord damage. Extubation criteria include arterial blood gases showing adequate ventilation and oxygenation, verification that the underlying disease process has been reversed, alert and oriented status, and appropriate bedside ventilatory parameters.

The source criteria are maximum inspiratory pressure more negative than or equal to -20 cm H2O, tidal volume more than 5 mL/kg ideal body weight, vital capacity more than 15 mL/kg ideal body weight or about 1.5 L, minute ventilation less than 10 L/min, and awake and alert status. Maximum inspiratory pressure evaluates inspiratory muscle strength. Maximum expiratory pressure evaluates expiratory muscle strength. Tidal volume, minute ventilation, and vital capacity can be measured with a Wright spirometer. Pressure measuring devices can obtain MIP and MEP.

For an 80 kg ideal body weight patient, the minimum tidal volume is 400 mL and the minimum vital capacity is 1200 mL. A measured MIP of -25 cm H2O, minute ventilation of 9 L/min, and awake and alert status support readiness. For a 50 kg ideal body weight patient, the source example lists MIP of -50 cm H2O, tidal volume of 350 mL, vital capacity of 850 mL, minute ventilation of 8 L/min, and awake and alert status. The clinician compares the patient's values with normal values for that specific patient.

Extubation procedure begins with assembling equipment, explaining the procedure, and positioning the patient. Equipment includes endotracheal suctioning equipment, Yankauer, 10-mL or 12-mL syringe, oxygen and aerosol therapy equipment, usually a high-flow aerosol device, face tent or mask, manual resuscitator and mask, aerosol nebulizer with racemic epinephrine if there are concerns about post-extubation edema, bronchodilator, intubation equipment, and incentive spirometer. Explain the procedure at a level the patient understands and place the patient in semi-Fowler's or Fowler's position.

The next step is suctioning the ETT and pharynx above the cuff to help prevent aspiration after cuff deflation. Suction above and below the cuff. Suction inline while placing the patient on 100% oxygen and suction orally. Then oxygenate the patient well after suctioning. Extubation is stressful and can cause hypoxemia and unwanted cardiovascular side effects. To help prevent these problems, administer 100% oxygen for 5 minutes.

An optional step, depending on hospital policy, is attaching a 10-mL or 12-mL syringe to the pilot tubing and listening for an audible leak around the tube and for stridor. If no audible leak is present, reinflate the cuff and discuss with the physician. To remove the tube, remove the tape or holder, have the patient take a large deep breath in, deflate the balloon, and remove the tube at maximal inhalation. This prevents vocal cord damage. Have the patient cough after maximal inhalation to remove secretions and suction secretions. Apply cool mist oxygen immediately after extubation using the same FiO2 that was on the ventilator before extubation.

After extubation, assess or reassess the patient. Auscultate over the larynx for stridor. Stridor is a common post-extubation complication and may be caused by laryngospasm or tracheal edema. The source notes aerosolized racemic epinephrine. Auscultate over lung fields. Wheezing indicates bronchospasm, and rhonchi indicates secretions. Monitor vital signs, SpO2, and respiratory rate. Observe for distress and dyspnea. Instruct the patient on incentive spirometry and do not leave until the patient is stable.

Airway emergency troubleshooting includes tube obstruction, cuff leaks, unplanned extubation, and repositioning endotracheal tubes. Tube obstruction causes include the tube being kinked or against the tracheal wall, a herniated cuff, and complete or partial obstruction from mucus or blood. If the tube is kinked or against the tracheal wall, adjust the patient's head, verify previous placement such as 22 at the teeth, and reposition by advancing or withdrawing if needed. If the cuff is herniated, deflate the cuff. For complete or partial obstruction, attempt suctioning and instill normal saline if necessary. Reintubate using an airway exchange catheter if the catheter passes the obstruction. If complete obstruction remains, extubate and reintubate or obtain an emergency surgical airway.

Cuff leak causes include pilot balloon, pilot tube, or cuff problems. Clinical signs include decreased peak pressures, decreased delivered tidal volumes, sounds heard over the trachea, and the patient talking. Troubleshooting includes determining where the leak originates, notifying the physician, and deciding whether to extubate and reintubate or manage the leak until the patient can be extubated. If the patient needs to remain intubated and the leak is significant, reintubate using an exchange catheter.

Unplanned extubation causes include sedation holidays, spontaneous breathing trials, inadequate restraints, transport, bathing, improper use of equipment such as securing devices, circuit placement, or suction catheter placement, and patient factors such as beards, diaphoresis, alcohol use, or drug abuse. Hazards and complications include laryngospasm, laryngeal edema, aspiration pneumonia, bronchospasm, vocal cord damage, respiratory failure, death, and longer ICU stay.

Need for endotracheal tube repositioning can be identified during ventilator checks by watching for equal chest movement, listening for equal breath sounds, checking tube position, and using chest x-ray. When possible, elevate the head of the bed to 45 degrees. Note current placement by markings at the teeth or gums. Hyperoxygenate the patient, suction the trachea and oropharynx, loosen the tape or securing device, deflate the cuff enough to cause a slight leak, advance or retract to the correct depth, reinflate the cuff and check cuff pressure, secure the airway, chart the new placement and depth, and recommend x-ray for definitive confirmation.

Patient positioning techniques are used to improve oxygenation and ventilation. Frequent changes in body position and posture are universal in healthy people even during sleep. Mobilization during illness and after surgery helps prevent bed sores, musculoskeletal wasting, atelectasis, pneumonia, and thromboembolism. The primary purpose is to promote lung expansion, improve oxygenation, and prevent retention of secretions. Positioning may be performed by the patient, caregiver, or special bed.

Relative contraindications to positioning include marked agitation, increased ICP, hypotension, worsening dyspnea, hypoxia, and cardiac arrhythmia. An absolute contraindication is unstable spinal cord injury. Common positions include low Fowler's at 15 to 30 degrees, semi-Fowler's at 30 to 45 degrees, high Fowler's nearly vertical, supine, Trendelenburg, reverse Trendelenburg, lateral decubitus, Sims, and prone. High Fowler's is the most common position for floor patients. Supine places the patient flat on the back and is considered the natural at-rest position; avoid leaving comatose patients in this position too long because of aspiration risk. Trendelenburg is supine with head lowered and feet raised, used for hypotensive shock. Reverse Trendelenburg is supine with head elevated and foot of bed lowered. Lateral decubitus is side-lying with pillows between legs and slight flexion of knees and arms. Sims combines prone and lateral with the leg flexed toward the chest and elbow flexed. Prone is lying on the stomach with head turned to one side and is used to improve oxygenation in ARDS.

Pulmonary disease-specific positions include ARDS, obstructive airway disease, and unilateral lung injury. In ARDS, prone positioning improves oxygenation. The source states it does not seem to improve survival rate. Suspected mechanisms include improved V/Q, recruitment of collapsed alveoli, increased FRC, and increased cardiac output. Disadvantages of proning include hemodynamic instability, decrease in gas exchange, specialized nursing care demands, and special equipment such as beds. In obstructive airway disease, upright Fowler's decreases resistance. Leaning forward in tripod position is used by severe COPD patients trying to relieve dyspnea. In unilateral lung injury, lateral decubitus with the good lung down improves oxygenation in both spontaneously breathing and mechanically ventilated patients.
