# NotebookLM Source: ICU Oxygenation & Ventilation Monitoring

This lesson covers oxygenation and ventilation monitoring in the intensive care unit. Oxygenation monitoring includes PaO2, SaO2, SpO2, arterial-venous oxygen content difference, arterial oxygen content, mixed venous oxygen content, the alveolar air equation, the A-a gradient, the P/F ratio, oxygenation index, shunt equation, and Murray Lung Score.

PaO2 is the partial pressure of oxygen dissolved in plasma. SaO2 is hemoglobin oxygen saturation percentage. SpO2 is hemoglobin oxygen saturation percentage measured by pulse oximetry.

The arterial-venous oxygen content difference, C(a-v)O2, measures oxygen consumption by the tissues. The formula is C(a-v)O2 = CaO2 - CvO2. The normal value is 4 to 5 vol%. It is used in the Fick equation to calculate cardiac output in L/min or oxygen consumption in mL/min. C(a-v)O2 increases when CvO2 decreases. It requires an ABG and a Swan-Ganz catheter.

Arterial oxygen content, CaO2, is the best measurement of oxygen delivered to the tissues and the best index of oxygen transport. It estimates oxygen carried by hemoglobin plus oxygen dissolved in plasma. The formula is CaO2 = (Hb x 1.34 x SaO2) + (PaO2 x 0.003). Normal value is 17 to 20 vol%, or mL/dL.

Mixed venous oxygen content, CvO2, is the total amount of oxygen carried in mixed venous blood, measured in mL O2 per 100 mL blood. The formula is CvO2 = (Hb x 1.34 x SvO2) + (PvO2 x 0.003). Blood is drawn from the pulmonary artery through a balloon-tipped, flow-directed Swan-Ganz catheter. Normal value is 14 vol%, with a range of 12 to 16 vol%. CvO2 decreases when cardiac output decreases. SvO2 also decreases when cardiac output decreases.

The A-a gradient measures the difference between alveolar and arterial PO2. The formula is A-aDO2 = PAO2 - PaO2. On 100% oxygen, 25 to 65 torr is normal, 66 to 300 torr indicates V/Q mismatch, and more than 300 torr indicates shunting. Normal value varies directly with FiO2.

The alveolar air equation calculates the partial pressure of oxygen in the alveoli. The formula from the source is PAO2 = (Pb - PH2O) x FiO2 - PaCO2 / 0.8. Normal value varies directly with FiO2 and barometric pressure.

The P/F ratio is the ratio of partial pressure of arterial oxygen to inspired oxygen fraction. The formula is P/F ratio = PaO2 / FiO2. A value of 380 torr or greater is normal. Less than 300 torr indicates acute lung injury, and less than 200 torr indicates acute respiratory distress syndrome.

The oxygenation index measures the amount of ventilatory support required to provide the level of oxygenation. The formula is OI = Paw x FiO2 x 100 / PaO2. Normal OI is less than 10. Increasing oxygenation index means the patient's condition is worsening. The source recommends ECMO for newborns with OI greater than 40.

The shunt equation measures the portion of cardiac output that is shunted and does not participate in gas exchange. The formula is Qs/Qt = (A-aDO2 x 0.003) / ((A-aDO2 x 0.003) + C(a-v)O2). Arterial and mixed venous blood gases are drawn and analyzed to determine the A-aDO2 and C(a-v)O2. Normal value is 3% to 5%.

The Murray Lung Score is a composite score that quantifies lung status according to different injury features, including gas exchange, radiographic findings, and mechanics.

Ventilation monitoring includes PaCO2, capnometry and capnography, and dead space. PaCO2 is the partial pressure of CO2 dissolved in plasma. The source prompts review of normal PaCO2 values and asks how respiratory therapists can regulate CO2.

Dead space types include anatomical dead space, alveolar dead space, physiologic dead space, and dead space ventilation. Anatomical dead space is the volume of inhaled gas that does not reach the alveolar-capillary level. It is estimated as 1 mL per pound ideal body weight. Alveolar dead space is inhaled gas that reaches alveoli but does not take part in gas exchange because of lack of perfusion to air-filled alveoli. Physiologic dead space is the sum of anatomical and alveolar dead space. Diseases that may cause increased dead space include pulmonary embolism, emphysema, shock, obstruction of pulmonary vessels by masses, and pulmonary hypertension.

The dead space to tidal volume ratio, VD/VT, is the percentage of tidal volume that is dead space and does not participate in gas exchange. It represents ventilation without perfusion. The formula is VD/VT = (PaCO2 - PECO2) x 100 / PaCO2. Normal value is 20% to 40%, and up to 60% may occur for ventilator patients. PECO2 is the average PCO2 of exhaled air measured by capnograph. An increase in VD/VT indicates a dead-space-producing disease such as pulmonary embolus.

Capnometry is a numeric display of CO2 measurements from the airway. Capnography is a graphic display of CO2 measurement from the airway. Indications include verification of artificial airway placement, assessment of pulmonary circulation and respiratory status, and optimization of mechanical ventilation. There are no absolute contraindications.

Equipment includes mainstream capnography, sidestream capnography, and colorimetric CO2. In mainstream capnography, the sensor is located between the airway and ventilator circuit, and CO2 measurement occurs at the airway. In sidestream capnography, an adapter is placed between the airway and ventilator circuit, and a small volume of exhaled gas is aspirated and measured by the capnograph. Colorimetric CO2 is used for confirming tube placement after intubation and should change from purple to yellow. The source lists normal EtCO2 as above 5%. The color ranges are purple for EtCO2 below 0.5%, tan for 0.5% to 2%, and yellow for above 2%.

Limitations of colorimetric CO2 include false positive yellow color when contaminated with acidic substances such as gastric acid. It may not provide a reading if clogged with secretions or broken. CO2 may not be detected in low cardiac output states such as shock, cardiac arrest, or CPR with inadequate chest compressions.

Steps for use include verifying the physician's order, assembling equipment and supplies, washing hands, calibrating the monitor according to manufacturer instructions, and connecting the monitor to the patient's airway or as close to the airway as possible. PetCO2 is recorded as the highest value and can be expressed as percent of exhaled gas or as mm Hg. The source lists 5% and 38 mm Hg as normal examples.

Capnography interpretation is used for diagnosis and ventilator troubleshooting. The waveform can be analyzed for height, frequency, rhythm, baseline, and shape. Normal capnography has three phases: Phase I is end inspiration, Phase II is beginning expiration, and Phase III is the alveolar plateau. The waveform has alpha and beta angles. Increasing CO2 indicates worsening ventilation. Decreasing CO2 indicates hyperventilation.

The source includes a clinical note that mottling is blotchy, red-purple marbling of the skin. It usually occurs first on the feet and travels up the legs. Mottling before death is common and usually occurs during the final week of life, although it can occur earlier. It occurs when the heart can no longer pump blood effectively.

ETCO2 during CPR is used to evaluate chest compression effectiveness. Cardiac compressions transport CO2 to the alveoli, so EtCO2 directly correlates with cardiac output. The goal is to keep EtCO2 as high as possible. Effective compressions should result in EtCO2 of 10 to 20 mm Hg. EtCO2 is the earliest indicator of return of spontaneous circulation. A sudden rise in EtCO2 indicates that when the heart restarts, cardiac output and perfusion increase, transporting accumulated CO2 to the lungs where it is exhaled.
