# NotebookLM Source: Tracheal Airways, Laryngectomy & Tracheostomy Care

This lesson covers tracheal airways, laryngectomy, tracheostomy, tracheostomy tubes, speaking devices, and tracheostomy care. Objectives include assessing the need for and selecting an artificial airway, identifying complications and hazards associated with insertion of artificial airways, describing orotracheal and nasotracheal intubation of an adult, and assessing and confirming proper endotracheal tube placement. This lesson focuses on the airway devices and tracheostomy content from those objectives.

The laryngeal mask airway, or LMA, is a large-bore tube with an inflatable mask at the distal end. It forms a seal around the glottis while excluding the esophageal opening. It is indicated for airway protection and ventilation during short surgical procedures, difficult intubation, and as a conduit for intubation. A standard endotracheal tube can be inserted directly through the LMA into the trachea if necessary. Complete upper airway obstruction is a contraindication.

The King tube is a supraglottic airway inserted blindly into the esophagus. It is a curved tube with ventilation apertures located between two inflatable cuffs. A single valve and pilot balloon inflate both cuffs. The distal cuff seals the esophagus, the proximal cuff seals the oral pharynx, and a 15-mm adapter attaches to a standard breathing circuit or resuscitation bag. Indications include unconscious patients without purposeful movement, no gag reflex, inability to perform endotracheal intubation, apnea, or a respiratory rate below 6/min. Contraindications include responsive patients with an intact gag reflex, known esophageal disease, ingestion of caustic substances, and patients less than 4 feet tall.

The esophageal tracheal Combitube, or ETC, is used in the prehospital or emergency setting only and is not a long-term airway. It is a dual-lumen airway blindly inserted through the oropharynx into either the trachea or esophagus. The proximal end is separated into two color-coded tubes. The distal end combines a tracheal lumen and esophageal lumen, and both cuffs are inflated after insertion. Esophageal placement is most common. In esophageal placement, the esophagus is sealed by the distal cuff, ventilation is provided through the blue lumen, and gas is delivered through perforations above the distal cuff. In tracheal placement, the trachea is sealed by the distal cuff, ventilation is provided through the clear lumen, and gas is delivered through the distal opening.

Endotracheal tubes are clear, semi-rigid PVC tubes with anatomical curvature. They have a 15-mm connector or adapter, which is standard for all airway equipment. They also have a radiopaque line visible on x-ray, a Murphy eye, depth markers, a left-facing beveled tip, a pilot balloon, and a spring-loaded one-way valve that allows cuff inflation and deflation. The air-filled tracheal cuff seals the trachea so gas cannot escape during positive pressure ventilation and protects the lower airway from secretions, blood, and vomit entering the trachea.

Endotracheal tube types include uncuffed, nasal, wire-reinforced, subglottic suction, and double-lumen tubes. Uncuffed endotracheal tubes are primarily used in neonatal and pediatric patients. Nasal endotracheal tubes are designed for nasal intubation and are longer, curved to fit the upper anatomy, and smaller in diameter. Wire-reinforced endotracheal tubes are also called anode tubes. They have spiraled wire embedded in the wall, giving strength and flexibility while resisting kinking. They are indicated for head and neck surgery where the tube may be sharply bent or compressed.

Subglottic suction endotracheal tubes are also called Hi-Lo tubes or continuous aspiration of subglottic secretions, abbreviated CASS. They have a separate suction port just above the cuff. Continuous suction is provided through a separate tube connected to vacuum pressure of 20 mm Hg. These tubes reduce the incidence of ventilator-acquired or ventilator-associated pneumonia. Double-lumen endotracheal tubes are indicated for unilateral lung disease, independent lung ventilation, and surgical procedures such as lobectomy. A larger cuff seals the tracheal lumen and allows gas to flow into one bronchus. A smaller cuff seals the opposite bronchial lumen.

A laryngectomy is surgical removal of the patient's larynx, performed to treat upper airway carcinoma. After laryngectomy, there is no longer any connection between the patient's upper and lower respiratory tract. The patient initially breathes through a laryngectomy tube and cannot be orally or nasally intubated. The laryngectomy tube is removed after 3 to 6 weeks, and then the patient has a permanent stoma. Laryngectomy tubes maintain a patent airway after laryngectomy. They are made of soft pliable material, are available in various sizes, are usually shorter than a standard tracheostomy tube, and may have an inner cannula. Laryngectomy tubes do not have inflatable cuffs. If positive pressure ventilation is required, replace with a cuffed endotracheal tube.

A tracheotomy is the surgical procedure that creates an opening through the neck into the trachea. A tracheostomy is the opening or stoma that results from the tracheotomy. A tracheostomy tube is placed through this opening to provide an airway and remove secretions. Indications include failed intubation, long-term ventilation, and upper airway obstruction. A tracheostomy tube is easier to stabilize, suction, and tolerate. The patient can eat and may speak with a tracheal speaking device. Compared with oral or nasal endotracheal tubes, tracheostomy tubes have fewer hazards and less airway resistance.

Advantages of tracheostomy tubes include no upper airway complications, more comfort, more security, aid with feeding, oral care, and speech, intact glottic function, less work of breathing, reduced anatomical dead space, and improved ventilator weaning. Immediate complications in the first 24 hours include bleeding, which is the major hazard, pneumothorax, air embolism, and subcutaneous emphysema. Late complications include infection, hemorrhage, obstruction, and tracheoesophageal fistula.

Standard tracheostomy tubes include uncuffed, stainless steel, fenestrated, and foam-cuffed tubes. A standard white plastic trach tube has a removable inner cannula for cleaning, a soft cuff, and an obturator used to facilitate insertion. Uncuffed tubes are used primarily in pediatric patients and in adults who need frequent suctioning but not positive ventilation. They should not be used in unconscious patients. Stainless steel Jackson trach tubes are metal tubes with an inner cannula and are not for resuscitation or positive pressure ventilation; replace with a cuffed trach tube if needed.

Fenestrated tubes have an opening in the outer cannula above the cuff. They are used for weaning and temporary mechanical ventilation with the inner cannula. They allow the patient to breathe through the upper airway and speak when the tube is plugged. When plugging the tube, deflate the cuff, remove the inner cannula, and then plug the tracheostomy tube. Foam-cuffed tubes are also called Bivona tubes. Their foam-filled cuff expands to seal the trachea when the pilot tube is left open to atmosphere. Do not inflate the cuff with a syringe. A tracheal button is a rigid plastic tube about 1 inch in length that can be placed in the stoma after the trach tube is removed to maintain the stoma.

A tracheal speaking device is a one-way valve that attaches to the tracheostomy tube. During inspiration, the valve opens and air enters the lungs through the tube. During exhalation, the valve closes and air passes around the cuff and through the vocal cords. The cuff must be deflated. Before placing the valve, suction the airway if needed and suction the mouth and nose as needed. Deflate the airway cuff. Attach the valve to the top of the tracheostomy tube with a twisting motion to the right, about one-quarter turn, to prevent it from popping off with coughing. Remove the valve by twisting it off to the left.

Speaking valve hazards and cautions include removing the valve during aerosol treatments, not using the valve while sleeping, removing it immediately if the patient has difficulty breathing, and never using the valve on airways with the cuff inflated. Clean the valve daily with mild soapy water, rinse thoroughly with cool to warm water, and allow it to air dry completely. Do not use a brush, vinegar, peroxide, bleach, or alcohol on the valve. Replace the valve when it becomes sticky, noisy, or vibrates.

The tracheostomy tube cuff should be kept inflated whenever the patient is eating and whenever positive pressure ventilation is used, including mechanical ventilation.

Tracheostomy care is indicated to maintain airway patency, prevent infection, and keep the incision site clean and dry to promote comfort and healing. Follow hospital policy, especially for a fresh trach, often defined as one placed in the last 48 hours. Step 1 is assembling and checking equipment: PPE, sterile gloves, suction equipment, oxygen and manual ventilation device, tracheostomy care kit, new inner cannula, and new tracheostomy tube. The kit may include sterile gloves, basin, brush, cotton-tipped applicators, hydrogen peroxide, sterile water, 4x4 gauze, precut gauze, and trach tie.

Before tracheostomy care, explain the procedure to the patient and place the patient in semi-Fowler's position unless contraindicated. Suction the patient, remove the inner cannula, and replace a disposable inner cannula with a new one. For a non-disposable inner cannula, use a brush to clean the inside, inspect the lumen to confirm it is clean and clear of mucus, and place it in peroxide solution to soak. Remove gauze from behind the flange. Clean around the stoma with cotton-tipped applicators and 4x4s dampened with peroxide and sterile water. Dry the site with sterile 4x4s. Examine the stoma for redness, swelling, pus, or foul smell, and notify the physician if any are present. Replace split gauze dressing, do not cut 4x4s to use as dressing, change the tie or holder, rinse the inner cannula in sterile water or saline, replace it, make sure it is locked in place, and assess the patient.
