# NotebookLM Source: Suctioning & Endotracheal Intubation

This lesson covers suctioning and endotracheal intubation. Suctioning is the application of negative pressure, or vacuum, to the airways through a collecting tube such as a flexible catheter or suction tip. Suctioning is used to maintain airway patency, collect sputum specimens, and stimulate cough. Oral suctioning uses an oral suction device such as a Yankauer or tonsil suction device to suction the mouth and throat using aseptic technique.

Suctioning is indicated to maintain patency and integrity of the airway, remove accumulated secretions, respond to a sawtooth pattern on ventilator graphics, respond to increased peak pressures or decreased volume delivery in pressure control ventilation, remove visible secretions in the airway, respond to deterioration in ABG values or oxygen saturation, manage an obstructed airway, respond to depressed cough, and manage inability to swallow. The source lists epiglottitis or croup as a contraindication.

Suctioning hazards include atelectasis, mucosal trauma, contamination, hypoxemia leading to tachycardia or arrhythmias, bradycardia from vagus nerve stimulation, and bleeding from suctioning that is too vigorous. Mucosal trauma is the most common hazard. Hypoxemia leading to tachycardia or arrhythmias is the most severe hazard. To reduce risk, lubricate the catheter for nasal-tracheal suctioning, use gentle technique, use aseptic technique, suction only when indicated, apply suction while withdrawing for no more than 15 seconds, and clear the suction catheter with sterile water or saline.

The vacuum regulator adjusts vacuum pressure. Adult suction pressure is 120 to 150 mm Hg. Child suction pressure is 100 to 120 mm Hg. Infant suction pressure is 80 to 100 mm Hg. Catheter size is listed in French units, which refer to circumference. The external diameter of the suction catheter should be no greater than one-half the inside diameter of the endotracheal or tracheostomy tube. To choose a catheter, multiply the tube's inner diameter by 2 and then use the next smallest even catheter size. A patient with an 8.0 mm ID endotracheal tube should use a 14 French catheter. A 7.0 tube should use a 12 French catheter. A 6.0 tube should use a 10 French catheter.

Suctioning techniques include open and closed suctioning. Nasotracheal suctioning is open suctioning. Lubricate the catheter with sterile water-soluble lubricant. Insert it gently through the nostril, directing it toward the septum and floor of the nasal cavity. As the catheter enters the lower pharynx, place the patient's head in the sniffing position. Advance until the patient coughs or resistance is felt in the airway. Apply suction and withdraw the catheter.

Endotracheal suctioning is usually closed suctioning. 100% oxygen before and after suctioning is required, and oxygenation should be provided for at least 1 to 2 minutes. Close cardiac monitoring by ECG is important to detect problems. Sterile catheter, solutions, and gloves should be used each time. Suction the endotracheal tube first, then the mouth. Change the catheter after suctioning the mouth before suctioning the endotracheal tube again.

Mini-bronchoalveolar lavage, or mini-BAL, is a non-bronchoscopic method of performing a small-volume BAL for quantitative culture results. It is used to guide antibiotic therapy for patients with ventilator-associated pneumonia. The catheter design allows samples of uncontaminated distal lung specimens.

Endotracheal intubation establishes an artificial airway by placing a tube through the mouth or nose, through the glottis, and into the trachea. Indications include providing a patent airway, respiratory failure, a means for mechanical ventilation, and direct instillation of medication when IV access is unavailable. Source medication examples include Valium or Versed for sedation, atropine for bradycardia, Narcan for narcotic overdose, and epinephrine for asystole. When administering medication through the endotracheal tube, double the normal IV dose, flush with 10 mL saline, and hyperventilate for 30 seconds. A contraindication is legal documentation indicating the patient does not want intubation, such as DNI status.

Complications and hazards of intubation include upper airway trauma, aspiration or pneumonia, vocal cord paralysis, laryngospasm, dental accidents, broken teeth, right mainstem intubation, and esophageal intubation. Laryngospasm is the most serious complication.

Intubation routes include orotracheal and nasotracheal. Oral intubation advantages include placing a larger tube, ease of insertion, less airway resistance, and reduced risk of kinking. Disadvantages include gagging, coughing, and salivation when airway reflexes are intact, difficulty securing the tube, and mucosal irritation and ulceration of the lips and mouth. Nasotracheal intubation is indicated when the oral route is unavailable, such as maxillofacial injuries or oral surgery. It may be done blindly or under direct visualization with a standard or flexible laryngoscope. Nasal intubation advantages include more comfort, decreased gagging, less salivation, easier swallowing, improved oral care, a more secure airway, and improved communication. Disadvantages include sinusitis, epistaxis, otitis, a more difficult procedure, need for a smaller tube, increased airway resistance, and less effective suctioning.

Evidence of a difficult airway includes tracheal shift or deviation, enlarged thyroid, short receding mandible, enlarged tongue or macroglossia, bull neck, limited neck or cervical spine range of motion, cervical spine immobilization collar, small mouth opening, and Mallampati Class III or IV. A thin soft guide, also called a bougie, can be used if a clinician has difficulty passing the ETT through the glottic opening.

Step 1 of intubation is assembling and checking equipment. Equipment includes PPE, laryngoscope handle, Miller straight blade, Macintosh curved blade, Magill forceps for nasal intubation, Yankauer oral suction, various sizes of endotracheal tubes, stylet, 10-mL syringe, bite block or oropharyngeal airway, device to secure the ETT or cloth tape, BVM with 100% oxygen, carbon dioxide detector, and sedative and paralytic agents for RSI. All suction equipment should be assembled and vacuum pressure checked. The appropriate laryngoscope blade should be attached to its handle and the light checked. If the light does not function, check the bulb and batteries.

Select the appropriate tube size plus one size larger and one size smaller. ETTs are sized by internal diameter in millimeters. Preterm infant sizes are 2.5 to 3.0 mm, full-term infant sizes are 3.0 to 3.5 mm, adult female sizes are 7.0 to 7.5 mm, and adult male sizes are 8.0 to 8.5 mm. Oral intubation depth should be about 21 to 25 cm at the lips. Nasal intubation depth should be about 26 to 29 cm at the naris. The laryngoscope handle is always held in the left hand. A curved Macintosh blade is placed into the vallecula. A straight Miller blade lifts the epiglottis and is usually used for pediatric and neonatal patients. Adult blade size is 3, pediatric size is 2, term infant size is 1, and preterm infant size is 0.

Before insertion, inflate the tube cuff and check for leaks, then deflate the cuff. Lubricate the tube with water-soluble gel to ease insertion. Insert a stylet into the tube to add rigidity and maintain shape. The stylet tip must never extend beyond the ETT tip. Magill forceps are used only to aid nasal intubation by lifting the tube into the trachea.

Step 2 is positioning the patient. The head should be midline and tilted back in the sniffing position to improve visualization of the vocal cords. This is contraindicated in neck injury. Align the ear opening with the sternum and use pillows, folded towels, or blankets under the head, neck, and shoulders. Step 3 is preoxygenating and ventilating with BVM and 100% oxygen. CPAP or high-flow nasal cannula may also be used during preoxygenation. No more than 30 seconds should be used for any intubation attempt. If intubation fails, immediately ventilate and oxygenate for 3 to 5 minutes before the next attempt.

Step 4 is inserting the laryngoscope. Use the left hand to hold it and the right hand to open the mouth. Insert the blade into the right side of the mouth and move it toward the center, displacing the tongue left. Advance along the curve of the tongue until the epiglottis is visualized. Step 5 is visualizing the glottis. If the arytenoid cartilage and epiglottis are not visible, the blade may be too far advanced and may be in the esophagus. Maintain upward force and slowly withdraw until the larynx is seen. Step 6 is displacing the epiglottis. The Macintosh blade indirectly displaces it by placing the blade tip in the vallecula and lifting up and forward. The Miller blade directly displaces it by advancing over the posterior surface of the epiglottis and lifting up and forward. Avoid levering the laryngoscope against the teeth.

Step 7 is inserting the tube. Hold the tube in the right hand and advance through the glottis. Once the tube is seen passing through the glottis, advance the cuff 2 to 3 cm past the cords. Maintain the tube with the right hand, remove the laryngoscope and stylet, inflate the cuff with 5 to 10 mL air, remove the syringe, and ventilate the patient. Cricoid pressure, or Sellick maneuver, is indicated if the larynx is anterior or the patient is at risk of aspiration. BURP means backward, upward, rightward pressure. It compresses the esophagus and may help prevent gastric inflation.

Step 8 is assessing tube position. Inspect for bilateral chest expansion. Suspect right mainstem intubation if only the right side of the chest rises and falls. Auscultate for bilateral breath sounds. Use capnography or colorimetry; gold or yellow is good. Note the ETT depth at the upper incisors. Step 9 is stabilizing the tube with cloth tape or a commercial device. After stabilization, obtain a chest x-ray to confirm position. Locate the carina and the tip of the ETT. The ETT tip should be 2 to 3 cm above the carina.

Cuff pressure should be monitored with a cuff manometer. The pressure should be 25 to 35 cm H2O or 20 to 25 mm Hg. This helps prevent tracheal stenosis in both endotracheal and tracheostomy patients. Minimal seal and minimal leak techniques exist when no cuff manometer is available, but the source states neither technique is recommended anymore.
