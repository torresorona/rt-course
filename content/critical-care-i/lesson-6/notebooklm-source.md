# NotebookLM Source: Neurological, Systemic & Pleural Monitoring

This lesson covers neurological monitoring, other ICU monitoring, and pleural emergency procedures. Neurological monitoring includes neurologic status and examination, Glasgow Coma Scale, and intracranial pressure.

A neurologic exam includes history, mental status, pupillary response, eye movement, corneal response, gag reflex, respiratory rate and pattern, and motor and sensory evaluations. The Glasgow Coma Scale is the most widely used scoring system for acute neurologic disorders. It is based on eye response, motor response, and verbal response.

Intracranial pressure, or ICP, is a pressure measurement of cerebrospinal fluid in the space between the skull and brain. It is measured by inserting a catheter capable of measuring pressure. ICP monitoring is indicated for life-threatening intracranial hypertension, infection, and assessment of therapy used to reduce ICP.

ICP monitoring techniques include intraventricular catheter, subdural or subarachnoid screw called a bolt, epidural sensor, and intraparenchymal fiber optic catheter. The intraventricular catheter is the most accurate method. A hole is drilled through the skull and the catheter is inserted through the brain into the lateral ventricle. It can also drain excess fluid, but it is difficult to place when ICP is high. The subarachnoid screw or bolt is used when monitoring needs to be done right away. A hollow screw is inserted through a hole drilled in the skull and placed through the membrane that protects the brain and spinal cord, allowing the sensor to record from inside the subdural space. The epidural sensor is inserted between the skull and dural tissue through a hole drilled in the skull. It is less invasive than other methods but cannot remove excess cerebrospinal fluid.

Measurements include intracranial pressure and cerebral perfusion pressure. Normal ICP in a supine patient is 10 to 15 mm Hg. Normal CPP is 83 to 93 mm Hg. CPP is calculated as MAP minus ICP. The source uses MAP of 93 mm Hg based on an assumed blood pressure of 120/80.

Increased ICP is bad. ICP of 15 to 20 mm Hg starts to compress the capillary bed. ICP of 30 to 35 mm Hg impedes venous drainage and edema starts to develop in uninjured tissue. ICP greater than 50 mm Hg causes cerebral ischemia. Decreased CPP is bad. If CPP is 0, perfusion stops and the brain dies.

The source recommends initiating treatment if ICP is greater than 20 mm Hg. Therapy to reduce ICP includes hyperventilation with a target PaCO2 of 25 to 30 torr. Hyperventilation temporarily decreases ICP through cerebral vasoconstriction. The effect may last minutes to hours and should be discontinued after 48 hours. Other therapy includes lowering jugular venous pressure by avoiding neck flexion, head turning, and trach ties that are too tight. Minimize increases in central venous pressure, keep the head of bed elevated at least 30 degrees, minimize straining, retching, and coughing, and minimize PEEP. Sedation and analgesia may include narcotics and benzodiazepines, while avoiding hypotension. Osmotic agents such as mannitol and hypertonic saline remove fluid from the brain.

Other monitoring includes renal and kidney function, liver function, and global monitoring indices. The kidney filters waste products and regulates volume and electrolytes. Renal monitoring includes BUN, creatinine, potassium, HCO3, and urine volume. Urine volume reflects kidney perfusion. Polyuria is urine output greater than 3 L daily. Oliguria is urine output less than 0.4 L daily.

The liver detoxifies waste from metabolism, digestion, and poisons. Indications for liver function evaluation include abdominal pain, jaundice, unexplained fever, nausea, and weight loss.

Global monitoring indices estimate illness acuity and mortality risk. They increase understanding of treatment effectiveness and aid in developing treatment standards. ICU scoring systems are collected on the first day. APACHE stands for Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation. APACHE II is designed to measure disease severity for adult patients admitted to intensive care units. Higher scores mean more severe illness and higher risk of death.

Pleural disease content includes pneumothorax treatment, needle decompression, and chest tube thoracostomy. Needle decompression is indicated for tension pneumothorax. Contraindications include local skin infection at the proposed insertion site and coagulation disorder. Equipment includes cleansing solution, 14-gauge or larger needle with catheter, and tape. The procedure uses the second intercostal space at the mid-clavicular line. Clean the area with iodine solution. Insert a 14-gauge or larger needle attached to a catheter perpendicular to the chest. The needle should be 5 to 8 cm long depending on muscle and fat tissue covering the rib cage. Go over the top of the rib because veins, arteries, and nerves run beneath the ribs. Advance until a hissing sound is heard, which is air leaving the pleural space. Remove the needle while keeping the catheter in place and secure the catheter to the patient's body.

Chest tube thoracostomy places a tube into the pleural space to remove air, blood, or pleural fluid from the thoracic cavity. Indications include pneumothorax that is recurrent, persistent, under tension, or bilateral; any pneumothorax in a patient on positive pressure ventilation; hemothorax; pleural effusion; emphysema; and chylothorax. Relative contraindications are local skin infection at the proposed insertion site and coagulation disorder. There are no absolute contraindications.

Equipment includes chest tube, chest tube insertion kit, sterile gloves, gown, mask, and drapes, anesthetic such as 1% lidocaine, drainage system, and Heimlich valve. Chest tubes are sized in French units and range from infants to adults. Small tubes are used for air and larger tubes for fluid. Tubes may be curved or straight and made of PVC or silicone. The chest tube insertion kit may include scalpel and blade, two straight hemostats, suture, suture scissors, needle driver, petroleum gauze, 20-mL syringe, and 18-gauge needle.

A drainage system collects chest drainage such as air, blood, and effusions. Components include collection chamber, water seal chamber, and suction control chamber. Expiratory positive pressure from the patient, such as cough or Valsalva, helps push air and fluid out of the chest. Gravity helps fluid drainage when the chest drainage system is below the level of the chest. Suction can improve the speed at which air and fluid are pulled from the chest.

In a one-bottle system, the straw attached to the chest tube from the patient is placed under 2 cm of fluid to create a water seal. It only works if air is leaving the chest. If fluid drains into the bottle, it increases water seal depth and makes it harder for air to push through, which could leave air in the chest. In a two-bottle system, the first bottle collects drainage and the second bottle provides the water seal, so the water seal remains at 2 cm. In a three-bottle system, the third bottle controls suction. The depth of water in the suction bottle determines negative pressure transmitted to the chest, not the vacuum regulator reading.

A Heimlich valve is a one-way rubber flutter valve that prevents air from entering the pleural space. The proximal end attaches to the chest tube. The distal end connects to suction or remains open to atmosphere. Advantages are that it is small, lightweight, and allows the patient to ambulate. Disadvantages are that it can only drain air, and fluid can cause the rubber to stick so it no longer works.

Chest tube placement uses external landmarks such as clavicle, ribs, and mid-axillary line. Placement depends on what the chest tube is draining. Gas collects in upper chest areas, and fluid accumulates in gravity-dependent areas. For fluid, use the fifth, sixth, or seventh intercostal space posterior of the axillary line. For air, use the third or fourth intercostal space anterior of the axillary line.

Before the procedure, place the patient supine. For anterior axillary line insertions, the head of the bed can be elevated 30 to 60 degrees to lower the diaphragm and decrease risk of injury to the diaphragm, spleen, or liver. Raise the patient's arm above the head on the insertion side to expose the area. Supplemental oxygen is usually applied. Note baseline vital signs and SpO2. Continue monitoring heart rate, color, and oxygen saturation.

Post-procedure, obtain a chest x-ray to verify correct catheter placement and check for residual or reaccumulated air or fluid. Monitor the water-seal chamber. The water level rises and falls with respirations. If pneumothorax is active, bubbling is continuous. As the pneumothorax resolves, bubbling becomes intermittent during inspiration. When pneumothorax is resolved, bubbling stops. Monitor vital signs, SpO2, changes in respiratory distress and breath sounds, and color and amount of drainage.
