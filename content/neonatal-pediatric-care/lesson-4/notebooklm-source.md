This lesson is about getting a neonatal or pediatric patient onto support — starting with non-invasive CPAP, understanding the physiology that makes these patients different, and then dialing in the conventional ventilator settings. Stay within the source material and walk through it in order.

## NCPAP and Non-Invasive Ventilation

CPAP for infants — we call it NCPAP — works much like it does in an adult, except we use nasal-specific masks and nasal prongs. Its job is to improve functional residual capacity and static lung compliance. The one prerequisite: the patient has to have adequate spontaneous effort and rate to be placed on non-invasive ventilation.

We reach for CPAP when arterial oxygenation is inadequate despite a higher FiO₂, and these are usually the RDS babies. CPAP is fundamentally a lung-recruitment tool, and it helps counter the PPHN and lung collapse tied to surfactant deficiency in premature infants. We start at 4 to 6 centimeters of water and increase by half or one increment at a time to improve oxygenation.

Here are the numbers that matter. Indications for CPAP: a PaO₂ less than 50, or an FiO₂ requirement greater than 60 percent. Contraindications: respiratory arrest, a pH less than 7.25, or a PaCO₂ greater than 50 — and the reason for that last one is crucial: CPAP is not indicated for CO₂ removal. Beyond the numbers, CPAP is indicated for respiratory distress, abnormal breathing patterns, pulmonary edema, post-extubation failure, lung disease, apnea of prematurity, obstructive sleep apnea, decreased lung volumes on radiograph, pneumonia, and tracheomalacia. We wean from CPAP once the FiO₂ is below 0.30 to 0.40, the work of breathing has dropped, and the chest film and clinical picture show the underlying problem has resolved.

A quick example to anchor it: an infant on nasal cannula with an SpO₂ of 86 percent, an FiO₂ requirement of 70 percent, and an ABG showing a PaO₂ of 48 and a PCO₂ of 45. The PCO₂ is fine, but oxygenation is failing on high FiO₂ — so the right move is to place the patient on NCPAP.

## The Physiology That Makes Neonates Different

Conventional mechanical ventilation is just the delivery of bulk flow of humidified gas into and out of the lungs. The mechanics are similar to adults, but the physiology is very different. The tidal volume you actually deliver depends on two properties: compliance and resistance. Compliance is how easily the lungs distend — how easy it is to get air in and stretch the chest wall. Resistance is the tendency of the airways to resist airflow because of friction.

Multiply those two together and you get the respiratory time constant — the time needed for a change in airway pressure to equilibrate. Time constant equals compliance times resistance. This matters enormously in these patients. A neonate right after birth often has stiff lungs from insufficient surfactant, meaning low compliance and therefore a short time constant — the lungs fill and empty fast, so they need shorter I-to-E ratios than adults. A pediatric patient with asthma or other high-resistance, impaired airflow has a long time constant — more time is required to fill and empty, so they need longer inspiratory and expiratory times.

## Infant Ventilators

Historically, neonatal ventilators were continuous flow, pressure limited, and time cycled. That changed once ventilators could detect tiny flow changes using a pneumotachograph. The pneumotachograph sits proximal to the airway and detects the patient's flow changes, which allows flow triggering even for the smallest infants, and breaths can be controlled by either volume or pressure.

When you first apply support, remember that infant and pediatric patients sit at wildly different levels of lung development, so every patient has their own set of matching volumes and pressures. While bag-mask ventilating with a manometer — a Jackson-Reese or flow-inflating bag — you can actually visualize the pressure needed to oxygenate the patient during resuscitation, and you can feel the difference in compliance as you squeeze the bag. The skill is to recognize the disease process and adjust tidal volumes and I-to-E ratios to match. And a structural note: neonatal ventilator guidelines are fairly straightforward and driven by the condition and disease process, while pediatric guidelines vary by hospital because pediatric development changes so much across age groups.

## Conventional Ventilator Settings

Start with peak inspiratory pressure. Use the pressure required to move the patient's chest during bag-mask ventilation. PIP should not exceed 30 centimeters of water, because higher pressures cause ventilator-induced lung injury — but that rule can be broken in cases of premature birth or chest-wall rigidity from a fentanyl infusion.

PEEP is your baseline pressure above zero, used to prevent alveolar collapse and maintain recruitment throughout the breath. Set it too low and you lose FRC, create V/Q mismatch, and cause hypoxemia; set it too high and you overdistend and injure the lung. Start it at 4 to 6 centimeters of water, possibly higher, and set it in conjunction with PIP. The gap between PIP and PEEP is the delta P, or ventilation pressure, and increasing or decreasing that delta P increases or decreases your tidal volume.

For tidal volume itself, use 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram for normal lung function, and 4 to 5 milliliters per kilogram for ARDS or lung injury. Rate runs 40 to 60 per minute for neonates and anywhere from 20 to 60 depending on the child's age, set according to the desired PaCO₂ — and some permissive hypercapnia is allowed depending on the hospital. Inspiratory time is 0.2 to 0.4 seconds for neonates and up to 1 second for pediatrics, chosen by the disease process. And oxygen concentration is kept as low as possible, because the immature neonatal lung is especially vulnerable to oxygen toxicity since the immune system can't yet defend against super-oxidants. Keep premature-infant saturation between 88 and 95 percent so retinopathy of prematurity doesn't develop.

## AARC Indications and Hazards for Intubation

The AARC indications to intubate a pediatric or infant patient are apnea, hypoxemia with a PaO₂ under 50, hypercapnia with a pH under 7.20 to 7.25, acidosis despite CPAP with supplemental oxygen, RDS, neurological compromise, and congenital issues.

On the flip side, the hazards and contraindications include air leak syndromes secondary to barotrauma and volutrauma, chronic lung disease from positive pressure ventilation and oxygen toxicity, airway complications like tracheomalacia, air leak, and subglottic stenosis, increased work of breathing, pneumonias, and decreased venous return.

Air leak syndrome deserves its own definition: it's air collecting within the baby's chest but outside the normal air cells of the lungs, where it presses on the lungs and makes breathing very difficult. Several forms can hit a neonate — pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, and pulmonary interstitial emphysema — and they're more likely in underdeveloped lungs.

## Monitoring and Assessing Outcome

For monitoring, watch O₂ and CO₂. Keep PaO₂ above 40 and below 70 to prevent ROP, and keep PaCO₂ at a level that holds the pH above 7.25. To judge whether ventilation is working, look for a reduction in work of breathing, radiographic evidence of improved lung volumes, a subjective improvement in lung volumes, and improved gas exchange — specifically a PaO₂ above 50 with an FiO₂ under 60 percent, and reversal of respiratory acidosis back above a pH of 7.25.
