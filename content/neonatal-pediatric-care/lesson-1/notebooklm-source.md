This lesson lays the groundwork for everything in neonatal and pediatric respiratory care: the basic terms, the fetal shunts that make a newborn's circulation so different from ours, the maternal factors that put a baby at risk, and the tests we use to assess the fetus before it is even born. Keep the discussion strictly to these source concepts and move through them in order.

## Basic Terms

The word neonatal comes from "neo," meaning new, and "natal," meaning pertaining to birth or origin. Neonatal care covers both mature and premature infants. A mature infant is born between 38 and 42 weeks of gestation. Gestation simply means the period of time between conception and birth, during which the embryo or fetus develops in the uterus. A premature infant is born before 38 weeks of gestation and is automatically considered high risk. And the word congenital means a disease or physical abnormality that is present at birth — examples include gastroschisis, omphalocele, and patent ductus arteriosus.

Neonatal respiratory care is difficult specifically because of the anatomical differences of the newborn. There is heart shunting, there are underdeveloped lungs in infants born before 38 weeks, and there are weight and size differences that make everything harder.

Pediatric care, by contrast, is much more like adult respiratory care — there are established disease processes and ventilator management strategies. The primary difference is where the child or adolescent is in their growth, which is constantly changing. Pediatrics can include patients anywhere from infant to 21 years of age, and some patients are followed by the pediatric team even past 21 if they had an established disease process during their pediatric years, such as cancer.

The big theme to plant early: maternal factors have a major effect on the health of the child at birth, so assessment of the mother should always be done initially.

## Fetal Circulation and the Three Shunts

In the womb, blood does not flow the way it does after birth. Three bypass pathways, or shunts, exist to push oxygenated blood from mom to the developing organs while skipping the non-working lungs and liver. These are the ductus venosus, the ductus arteriosus, and the foramen ovale. The single most important idea is this: if any of these shunts stay open after birth, oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix, and that mixing causes problems in the infant.

The ductus venosus is a shunt between the liver and the inferior vena cava. It takes two-thirds of the oxygenated blood coming from mom and routes it past the liver so it gets back to the heart quickly. It closes rapidly once placental blood flow stops, which is why clamping the umbilical cord helps close it.

The ductus arteriosus sits between the pulmonary artery and the aorta, and it shunts blood away from the non-functional lungs. In utero, pulmonary vascular pressure is higher than systemic pressure, so blood naturally takes the path of least resistance away from the lungs. This is normal and necessary because the lungs can't oxygenate or ventilate yet — the baby depends on placental blood. After birth, as PaO₂ and PaCO₂ change in the pulmonary capillaries, those capillaries dilate, pulmonary vascular resistance drops, and the ductus arteriosus constricts and closes.

Here's a clinically important twist: the hormone prostaglandin keeps the ductus open, and it can actually be given as a drug to maintain patency of the ductus after birth. That's lifesaving in certain ductal-dependent congenital heart defects. Normally, prostaglandin slowly dissipates after birth. When the vessel smooth muscle senses that drop in prostaglandin combined with the rising PaO₂, it triggers constriction.

The foramen ovale is the shunt between the two atria, letting blood flow from the right atrium to the left atrium through the interatrial septal wall. It lets the oxygenated blood arriving via the ductus venosus get to the left side of the heart quickly so it can reach the body. It usually closes about three weeks after birth. As circulation normalizes, left-sided heart pressure — systemic vascular resistance, or SVR — rises and presses a flap of tissue over the foramen ovale, sealing it shut.

## Maternal Risk Factors

Maternal factors that signal a possible adverse outcome for the neonate fall into three buckets: medical, physical, and social. Gestational diabetes is associated with a large-for-gestational-age baby, congenital malformations, RDS, and hypoglycemia. Maternal age matters at both ends: under 17 years brings low birth weight and prematurity, while over 35 years brings prematurity and chromosomal defects like Down syndrome. Pregnancy-induced hypertension is linked to prematurity, small-for-gestational-age babies, and preeclampsia.

Two placental problems are worth naming. Placenta previa is when the placenta attaches over the opening of the uterus — the cervix — raising the risk of premature delivery, bleeding, and a small-for-gestational-age baby. Abruptio placentae is when the placenta detaches from the uterine wall, or blood builds up between the placenta and the wall, leading to fetal asphyxia and bleeding.

On the social side, alcohol consumption is associated with small gestational age, CNS dysfunction, mental retardation, and facial dysmorphology. Smoking is associated with small gestational age, prematurity, mental retardation, and sudden infant death syndrome. Drug use is associated with placental abruption, intrauterine growth retardation, prematurity, CNS abnormalities, and withdrawal disorders.

And the placenta itself is the organ of respiration for the fetus during roughly 40 weeks of gestation — it extracts oxygen and excretes CO₂, with fetal hemoglobin and maternal hemoglobin exchanging the gases. It is about 55 centimeters long and contains two arteries and one vein.

## Fetal Assessment and Tests

Before birth, we assess the fetus with fetal scoring systems, ultrasonography, amniocentesis, fetal heart rate monitoring, and fetal blood gas analysis.

Amniocentesis is the direct sampling and quantitative assessment of amniotic fluid. That fluid can reveal meconium — fetal bowel contents — as well as blood and sloughed cells, and those sloughed cells let us detect certain genetic abnormalities in utero.

Two amniotic-fluid tests assess surfactant. The L/S ratio measures two phospholipids, lecithin and sphingomyelin, that the fetus synthesizes. The ratio increases with gestational age, and a 2-to-1 ratio indicates stable surfactant production in a mature lung. Phosphatidylglycerol is another lipid found in surfactant; a value greater than 1 percent means less than a 1 percent chance of respiratory distress.

Fetal heart rate monitoring tracks heart rate against uterine contractions to spot a fetus in distress. Normal fetal heart rate is 100 to 160 beats per minute, and tachycardia can mean fetal hypoxemia, prematurity, or maternal fever. Decelerations are temporary drops in heart rate, graded mild at less than 15 beats per minute, moderate at 15 to 45, and severe at more than 45. They come in early, late, and variable flavors, and some of them warn us about constriction of the umbilical cord lifeline.

Speaking of that lifeline, the umbilical cord carries umbilical arteries and a vein, all wrapped in a thick gelatinous material called Wharton's jelly. During fetal movement the cord can pinch and interrupt blood flow, especially once the amniotic fluid is displaced during birth. Two specific dangers: a prolapsed cord, where the cord slips through the cervix ahead of the baby — and if it's compressed inside the uterus that's called an occult prolapse — and a nuchal cord, where the cord wraps around the infant's neck.

## Normal Birth and Delivery

Finally, delivery happens one of two ways. A vaginal delivery lets the baby clear secretions by being squeezed through the birth canal. A cesarean section cuts through the abdominal wall to remove the baby and is becoming much more common. That missing "squeeze" in a C-section is exactly why some of these babies retain lung fluid — a setup for transient tachypnea of the newborn that we'll see again in the disease processes lesson.
