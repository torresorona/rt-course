# NotebookLM Source: The Newborn Assessment & RDS

This lesson is about what we actually do in the first minutes and hours of a baby's life: how we evaluate the newborn at delivery, how we resuscitate, the scores and classifications we assign, and how we recognize and treat respiratory distress syndrome. Keep everything tied to the source material and walk through it in this order.

## Evaluating the Newborn

Every newborn should be assessed immediately upon delivery. In a high-risk birth, the team in the operating room usually includes a doctor, a nurse, and a respiratory therapist, all there for immediate resuscitation. The good news is that more than 90 percent of babies will not need an intervention. The two groups most likely to need help are infants with evidence of meconium and premature infants. The first steps for any newborn are simple: dry the baby, warm the baby, place it in an incubator, and assess.

A quick word on lung development, because it explains who is viable. Between weeks 17 and 26 the airways are increasing in length and diameter, but the capillary system surrounding the lungs doesn't develop adequately until weeks 24 to 25. Once the primitive alveoli and the capillaries are close enough for gas exchange — around weeks 23 to 26 — the infant becomes viable and can be born and supported. And meconium, just to define it here, is the infant's first stool, a very sticky green-black substance that causes significant respiratory problems if it's inhaled.

## Resuscitating the Newborn

The single most important action a respiratory therapist takes in newborn resuscitation is effective ventilation, delivered through effective bag-mask ventilation. The rule to lock in: if a premature infant has a heart rate less than 100 beats per minute or is apneic, start bag-mask ventilation first, at a rate of 40 to 60 per minute, using room air.

Oxygen is dosed by gestational age and kept as low as possible. For infants greater than 35 weeks, start at 21 percent. For infants less than 35 weeks, start at 30 percent. Effective positive pressure ventilation usually produces a rapid improvement in heart rate. Initial bag-mask pressures may run 30 to 40 centimeters of water, particularly for premature deliveries. Here's the critical sequence: if the heart rate keeps dropping below 60 per minute, apply PPV first for 30 seconds. If there is no recovery within those first 30 seconds, then you add chest compressions.

## Scores and Classifications

The APGAR score is an objective system for rapidly evaluating a newborn, assigned at one minute and again at five minutes of life. A score of 7 or higher is normal. A score around 7 may need some intervention, and a score less than 6 after one minute may require more aggressive support.

The Ballard Maturational Assessment determines gestational age using six physical and neurological signs to assign a week of gestation to the newborn.

Weight classifications are worth memorizing cold. Low birth weight is under 2500 grams. Very low birth weight is under 1500 grams. Extremely low birth weight is under 1000 grams. These weights can be compared against the Ballard score to define the developmental status of the child.

The Colorado intrauterine growth chart plots weight against gestational age across a large population of infants to create a growth curve. Babies in the 90th percentile and above are large for gestational age, and babies in the 10th percentile and below are small for gestational age. Small preterm infants carry the highest risk across the board: their lungs aren't ready for gas exchange so they develop RDS, their digestive tracts struggle to absorb fats, their immune systems can't fight infection, their large surface-area-to-weight ratio drives heat loss and poor thermoregulation, and their underdeveloped vasculature makes them prone to hemorrhage, especially in the developing ventricles of the brain.

## Vital Signs

Normal infant respiratory rate is 40 to 60 breaths per minute, and the lower the gestational age, the higher the likely rate. Above 60 is tachypnea, and anything under 40 should be checked against the child's own baseline. Normal infant heart rate is 100 to 160 beats per minute, best assessed by listening to the apical pulse at the fifth intercostal space, midclavicular line.

For pulses, check the femoral and the brachial sites. Weak pulses point to hypotension, shock, or vasoconstriction. Two patterns are high-yield: bounding peripheral pulses occur with major left-to-right shunting through a patent ductus arteriosus, and a strong brachial pulse with a weak femoral pulse suggests a PDA or coarctation of the aorta.

## The Silverman Score

The Silverman scoring system identifies the severity of lung disease after birth. Because auscultating an infant's chest is hard — they're small and sound transfers strangely — we judge breathing difficulty by watching for physical signs: nasal flaring, cyanosis, expiratory grunting, tachypnea, retractions, and paradoxical breathing.

## Respiratory Distress Syndrome

RDS is expressed immediately after birth and is most likely caused by insufficient surfactant. You see tachypnea with worsening retractions, paradoxical breathing, audible grunting, and nasal flaring. With increased fluid and atelectasis, auscultation reveals decreased breath sounds or fine crackles. Cyanosis may or may not be present. The chest x-ray is the definitive finding: diffuse, hazy, reticulogranular — or ground glass — densities, with air bronchograms and low lung volumes.

It helps to understand what each sign is doing. Nasal flaring decreases airway resistance and reduces airway collapse during the negative force of inhalation. Grunting splints the airway by forcing air against a partially closed glottis, and it signals alveolar collapse. Retractions — suprasternal, substernal, and intercostal — indicate increased work of breathing, especially with decreased compliance. Paradoxical breathing is that see-saw pattern where the chest wall draws in while the abdomen stays put. One crucial caveat: cyanosis can be completely absent despite severe hypoxemia, because high fetal hemoglobin and hyperbilirubinemia can keep an infant looking pink even at a PaO₂ of 30.

The pathophysiology is a snowball. The major factors are surfactant deficiency below 34 weeks, decreased alveolar surface area from collapse and low compliance, increased small-airway compliance, and the presence of the ductus arteriosus. Low oxygenation and ventilation drive up pulmonary vascular resistance, which causes right-to-left shunting — that's PPHN — and the shunting worsens as hypoxemia worsens. Hypoxemia and the acidosis from poor ventilation then reduce surfactant even further and worsen compliance. Treatment centers on CPAP and PEEP, with high-frequency ventilation reserved for rapid deterioration. Optimizing PEEP or CPAP improves oxygenation and also improves FRC and ventilation.

## Surfactant Deficiency and Therapy

Surfactant production begins at 24 weeks. But before 34 to 35 weeks, even though the amount is adequate, it's trapped inside the alveolar type II cells and can't be released to lower surface tension. Think LaPlace's law — high surface tension means decreased compliance. So the cascade goes: surfactant deficiency causes decreased compliance and high surface tension, which collapses alveoli into atelectasis and raises the work of breathing; then that high surface tension pulls fluid out of the pulmonary capillaries, dropping compliance even more.

For replacement, there are three preparations in the United States, generally derived from bovine or porcine sources. Beractant is Survanta, calfactant is Infasurf, and poractant alfa is Curosurf. Some centers give prophylactic surfactant to infants under 1500 grams. All surfactants are delivered through the endotracheal tube for rapid distribution, with the infant usually positioned on their sides per the manufacturer's guidelines — though they can receive it supine if they can't be repositioned. Don't memorize the dosing chart; just know the three drug-and-brand pairs and that they all go in through the ETT.

## Blood Gases and Saturation Targets

We use capillary blood gases and pulse oximetry to screen for both congenital heart defects and respiratory disorders. Keep pre-term infants above 88 percent saturation with a PaO₂ of 40 to 70, and keep term infants and pediatrics above 95 percent.

A patent ductus arteriosus and patent foramen ovale can produce right-to-left shunting that lowers saturation through mixing. We detect it with pre- and post-ductal probes: one on the right hand, which is preductal, and one on the left lower leg, which is postductal. A difference of more than 5 percent between them indicates a right-to-left shunt, most likely a patent ductus arteriosus and patent foramen ovale.

The capillary stick, drawn from the heel of the foot, is important for monitoring PCO₂ and electrolytes. Its oxygen value is inaccurate because it's a mixed venous sample. But here's the clinical pearl: if an infant has chronically high PCO₂ with normal oxygenation, the capillary stick is the best method for assessing that PCO₂ status.

## Pediatric Assessment

Assessing pediatric patients is hard because of age differences and rapid growth. Start with adequate perfusion and heart rate, beginning with airway patency. Compare heart rate and respiratory rate to an age-appropriate chart — generally 20 to 40 breaths per minute and a heart rate of 60 to 100 are acceptable, though younger infants and toddlers can run higher. Asthma, allergic reactions, and infections are common, so effective medication use is essential.

For recognizing distress: accessory muscle use, grunting, flaring, and retraction are signs of respiratory distress, broadly called RDS. Head bobbing, chin up, and abdominal expansion signal impending respiratory failure. And levels of alertness — fully awake, agitated, minimally responsive, unresponsive — help decide whether the child needs an advanced airway. Finally, remember that some newborns with cardiac anomalies depend on their intracardiac shunts to survive. Increased saturation normally constricts the ductus arteriosus, which is usually good, but in an infant with a ductal-dependent defect — like hypoplastic left heart syndrome, where the left heart structures are poorly developed — that ductus is the lifeline for systemic blood supply, and premature closure is dangerous.
