# NotebookLM Source: Neonatal & Pediatric Respiratory Equipment

This lesson is the equipment tour — the monitors, gases, beds, and oxygen devices that make up neonatal and pediatric respiratory care, plus how we actually employ basic therapies at the bedside. Stay within the source and move through it in order.

## Transcutaneous Monitoring

Transcutaneous blood gas monitoring lets us continuously monitor a patient's PaO₂ and PCO₂ without repeatedly drawing blood gases. The key difference from a regular saturation monitor is that a transcutaneous monitor sees both oxygen and carbon dioxide — reported as PtcO₂ and PtcCO₂. It pulls this off by heating the skin, which arterializes the capillaries underneath and increases the skin's permeability to oxygen and CO₂, so those gases diffuse more readily to the sensor.

A few factors affect accuracy. Age and perfusion status — vasoconstriction, dehydration, low cardiac output — all directly impact the reading. Premature infants, neonates, and children actually give the greatest accuracy because of their skin composition, which makes changes easier to detect. We indicate transcutaneous monitoring for hemodynamically stable patients who need PaCO₂ monitoring but have trouble collecting blood or have low hemoglobin counts, and diagnostically to assess functional shunts or a patient's response to an oxygen challenge for congenital heart disease. The cautions: the heating can cause thermal damage, so reposition the electrode every 2 to 6 hours, and remember stabilization can take up to 20 minutes after you move it. Watch for false negatives that could lead to false treatment, trend the values against actual blood gases, and know that readings can vary by more than 10 percent even with proper placement — it's not a 100 percent reliable way to determine PaCO₂ and PaO₂.

## Inhaled Nitric Oxide

Nitric oxide is a selective pulmonary vasodilator used for newborns on mechanical ventilation primarily for hypoxic respiratory failure. It works by fixing shunts: the body naturally constricts the pulmonary vasculature around alveoli that aren't being well ventilated, and iNO dilates those constricted capillaries around healthy alveoli to overcome the shunting. The payoff is improved oxygenation and a reduced need for ECMO. The normal starting dose is 20 PPM, and the therapeutic range is 2 to 20 PPM.

We indicate iNO for hypoxic respiratory failure, for term and near-term neonates over 34 weeks with PPHN, for a gradient between preductal and postductal SpO₂, for congenital diaphragmatic hernia, and for an oxygenation index over 25 — where OI equals Paw times FiO₂ divided by PaO₂.

Weaning and toxicity are critical. NO combines with oxygen to form nitrogen dioxide, which is far more toxic and must be kept below 2 PPM. iNO can also form toxic molecules like peroxynitrite that damage tissue. We wean by 50 percent down to 1 PPM and then come off, and we increase FiO₂ as we wean to prevent rebound hypoxemia and a return to PPHN. We also monitor methemoglobin, which forms when NO combines with oxyhemoglobin to create a ferrous form of hemoglobin that can no longer take up or release oxygen — keep it below 5 percent of total hemoglobin.

## ECMO

ECMO, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, is a modified form of cardiopulmonary bypass for relatively long-term pulmonary or cardiopulmonary support when maximum medical intervention has failed. Blood gets pumped through an artificial lung called a Quadrox, where it's oxygenated, then warmed and returned to the body. There are two flavors. Veno-arterial, or VA, supports both the heart and the lungs — a cannula goes into the right internal jugular vein and down to the right atrium, and another goes into the right common carotid artery to return oxygenated blood to the aortic arch. On VA, a higher pump RPM can produce a higher blood pressure. Veno-venous, or VV, supports only the lungs, with cannulas in the right internal jugular vein and the right femoral vein, and it provides less cardiac support than VA.

## Incubators and Radiant Warmers

Most NICUs have radiant warmers and isolettes, which are incubators. These beds can deliver oxygen, suction, and heat. The closed incubator gives a quiet, isolated environment for the newborn to feed and grow and is great for holding exact temperatures, but it makes the patient hard to reach in an emergency. The radiant warmer is used more for transport and in early or late stages, gives an open environment to manipulate the patient and perform CPR, and is the standard unit in delivery rooms — and it can take oxygen canisters on the back. The respiratory therapist's main role here is to stock and maintain the supplies in the drawers beneath the beds: suction, blades, nasal cannulas, an ambu bag with mask, a heat probe, a saturation probe, and towels.

## Oxygen Delivery Devices

A handful of formulas help size an endotracheal tube. Tube internal diameter is age plus 16, divided by 4, or alternatively height in centimeters divided by 20. Tube length is 12 plus age over 2 for oral, or 15 plus age over 2 for nasal.

Now the devices. A nasal cannula works from premature infants all the way to adults, delivering low flow from 0.25 milliliters per minute up to 6 liters per minute; it's tolerated by all ages, but FiO₂ is inaccurate, excessive flows can cause inadvertent CPAP in infants, and you can get precise FiO₂ with an oxygen blender. An oxyhood is for premature infants up to about 6 months and delivers 21 to 100 percent with warmed gas at higher flows of 7 liters per minute and up; it's good at eliminating small pneumothoraces and identifying right-to-left shunting, but overheating can cause apnea and dehydration, underheating raises oxygen consumption, and inadequate flow causes CO₂ buildup. An incubator delivers under 40 percent oxygen, so we combine it with a cannula or hood for precise FiO₂, and it's excellent for heat though it has a long stabilization time and limits access. An air entrainment mask is for children 3 years and older, gives precise high-flow FiO₂ from 0.24 to 1.0, and is good for transport, but it has low humidity, can cause pressure necrosis, is hard to keep on an active child, and is not recommended for infants. And a pediatric simple mask stores oxygen between breaths; greater than 10 liters per minute is satisfactory, while less than 5 liters per minute causes CO₂ rebreathing, and the FiO₂ varies with the input flow, mask volume, leak, and breathing pattern.

The air-oxygen blender takes in oxygen and air through a dual pressure regulator and then a precision proportioning valve, where the two gas pressures are equal and the varying inlet sizes give precise control of concentration. When a gas source fails, you'll hear a harmonic sound, and you should always confirm the FiO₂ with an oxygen analyzer.

High-flow nasal cannula is well suited to neonates and children. Studies show it generates a certain amount of CPAP in children, increasing lung recruitment and improving oxygenation and ventilation, and it delivers maximum humidification with minimal rainout in the circuit. Then there's the family of SiPAP, CPAP, and BiPAP devices — bubble CPAP, SiPAP, and the V60 — that provide an exact pressure measurement. They generally start at 4 to 6 centimeters of water and increase by half or one increment to improve oxygenation, and they're used for lung recruitment and to counter PPHN and lung collapse from surfactant deficiency. The indications are a PaO₂ under 50 or an FiO₂ requirement over 60 percent, and the contraindications are respiratory arrest, a pH under 7.25, and a PaCO₂ over 50.

## Employing Basic Therapies

For a neonate or infant with worsening oxygenation, there's a typical escalation. You'll usually start with high-flow nasal cannula, like a Vapotherm or Fisher and Paykel. If that doesn't work, you move to a CPAP or BiPAP machine, usually a SiPAP. And the last step is intubation, commonly using a Draeger ventilator since it's so common in hospitals.

Medication delivery has to account for tiny tidal volumes. For infants and neonates we assist bronchodilator and corticosteroid delivery by bagging the medication in with a spacer and a Jackson-Reese bag. For older pediatric patients, you'll use the familiar facemask and small-volume nebulizer like in adults. And the Aerogen is an ultrasonic nebulizer that delivers very tiny droplets, around 0.3 to 0.5 microns, which is very useful for ventilated pediatrics and neonates and can be placed inline.

The Jackson-Reese bag, also called an anesthesia bag, is a flow-inflating bag with a flow-control valve to regulate pressure. Whatever settings get you proper chest rise and SaO₂ can serve as your initial ventilator settings until you can get an ABG. For intubation equipment, there's even a size 00 blade for the very small pre-term infant, and we secure the tube with tape in an X or Y pattern, with Neobar and Neotech equipment usually available — tape is handy because infants and children come in so many sizes. The commonly used ventilator modes are PC-SIMV with VG and PC-VC; PRVC and VG itself are saved for Phase II. And always finish with an alarm check, making sure you bracket your alarms appropriately.
