## RDS — Hyaline Membrane Disease

Respiratory distress syndrome affects 60,000 to 70,000 infants a year and goes by another name, hyaline membrane disease — it's fundamentally a lung disease of prematurity. The relationship to remember is inverse: as gestational age decreases, the incidence of RDS increases. It's caused by surfactant deficiency below 34 weeks, decreased alveolar surface area, increased small-airway compliance, and the presence of the ductus arteriosus. Low oxygenation and ventilation drive pulmonary vascular resistance up, which causes right-to-left shunting that worsens with hypoxemia, while acidosis reduces surfactant even further. You see tachypnea, retractions, paradoxical breathing, grunting, and flaring, with a chest x-ray showing reticulogranular densities, air bronchograms, and low lung volumes. We treat with CPAP and PEEP, reaching for high-frequency ventilation if the infant deteriorates rapidly.

## Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn

TTN, also called Type II RDS, is the most common respiratory disorder of newborns. The cause is slow clearance of lung fluid after delivery. Normally two-thirds of that fluid gets squeezed out through the birth canal, but a cesarean section skips that squeeze, so fluid stays behind, dropping compliance and raising airway resistance. The baby is tachypneic, and the chest x-ray shows diffuse haziness with perihilar streaking. Treatment is increased FiO₂ by oxyhood or nasal cannula, and CPAP or high-flow nasal cannula is extremely helpful because the added intrapulmonary pressure pushes that extra fluid into the lymphatics and capillaries. The good news: TTN resolves within 24 to 48 hours.

## Apnea of Prematurity

Apnea of prematurity is a common, controllable disorder of premature infants. They can stop breathing for 5 to 10 seconds, then breathe rapidly to compensate. It becomes a problem when apnea lasts beyond 15 seconds, or when cyanosis, hypotonia, pallor, or bradycardia appear. The root cause is immature control of ventilation from the CNS and peripheral chemoreceptors — instead of raising both tidal volume and rate to clear CO₂, only tidal volume rises, so CO₂ creeps up, and repeated episodes cause acidosis. We treat with theophylline, caffeine, CPAP, and simple stimulation in milder cases. It's usually self-limiting and ends somewhere between 37 and 44 weeks.

## Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn

PPHN is also known as persistent fetal circulation. Hypoxemia, metabolic and respiratory acidosis, and overdeveloped pulmonary vasculature constrict pulmonary blood flow. As the pulmonary capillaries constrict, pulmonary vascular resistance climbs above systemic vascular resistance, so blood keeps shunting through the foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus even after birth. The clue at the bedside is that saturation or PaO₂ changes rapidly no matter how you adjust FiO₂. A pre- and post-ductal saturation difference of 5 percent or more — right arm versus left leg — points to a patent ductus arteriosus and suggests PPHN. We treat by removing the underlying cause: correcting acidosis, adjusting FiO₂, giving surfactant. If the infant doesn't improve, we move to intubation and mechanical ventilation or high-frequency ventilation.

## Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia

BPD is a progressive disease, a combination of immaturity, genetics, malnutrition, oxygen toxicity, and mechanical ventilation that destroys lung function. From the respiratory therapist's standpoint, the two factors that matter most are atelectrauma, which is lung collapse, and volutrauma, from large tidal volumes. Hyperoxia adds insult by producing super-oxidants that destroy tissue, and preterm infants don't have the immune development to defend against them, so you get inflammation and injury to the alveolar-capillary membrane and bronchial tree. The longer a baby stays on the ventilator and the more tidal volume and FiO₂ it needs, the more progressive the damage. It looks a lot like adult COPD, with atelectasis and emphysema, and fibrosis from hyaline membrane buildup raises airway resistance. The treatment is really prevention: appropriate tidal volumes and FiO₂, just like preventing adult ARDS, with optimal PEEP and FRC, lung-protective volumes, surfactant, and just enough oxygen to hit the target PaO₂.

## Meconium Aspiration Syndrome

MAS is a disease of term and near-term infants, the inhalation of fetal bowel movements — essentially bile, cholesterol, and GI contents. Normally meconium isn't passed until after delivery, but a distressed fetus may pass it early, and aspiration can even happen in utero. You'll see meconium streaking, fetal tachycardia before birth, gasping retractions after delivery, an APGAR of 5 or less, and a low umbilical pH. Because meconium is so viscous, it causes severe air trapping and airway obstruction, and in large quantities it leads to PPHN, hypoxemia with acidosis, and airway collapse. In a severely depressed infant with meconium streaking, intubate and apply direct suction to the ETT immediately, continuing until no meconium is seen or after two to four aspirations, with suction regulated between negative 70 and negative 100 millimeters of mercury. Then leave a clean ETT in place and start mechanical ventilation or HFV.

## Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia

CDH is a rare defect where abdominal contents push up into the thoracic cavity. There are milder and more severe versions. A chest x-ray reveals the intestines, stomach, and sometimes the liver sitting in the chest, which raises pulmonary resistance, severely damages the one lung the infant is subsisting on, and can compress the heart and drop blood pressure. Treatment is to remove the bowel gas, slowly suction the abdominal contents back to their normal positions, and close the breach.

## Retinopathy of Prematurity

The retinal capillaries start branching at 16 weeks, growing from the optic nerve toward the ora serrata at the front of the retina. In the presence of high FiO₂ and therefore high PaO₂, those capillaries constrict, necrose, and die. The ones that survive proliferate into the eye, then eventually necrose and bleed, forming large hematomas that obstruct vision and can detach the retina. Prevention is about oxygen: keep PaO₂ under 80 in premature infants, and combine limited oxygen with newer laser eye surgery. ROP develops in 25 to 35 percent of premature infants, so maintain saturation between above 88 and below 94 percent to reduce the risk.

## Pulmonary Interstitial Emphysema

PIE comes from mechanical ventilation — chronic high PEEP, high PIP, and prolonged inspiratory times. Air dissects into the interstitial space and compresses small airways and capillaries, creating V/Q mismatch and worsening ABGs. The trap is that our instinct to crank up ventilator pressures and PEEP actually makes PIE worse and traps more gas. The right move is to lower airway pressures while maintaining oxygenation and ventilation, ideally by discontinuing conventional ventilation and switching to high-frequency jet ventilation, which prevents air leaks while still ventilating. And remember, PIE increases the likelihood of developing BPD.

## Gastroschisis and Necrotizing Enterocolitis

Gastroschisis is a congenital birth defect of the abdominal wall, where the intestines sit outside the body, exiting through a hole beside the umbilicus — and it creates multiple complications for respiratory therapists. Necrotizing enterocolitis, or NEC, is a common intestinal infection in neonates. Premature infants have low blood flow to their intestines and underdeveloped intestinal tracts; that, combined with early introduction of gut bacteria, infects the intestinal wall and kills the tissue, which can lead to sepsis and death.

## SIDS, GERD, and Bronchiolitis

Sudden infant death syndrome is the leading cause of death for infants under age 1, usually within the first one to four months of life, claiming about 7,000 infants a year in the United States. A previously healthy infant dies unexpectedly, most often during sleep, and autopsies show evidence of repeated episodes of hypoxemia or ischemia, but otherwise no known cause. Risk factors include winter, a prior apparent life-threatening event where the infant was cyanotic, apneic, or limp, and the prone sleeping position. Prevention is everything: keep the child supine or side-lying and clear excess blankets and toys to reduce accidental suffocation — though intentional suffocation and SIDS are very hard to tell apart.

GERD is the regurgitation of stomach contents, and it can trigger reactive airway disease, pneumonia, and other upper-airway problems.

Bronchiolitis is an acute lower respiratory tract infection, usually from respiratory syncytial virus. Nearly one in ten infants under two gets it, and fewer than 1 percent of those hospitalized die from respiratory failure. The infants most at risk are those with BPD or who are immunologically suppressed — basically our NICU graduates. It causes inflammation and obstruction of the small bronchi and bronchioles, usually right after a viral upper respiratory infection, starting as a small cough that progresses to dyspnea, tachypnea, and expiratory wheezes with rising airway resistance, and a chest x-ray showing hyperinflation with areas of consolidation. Treatment is often prophylaxis — passively immunizing high-risk infants and children against RSV.

## Croup and Epiglottitis

Croup, or laryngotracheobronchitis, is subglottic swelling and obstruction, seen as a steeple sign or picket-fence sign on an A-P neck x-ray — a narrowing of the trachea at the level of the cricoid cartilage. It's usually caused by the parainfluenza virus and is the most common airway obstruction in children 6 months to 6 years old, sometimes worsened by bacteria like staph aureus, group A strep pyogenes, and H. influenzae. It starts as nasal congestion, fever, and cough, then progresses to stridor and a barking seal cough, sometimes with dyspnea, cyanosis, and exhaustion. Treatment is reserved for signs of severe respiratory distress with cyanosis — cool mist for mild to moderate cases, corticosteroids and epinephrine for severe cases, and heliox to overcome the obstruction.

Epiglottitis is the dangerous cousin: an acute, often life-threatening supraglottic swelling that severely obstructs the upper airway, most commonly caused by Haemophilus influenzae. Symptoms mirror croup — labored breathing, fever, sore throat, difficulty swallowing, and stridor — but on a lateral neck x-ray you see a thumbs-up, or thumbprint, sign from the swollen epiglottis, versus the steeple sign of croup. The danger is that any further inflammation can cause complete obstruction, so only staff familiar with emergency intubation should manage the airway. We treat with antibiotics, corticosteroids, racemic epinephrine, and heliox to bypass the airway resistance. The clean contrast to keep straight: croup is subglottic with a steeple sign from a virus, epiglottitis is supraglottic with a thumb sign from H. influenzae.

## Cystic Fibrosis

Cystic fibrosis is one of the most common genetic diseases among whites in the United States, an autosomal recessive trait affecting about 30,000 people. It's caused by a mutation in the gene coding for CFTR — the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator — which controls the movement of chloride ions across the cell membrane, and that movement is vital to secreting mucus and transporting fluid throughout the body. With around a thousand CFTR variants, the severity varies widely. Because CFTR governs chloride distribution, CF affects every exocrine function — sweat glands, pancreas, and lungs. Patients can't reabsorb salt before it leaves the skin, so their sweat tastes salty, which is the basis of the sweat chloride test, and they're prone to dehydration in hot weather. Exocrine dysfunction causes pancreatic insufficiency with a sharp drop in digestive enzymes. Lung disease is the primary cause of death: mucus buildup and recurrent infections lead to recurrent bronchiolitis and bronchiectasis, atelectasis, pneumonia, and lung abscesses, and as it progresses it can cause cor pulmonale with marked hypoxemia. Diagnosis is suspected from clinical signs or close family history, confirmed by recurrent early pulmonary infections plus the sweat chloride test, also called iontophoresis. Treatment is complicated because CF is progressive — pancreatic enzymes to support digestion, plus antibiotics, anti-inflammatories, bronchodilators, and lung-clearance therapies like chest physiotherapy to stretch out the time between hospital treatments.
