## Topic 1: Cardiopulmonary Stress Testing Definition

Cardiopulmonary stress testing may be called CPX, CPEX, or CPET.

It is a test designed to assess or measure ventilation, gas exchange, and cardiovascular function during exercise.

## Topic 2: CPX Indications

CPX may be indicated for evaluation of exercise intolerance, unexplained dyspnea, evaluation of patients with cardiovascular disease, evaluation of patients with respiratory disease, and specific clinical applications.

## Topic 3: CPX Contraindications

Contraindications include limiting neurologic or neuromuscular disorders, limiting orthopedic disorders, PaO2 less than 40 mmHg on room air, and PaCO2 greater than 70 mmHg.

## Topic 4: CPX Equipment

Exercise equipment may be a treadmill or cycle ergometer. There is no significant clinical difference; the choice is dictated by the patient's condition.

Other equipment includes PFT machine, gas analyzers, Douglas bag collection, mixing chamber, or breath-to-breath analysis for exhaled gas collection, 12-lead EKG, blood pressure cuff, pulse oximetry, and optional arterial line.

Gas analyzers measure exhaled O2, CO2, and N2.

## Topic 5: CPX Pre-Procedure

Before CPX testing, PFTs should include FVC and MVV, and DLCO should be performed if indicated.

ABG is used if hypoxemia is suspected. Chest X-ray, 12-lead EKG, and cardiology consult for patients with a history of CAD may be included.

The patient should wear comfortable clothing and tennis shoes. The patient should not smoke cigarettes for 8 hours before testing and should not exercise the day of the test. Medications continue as ordered. Signed consent is very important.

## Topic 6: CPX Procedure

Exercise protocols include maximal incremental cycle, maximal incremental treadmill, and constant work rate.

Maximal incremental cycle includes 3 minutes rest, 3 minutes unloaded cycling, and increasing watts every minute until exhaustion or termination.

Maximal incremental treadmill is similar to cardiac stress testing. Examples include Bruce, Naughton, and Balke.

## Topic 7: CPX Termination Criteria

Indications for termination include chest pain, signs of respiratory failure such as cyanosis, lightheadedness, or mental confusion, abnormal ECG reading, muscle cramping, and hypertension greater than 250 mmHg systolic and greater than 120 mmHg diastolic.

## Topic 8: CPX Values

Measurements obtained include ventilation, tidal volume, minute ventilation, frequency, oxygenation by pulse oximeter, ABG if detailed analysis of pulmonary limitations to exercise is needed, VO2, VCO2, ECG, and blood pressure.

VO2 is oxygen consumption, or the volume of O2 used by tissues each minute.

## Topic 9: CPX Interpretation

The practitioner first assesses the degree of effort and determines whether the test is maximal or sub-maximal.

A maximal study is identified when heart rate is greater than 85-90% predicted or SaO2 is less than 80%. Once a single criterion is met, the test is graded as maximal effort.

A sub-maximal study is used when the patient is physically unable to reach maximal effort. A criterion that the patient can meet despite impairments is set.

## Topic 10: MVV Definition

Maximum voluntary ventilation evaluates the maximum volume a patient can breathe over a specified period of time, usually 12 seconds.

MVV is measured in L/min BTPS using the same equipment as spirometry.

## Topic 11: MVV Indications

MVV is useful in conditions where ventilatory capacity may be impaired by mechanisms different from those affecting FEV1.

It is required for evaluation of some occupationally related disorders and may be used to set a ventilatory limit during exercise.

## Topic 12: MVV Contraindications and Hazards

There are no contraindications specific to the MVV maneuver. The same contraindications to spirometry apply.

Hazards include syncope and dizziness, which are common due to hyperventilation, paroxysmal coughing, nosocomial infections, bronchospasm, and desaturation due to interruption of O2.

## Topic 13: MVV Procedure

Have the patient breathe as deeply and rapidly as possible for 12-15 seconds.

The patient should be enthusiastically coached throughout the test. Volume should be greater than tidal volume but less than vital capacity. Rate should be between 90 and 110 breaths/min. Reproducible results should be within 20%.

## Topic 14: MVV Values and Interpretation

MVV values vary as much as 30% from the mean, so only large reductions are significant.

MVV is a test of the entire respiratory system. It is influenced by respiratory muscles, compliance of the lung-thorax system, resistance offered by airways and tissues, and condition of the ventilatory control mechanism.

MVV is decreased with moderate to severe obstructive disease and may be normal with restrictive disease.

## Topic 15: MIP and MEP Definition

MIP and MEP are tests of respiratory muscle strength. They may also be called negative inspiratory force, NIF, PImax, or PEmax.

MIP reflects strength of the diaphragm and other inspiratory muscles. MEP reflects strength of abdominal and other expiratory muscles.

## Topic 16: MIP and MEP Indications

Indications include suspected respiratory muscle weakness, such as a patient with dyspnea, weak cough, and known neuromuscular disease.

MIP and MEP may also be used when vital capacity is low for an unknown reason and respiratory muscle weakness is in the differential diagnosis.

They can help determine whether known respiratory muscle weakness has improved, remained stable, or worsened.

## Topic 17: MIP and MEP Equipment

Contraindications are none.

Equipment includes a manual pressure gauge, pulmonary function machine if capable of measuring MIP and MEP, mouthpiece, and nose clips.

## Topic 18: MIP Procedure

For MIP, instruct the patient to seal lips firmly around the mouthpiece, exhale slowly and completely, and then pull in hard.

Inspiratory effort should be maintained for 1-3 seconds. Record the largest negative pressure sustained for at least 1 second, not a transient spike.

Allow a brief rest and repeat the maneuver three times. Report the maximum value of three maneuvers that varied by less than 20%.

## Topic 19: MEP Procedure

For MEP, instruct the patient to inhale completely and then push or blow as hard as possible.

Expiratory pressure should be maintained for 1-3 seconds. Record the largest positive pressure sustained for at least 1 second, not a transient spike.

Allow a brief rest and repeat the maneuver three times. Report the maximum value of three maneuvers that varied by less than 20%.

## Topic 20: MIP and MEP Values

Male MIP should be greater than -75 cmH2O. Female MIP should be greater than -50 cmH2O.

Male MEP should be greater than 100 cmH2O. Female MEP should be greater than 80 cmH2O.

MIP and MEP are rarely used alone. They are considered with other measures, such as VC and FVC.

## Topic 21: MIP and MEP Interpretation

Reduced MIP and/or MEP suggests respiratory muscle weakness, which can have many causes.

Neuromuscular diseases include ALS, myasthenia gravis, polymyositis, and Guillain-Barre syndrome.

Decreased MIP with normal MEP suggests inspiratory muscle weakness, usually diaphragmatic. Normal MIP with decreased MEP suggests expiratory muscle weakness, which is rare.

Systemic conditions can affect skeletal muscle strength. Examples include heart failure, malnutrition, and idiopathic myopathy.

COPD can reduce MIP and MEP unrelated to respiratory muscle weakness because of lung hyperinflation.
