# Lung Volume Studies

Objective: perform a lung volume study.

Lung volume studies are pulmonary function tests that measure functional residual capacity, or FRC. FRC is the volume of gas remaining in the lungs at the end tidal expiratory level. FRC cannot be measured directly because the gas in this compartment cannot be completely exhaled from the lungs.

The three ways to measure FRC are nitrogen washout, helium dilution, and thoracic gas volume by body plethysmography. Thoracic gas volume is also called VTG, body plethysmography, or BodyBox. It uses Boyle's law and is used to compute the patient's total lung capacity.

Indications include diagnosing restrictive lung disease patterns, differentiating obstructive and restrictive patterns when vital capacity is reduced, assessing response to therapeutic intervention, and pre-operative assessment.

Contraindications are the same as spirometry: hemoptysis of unknown origin, pneumothorax, unstable cardiovascular status, recent MI or PE, thoracic, abdominal, or central aneurysms, and inability to access the body box.

Hazards include hypoxemia from interruption of oxygen therapy, depression of ventilatory drive in CO2 retainers from breathing 100% oxygen during nitrogen washout, hypercapnia during helium dilution if CO2 is not removed, and hypoxemia during helium dilution if oxygen is not added.

Equipment includes a spirometer, helium analyzer, nitrogen analyzer, and plethysmograph.

In nitrogen washout, nitrogen in the lungs is presumed to be equal with the atmosphere, about 75-80%. The patient breathes 100% oxygen, which washes nitrogen out of the lungs. A nitrogen analyzer and spirometer provide breath-to-breath analysis of expired nitrogen. The test lasts up to seven minutes or until the exhaled nitrogen concentration is less than 1%.

Corrections are made for nitrogen washed out of blood and tissue and for small amounts of nitrogen in pure oxygen. For each minute breathing oxygen, approximately 30-40 mL of nitrogen is removed from blood and tissue. The formula is 0.04 x time = nitrogen tissue correction. This result is subtracted from the volume of exhaled nitrogen.

In helium dilution, helium is metabolically inert and can be distributed throughout the lungs without significant absorption. The spirometer is filled with a known volume, usually 600-800 mL, and helium is added to achieve a helium concentration of 10%. The patient rebreathes the gas in the spirometer until the helium concentration falls to a stable level. This takes about 3-4 minutes in healthy patients. The system must have a CO2 absorber, such as soda lime, and oxygen must be added to maintain the patient's oxygen demand. A 100 mL correction is subtracted from FRC for helium that dissolves in the blood. If this is not subtracted, FRC will be overestimated.

Thoracic gas volume is the quickest and most accurate method for determining FRC. VTG is the volume of gas contained in the thorax, whether it communicates with open airways or is trapped in a compartment of the thorax. VTG measurement begins at end tidal expiration, which is FRC. The patient sits in the box with the door shut for at least 30 seconds so temperature can equalize. The patient breathes normally on the mouthpiece, and at least three tidal breaths that vary by less than 100 mL are recorded before the SVC maneuver. The patient then performs an SVC maneuver, and SVC should be greater than or equal to FVC.

When the patient returns to baseline, prepare them for panting. The patient places their hands on their cheeks. Panting frequency is usually 30-60 breaths per minute, depending on the manufacturer. The airway is occluded and the patient pants against a closed shutter. Pressure is measured at the airway. When there is no airflow, mouth pressure equals alveolar pressure. As the patient inspires against the closed shutter, volume in the thorax increases and pressure in the box decreases. Proximal airway pressure and box pressure are measured simultaneously. Boyle's law, P1V1 = P2V2, is used to compute VTG. VTG measurements are often larger than FRC values from gas dilution methods, especially with emphysema, air trapping, and uneven distribution of ventilation.

Tidal volume is the volume of air moved in and out of the lungs during normal breathing. It is measured while the patient breathes quietly until three consecutive breaths vary by less than 100 mL.

Inspiratory reserve volume is the largest volume of gas that can be inspired from the resting end-tidal inspiration level.

Expiratory reserve volume is the largest volume of gas that can be expired from the resting end-expiratory level. ERV normally comprises 25% of vital capacity. Changes in ERV parallel increases or decreases in vital capacity. Reduced ERV is consistent with restrictive defect. Erroneous ERV estimates cause miscalculation of RV because ERV is subtracted from FRC to calculate RV.

Residual volume is the volume of gas remaining in the lungs at end maximum expiration. RV = FRC - ERV. Increased RV indicates that despite maximal expiratory effort, the lungs still contain large amounts of gas. Increased RV is characteristic of emphysema and bronchial obstruction and may cause chronic air trapping. RV and FRC usually increase together. RV is usually decreased in restrictive disorders.

Inspiratory capacity is the largest volume of gas that can be inspired from the resting expiratory level. IC is a subdivision of vital capacity and comprises approximately 75% of VC. Reduced IC is consistent with restrictive defects.

Vital capacity, or SVC, is the volume of gas measured on a slow, complete expiration after maximal inspiration, without forced or rapid effort. It may also be measured from maximum expiration to maximal inspiration. Decreased VC can be caused by loss of distensible lung tissue.

Total lung capacity is the volume of gas in the lungs at end maximal inspiration. TLC = FRC + IC. TLC = VC + RV. TLC may be normal or increased in obstructive disease. If TLC is less than LLN, a restrictive process should be suspected.

Restrictive abnormality is characterized by decreased TLC. TLC less than LLN but greater than 70% predicted is mild restriction. TLC less than 70% but greater than 60% predicted is moderate restriction. TLC less than 59% predicted is moderately severe restriction.

Obstructive abnormality: FRC greater than ULN with TLC within normal limits represents air trapping. FRC and TLC greater than ULN predicted represents hyperinflation.

Combined abnormality requires obstruction confirmed by spirometry, FEV1 less than LLN, and TLC less than LLN by lung volume testing.

With body plethysmography, airway resistance and conductance values can also be obtained: Raw, Gaw, sRaw, and sGaw.

Airway resistance, or Raw, is opposition to flow caused by friction. It is measured as the ratio of driving pressure to rate of airflow: (Pmouth - PA) / flow. Raw is a measure of the difficulty with which air flows through the lungs. It is measured in cmH2O/L/sec. Normal Raw is 0.6-2.4 cmH2O/L/sec.

Airway conductance, or Gaw, is flow generated per unit of pressure drop in the airway. It is the reciprocal of Raw and measures the ease of airflow. It is measured in L/sec/cmH2O. Normal Gaw is 0.42-1.67 L/sec/cmH2O.

Airway diameter changes with lung volume. Airway caliber increases as the lungs expand. Specific resistance and specific conductance normalize this effect by evaluating resistance and conductance at the lung volume where they were measured, usually VTG at FRC. sGaw = Gaw divided by VTG. sRaw = Raw multiplied by VTG. There are no reliable reference sets for sRaw. Normal sGaw is greater than 0.15 L/sec/cmH2O.

Obstruction is suggested by Raw greater than 2.4 cmH2O/L/sec and sGaw less than 0.15 L/sec/cmH2O. Restrictive disease shows normal airway resistance and conductance values.

In the normal airway, resistance is distributed as 50% nose, mouth, and upper airway; 30% trachea and bronchi; and 20% small airways. All obstructive diseases can increase Raw and decrease Gaw. Large airway obstruction from tumor, trauma, or foreign body can cause a significant increase. Acute asthma attack can increase Raw up to three times normal. Emphysema increases Raw due to airway narrowing and collapse, especially in the bronchioles. Bronchitis may cause an increase relative to disease severity.
