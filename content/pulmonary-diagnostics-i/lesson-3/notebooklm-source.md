# Lung Diffusion and Bronchoprovocation Studies

Objective: perform a lung diffusion capacity test and perform bronchoprovocation testing.

DLCO is a test that measures the gas exchange capacity of the lungs. Carbon monoxide is used as a surrogate for oxygen. DLCO can be measured by single breath, steady state, and rebreathing techniques.

DLCO indications include evaluating or following parenchymal lung disease such as asbestosis, silicosis, and allergic alveolitis. DLCO can evaluate, differentiate, or follow obstructive lung disease such as emphysema, cystic fibrosis, asthma, and chronic bronchitis. It can evaluate pulmonary involvement in systemic diseases such as sarcoidosis, rheumatoid arthritis, and lupus. It can also monitor patients at risk of drug toxicity that can affect the alveolar-capillary membrane, including amiodarone and bleomycin.

DLCO contraindications include carbon monoxide toxicity, which is absolute; patient inability to perform the maneuver; smoking the day of the test; and desaturation without supplemental oxygen. Hazards include hazards common to all pulmonary function testing and interruption of supplemental oxygen causing desaturation.

Special analyzers are required for DLCO. The infrared absorption analyzer measures CO based on absorption of infrared radiation. The thermal conductivity analyzer measures helium or methane by detecting the rate at which gases conduct heat. The gas chromatograph separates a sample gas into component gases.

The DLCO test gas contains 10% helium or methane to measure alveolar volume, 0.3% CO to measure diffusion rate, 21% oxygen, and balance nitrogen. Water vapor must be removed from gas samples before CO analysis, such as with a desiccator column.

Before DLCO testing, the patient should not smoke the day of the test to reduce CO levels in the blood. The patient should be off supplemental oxygen for 10 minutes before testing. The technologist should wait 20 minutes after shunt studies or nitrogen washout.

The modified Krogh technique is the single-breath DLCO technique. It is the most commonly used method because it is simple, non-invasive, and useful for screening and clinical application. Disadvantages are sensitivity to distribution of ventilation and V/Q mismatch, the nonphysiological breath hold, and poor practicality for exercise.

DLCO is performed sitting. The patient exhales to residual volume, inspires quickly to total lung capacity, holds the breath for 8-12 seconds, and then exhales normally. The first 0.75-1 L of exhaled gas is washed out into the room to remove anatomic dead space. The next 0.5-1 L is analyzed for CO, which measures DL, and helium or methane, which measures VA. Washout volume may need to be reduced if vital capacity is less than 2 L. Reducing washout may add dead space gas to the analyzed sample. If washout is analyzed as part of the alveolar sample, DL will be underestimated. Correct for hemoglobin and carboxyhemoglobin if available from ABG. Wait at least 4 minutes between tests so test gas can wash out of the lungs.

ATS acceptability criteria for single-breath DLCO: inspire to TLC within 4 seconds; inspiratory vital capacity greater than 85% of VC; breath hold 8-12 seconds; smooth exhalation within 4 seconds; sample volume measured on plateau; and VA less than TLC as measured by gas technique or body box. Tests outside these limits should be discarded. At least two tests are performed, and final results can be averaged. Efforts must meet acceptability criteria. DL values must be within 3 mL of each other or 10% of the highest value.

Average DLCO-SB is 25 mL CO/min/mmHg STPD. DLO2 can be estimated by DLCO x 1.23, but DLCO is normally reported clinically. DLCO is decreased in sarcoidosis, asbestosis, berylliosis, oxygen toxicity, pulmonary edema, emphysema, space occupying lesions, lung resections, and V/Q mismatch.

DLCO severity: LLN to ULN is normal. 60% to LLN is mild. 40-60% is moderate. Less than 40% is severe.

DL is diffusion rate. VA is alveolar volume. Ten percent helium or methane measures VA. 0.3% CO measures DL. DL/VA differentiates disease processes that decrease DLCO. Healthy DL/VA is 4-5 mL CO/min/L lung volume.

In obstruction, decreased DLCO with normal VA creates a low ratio. DL is less than LLN, VA is normal, and DL/VA is less than LLN. This is caused by uneven V/Q or uneven distribution of inspired gas. In restriction, decreased DLCO parallels loss of lung volume, so DL is less than LLN, VA is less than LLN, and DL/VA is normal. This is a direct result of loss of lung tissue.

Bronchoprovocation is a test where bronchospasm is induced. It is used to determine whether airway hyper-reactivity, a component of asthma, is present. Two bronchoprovocation studies are methacholine challenge and exercise-induced asthma or exercise-induced bronchospasm testing.

Methacholine increases parasympathetic tone in bronchial smooth muscle and causes bronchoconstriction. In individuals without asthma, the dose used will not cause a significant response, so the test is highly specific for airway hyper-reactivity.

Methacholine challenge is indicated for patients with history or symptoms suggestive of asthma but no evidence of airflow obstruction on PFTs or uncertain bronchodilator study results. Symptoms include wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, and persistent cough.

Relative contraindications to methacholine are inability to perform acceptable spirometry and pregnancy or nursing mothers. Absolute contraindications are severe airflow obstruction with FEV1 less than 60% predicted, heart attack or stroke within the last 3 months, uncontrolled hypertension with systolic greater than 200 or diastolic greater than 100, and known aortic aneurysm.

Hazards include shortness of breath, cough, chest tightness, wheezing, and headache. Because methacholine testing has risk, a physician should be immediately available.

Equipment includes methacholine or histamine, usually mixed in batches by pharmacy and kept refrigerated until 30 minutes before the test; a hand-held nebulizer with filtered exhalation port; and a fast-acting bronchodilator to reverse bronchospasm. Nebulizers may be wall powered or powered by a dosimeter.

Before methacholine challenge, withhold short-acting bronchodilators including metaproterenol, albuterol, and levalbuterol for 8 hours. Withhold ipratropium for 24 hours. Withhold salmeterol and formoterol for 48 hours. Withhold tiotropium for 1 week. Withhold liquid theophylline for 12 hours, intermediate-acting theophylline for 24 hours, and long-acting theophylline for 48 hours. Withhold cromolyn sodium for 8 hours, nedocromil for 48 hours, montelukast for 24 hours, and cetirizine for 72 hours. Patients on corticosteroids should be challenged while taking a stable dosage. The patient should have no coffee, tea, cola drinks, chocolate, or smoking the day of the study.

The patient should be asymptomatic at the time of testing. Baseline spirometry is performed, and FEV1 must be at least 60-70% predicted. A nebulized diluent step with NaCl is optional and helps the patient become familiar with the nebulizer and spirometry; highly reactive airways may respond to diluent alone. Breaths are slow and deep to TLC with a 5-second breath hold, or tidal volume breathing for 2 minutes. Perform three acceptable FVC maneuvers 30 seconds after the last nebulizer inhalation. If post-diluent FEV1 drops 10% or more from baseline, the test is positive. If not, the methacholine challenge begins.

Methacholine dosing begins with the lowest concentration and continues higher if needed. Dose range is 0.0625-16 mg/mL. Five-breath dosimeter dosing quadruples. Two-minute tidal breathing dosing doubles. The patient takes five breaths of solution with slow deep breaths to TLC and 5-second breath hold, or breathes at tidal volume for 2 minutes. Perform three acceptable FVC maneuvers 30 seconds after the last inhalation and complete FVC maneuvers within 3 minutes.

Percent decrease equals ((x - y) / x) x 100, where x is baseline and y is current. If FEV1 does not decrease by 20%, give the next higher dose. Sustained decrease of 20% in FEV1 in all three efforts is a positive test, and methacholine testing is terminated. Bronchospasm should be reversed with bronchodilator, and spirometry documents bronchodilator effectiveness.

Exercise-induced asthma or exercise-induced bronchospasm is bronchospasm occurring during or immediately after vigorous exercise. It is caused by water loss and heat exchange. During exercise, ventilation can exceed 200 L/min and breathing occurs through the mouth. The nose, pharynx, and first seven generations of bronchi condition air. During exercise, the upper airways contribute water vapor from the respiratory epithelial surface liquid. Exercise also cools the airways. After exercise stops, bronchial vasculature dilates and engorges to rewarm the epithelium. Rebound hyperemia can narrow the airway. Engorged vessels may leak, leading to mediator release and bronchospasm. This may explain EIA/EIB after exercise has stopped.

EIA/EIB indications include shortness of breath on exertion with normal resting PFTs, symptomatic patients with negative or uncertain methacholine results, known EIA/EIB where therapy is being evaluated, and evaluating exercise limitation in asthmatics.

Contraindications and hazards are the same as methacholine. Equipment includes EKG monitor, treadmill or bicycle ergometer, pulse oximeter, spirometer, defibrillator, and code cart.

Before EIA/EIB, withhold the same medications as methacholine challenge. Avoid heavy exercise at least 4 hours before the test. The patient should wear comfortable clothing and running shoes and should be asymptomatic. Baseline FVC is performed. FEV1 must be at least 70% predicted. The patient is connected to EKG and pulse oximeter. Maximum heart rate is 220 minus age. The patient wears nose clips to ensure mouth breathing.

Exercise should be vigorous enough to reach 80-90% predicted maximum heart rate for 6-8 minutes. Exercise continues until target heart rate is reached for the set time or the patient becomes symptomatic. Post-exercise FVC is performed within 1-2 minutes, then at 5-minute intervals for 20-30 minutes.

Percent decrease equals ((x - y) / x) x 100, where x is baseline and y is current. Sustained decrease of 10-15% in FEV1 in all three efforts is a positive EIA/EIB test, and the test is terminated. Bronchospasm should be reversed with bronchodilator, and spirometry documents bronchodilator effectiveness.
