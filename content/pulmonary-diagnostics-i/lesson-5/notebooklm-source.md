## Topic 1: FeNO Definition

Exhaled gas contains trace amounts of nitric oxide. As airway inflammation worsens, fractional exhaled nitric oxide, or FeNO, increases.

FeNO is a marker of airway inflammation and is measured in parts per billion.

## Topic 2: FeNO Indications

FeNO can diagnose airway inflammation, establish a baseline while stable for known asthmatics, determine likely response to corticosteroid treatment, guide changes in corticosteroid dose, monitor, and monitor adherence to anti-inflammatory medications including corticosteroids.

## Topic 3: FeNO Relative Contraindications and Pre-Test Factors

When possible, patients should refrain from eating and drinking for 1 hour before FeNO.

Foods high in nitrates, such as lettuce and broccoli, can increase FeNO, with maximum effect 2 hours after ingestion.

Caffeine may transiently alter FeNO. Patients should refrain from smoking for 1 hour before measurement.

Upper and lower respiratory tract viral infections may increase exhaled NO in asthma. FeNO should be deferred until recovery if possible, or the infection should be recorded in the chart.

No hazards are currently known.

## Topic 4: FeNO Equipment and Procedure

Equipment includes an exhaled nitric oxide device, mouthpiece or filter, and nose clip.

FeNO measurement should always be performed before spirometry or other PFTs.

The source states ATS has no standards for FeNO acceptability or reproducibility, so follow manufacturer recommendations for the procedure.

## Topic 5: FeNO Values

Adult normal is 5-25 ppb. Less than 25 ppb indicates eosinophilic inflammation is unlikely. Greater than 50 ppb indicates eosinophilic inflammation is likely and, in symptomatic patients, responsiveness to corticosteroids is likely.

Children normal is 5-20 ppb. Less than 20 ppb indicates eosinophilic inflammation is unlikely. Greater than 35 ppb indicates eosinophilic inflammation is likely and, in symptomatic children, responsiveness to corticosteroids is likely.

## Topic 6: Timed Walk Definition

The timed walk test determines the physical capability of patients with pulmonary disease.

It measures the distance a patient can walk on a flat, hard surface in 6 minutes and reflects ability to perform daily physical activities.

## Topic 7: Timed Walk Indications

Timed walk can be used as a single measurement of functional status in COPD, cystic fibrosis, CHF, pulmonary vascular disease, and elderly patients.

## Topic 8: Timed Walk Equipment

Equipment includes at least 30 meters or 100 feet of clear hallway, a marked turnaround point, course markings every 10 feet, stopwatch, Modified Borg RPE scale, portable oxygen with nasal cannula, and optional handheld pulse oximeter.

## Topic 9: Timed Walk Pre-Procedure

The patient should wear comfortable walking clothes and shoes. Walking aids are allowed.

The patient takes medications as prescribed, including oxygen. A light meal is allowed before early morning or early afternoon tests. The patient should not exercise vigorously within 2 hours before testing.

Have the patient sit in a chair for 10 minutes before walking and obtain baseline BP, SpO2, and HR. Have the patient stand and report RPE using the Borg scale.

## Topic 10: Timed Walk Procedure

The objective is to walk as far as possible for 6 minutes.

The patient follows the predetermined course and walks at their own pace. The patient may slow down or take breaks as needed, but time continues.

If the patient rests, document duration and ask for RPE. Encourage the patient and give time hacks. When time expires, tell the patient to stop. Calculate distance by walking to the patient. Ask the patient to report RPE using the Borg scale.

## Topic 11: Supplemental Oxygen During Timed Walk

If the patient is on supplemental O2, the patient must carry their own gas source and be on ordered flow settings.

Technicians should not walk with the patient. Even walking behind the patient can affect pace.

For very deconditioned patients, have a chair available along the course or follow with a wheelchair.

## Topic 12: Timed Walk Values and Interpretation

There are no valid reference sets, so there are no normal values for interpreting a single 6-minute walk.

Small studies show healthy men average around 1800 feet and women around 1500 feet.

Most 6-minute walks are performed pre and post intervention, and the difference is reported as an absolute value.

A change distance of more than 150 feet is clinically significant in most disease states. Distances less than 1000 feet are associated with increased mortality in COPD, CHF, and pulmonary arterial hypertension.

## Topic 13: Oxygen Titration with Exercise Definition

Oxygen titration with exercise determines the degree of oxygen desaturation and/or hypoxemia that occurs on exertion.

## Topic 14: Oxygen Titration Indications

Indications include assessing and quantifying arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation during exercise in patients suspected of desaturation, dyspnea on exertion, decreased DLCO, decreased PaO2 at rest, known pulmonary disease, titrating supplemental O2 to treat hypoxemia or desaturation during activity, pre-operative assessment for lung resection or transplant, and disability evaluation such as pneumoconiosis or asbestosis.

## Topic 15: Oxygen Titration Contraindications

Relative contraindications include invalid pulse oximetry from COHb or decreased perfusion, severe pulmonary hypertension, resting diastolic BP greater than 110 torr, resting systolic BP greater than 200 torr, SaO2 or SpO2 less than 85% on room air, and inability to cooperate or follow directions.

Absolute contraindications include acute ischemia or serious cardiac dysrhythmias, unstable angina, recent MI within 4 weeks, uncontrolled hypertension, uncontrolled or untreated asthma, and pulmonary edema.

## Topic 16: Oxygen Titration Hazards

Hazards include severe desaturation indicated by SaO2 less than or equal to 80% or SpO2 less than or equal to 83%, a 10% fall from baseline values, angina, hypotensive responses, decrease of more than 20 torr in systolic pressure after the normal exercise rise, decrease in systolic blood pressure below the pre-exercise level, lightheadedness, and patient request to terminate the test.

Abnormal responses may require discontinuation of exercise if systolic BP rises to greater than 250 torr, diastolic BP rises to greater than 120 torr, systolic pressure rises less than 20 torr from resting level, or the patient develops mental confusion, headache, cyanosis, nausea, vomiting, or muscle cramping.

## Topic 17: Oxygen Titration Equipment

Equipment may include treadmill, cycle ergometer, arm crank ergometer, stair climbing, step test, timed walking, pulse oximeter with ear, forehead, or finger probe, portable oxygen with nasal cannula, ABG sampling equipment, EKG monitor, code cart, and Borg scale.

## Topic 18: Oxygen Titration Procedure

Obtain baseline blood pressure, oxygen saturation, heart rate, and RPE.

Place the pulse oximeter on continuous monitoring and use paper strip recording for HR and SpO2 throughout the test.

When testing on room air, if SpO2 drops below 85%, stop the patient and have the patient recover before continuing. The doctor may want the test repeated on oxygen to determine the O2 level needed.

When testing on oxygen, if SpO2 drops below 88%, increase oxygen 1-2 L at a time without causing the patient to slow down. Maintain oxygen saturation greater than or equal to 89%.

During the test, use encouragement and tell the patient when 2, 4, and 6 minutes have elapsed.

Accurate evaluation of desaturation on exertion requires arterial blood samples drawn with the subject at rest and peak exercise.

SpO2 of 93% should be used as a target. Continuous pulse oximetry can provide qualitative information and an approximation of oxyhemoglobin saturation. A 4% decrease in SpO2 is significant.
