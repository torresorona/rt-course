## Topic 1: Pulmonary Diagnostic Testing Overview

Pulmonary diagnostic testing includes pulmonary function tests, spirometry, lung volumes, DLCO, bronchoprovocation, CPX, bronchoscopies, and sleep studies.

This lesson focuses on spirometry and post-bronchodilator studies.

## Topic 2: Spirometry Definition

Spirometry is the measurement of air entering and leaving the lungs. It includes measurements of several values of forced airflow and volume during inspiration and expiration.

Objectives include:

- Perform spirometry
- Evaluate flow-volume loops
- Follow user maintenance and quality-control procedures

## Topic 3: Pattern Memory Aids

Volume problems are remembered with PAINT:

- Pulmonary
- Alveolar
- Interstitial
- Neuromuscular
- Thoracic

Flow problems are remembered with CBABE:

- Cystic fibrosis
- Bronchitis
- Asthma
- Bronchiectasis
- Emphysema

Reduced volume suggests a possible restrictive pattern. Reduced flow suggests a possible obstructive pattern.

## Topic 4: Indications

Spirometry is indicated to establish baseline pulmonary function, detect disease, follow the course of a disease or monitor treatment, evaluate impairment, test dyspnea to see if symptoms are cardiac or pulmonary, perform pre-operative evaluation, and identify the high-risk smoker.

## Topic 5: Contraindications and Hazards

Contraindications include hemoptysis of unknown origin, pneumothorax, unstable cardiovascular status including recent MI or PE, thoracic/abdominal/cerebral aneurysms, and recent eye/chest/abdominal surgery.

Hazards include syncope or dizziness, paroxysmal coughing, nosocomial infections, bronchospasm, and desaturation due to interruption of oxygen.

## Topic 6: Equipment

Spirometers include volume-sensing spirometers and flow-sensing spirometers. Flow-sensing spirometers include pneumotachometers.

Volume-sensing spirometers have the primary advantage of better long-term accuracy if calibration is not done regularly.

Additional equipment includes filter and mouthpiece assembly, nose clips, and appropriate gas sources.

## Topic 7: Predicted Reference Values and LLN

Spirometry values are compared with predicted values based on race, age, sex, and height. Predicted values are expected reference values for lung function tests, usually derived from a large healthy population.

Predicted percent equals actual value divided by predicted value times 100.

LLN means lower limit of normal. PFT values are normal if they are greater than or equal to LLN. Values below LLN are low. Values above ULN are high.

The 5th percentile is the value below which 5% of normal reference results may be found. Below LLN means there is only a 5% chance that the result is normal and a 95% chance that it is abnormal.

## Topic 8: Pre-Procedure Assessment

Withhold bronchodilators before testing when ordered by protocol.

Collect age, height or arm span, sex, race, smoking history, cough and sputum description, dyspnea description, and occupational history.

Smoking history includes cigarettes, pipe, cigar, marijuana, cocaine, how long the patient smoked, how long ago the patient quit, and whether they live with a smoker.

Pack years equals number of packs per day times years smoked.

Occupational history asks whether the patient worked in or around mines, quarries, foundries, gases, fumes, or asbestos.

## Topic 9: Forced Vital Capacity Procedure

The FVC maneuver is performed seated.

The patient seals tightly around the mouthpiece, avoids obstructing the mouthpiece with the tongue, wears nose clips, and breathes tidally for a few breaths.

At end exhalation, coach the patient to inhale maximally to TLC, forcefully exhale until completely empty at RV, then inhale as rapidly as possible back to TLC.

Repeat until three efforts meet ATS criteria for acceptability and reproducibility.

Coaching phrases include tight seal, normal breathing, fill the lungs completely, blast the air out, keep going, push push push, and big deep breath in.

## Topic 10: ATS Acceptability Criteria

Each effort must be free from artifact.

Start-of-test errors include poor initial blast, excessive extrapolated volume, cough in the first second, and sub-maximal inhalation.

Middle-of-test errors include variable effort, partially blocked mouthpiece, glottic closure or breath hold, early termination or no plateau, and FIVC minus FVC greater than 0.100 L or 5% of FVC, whichever is greater.

End-of-test errors include extra breath, positive zero-flow error, negative zero-flow error, and leak.

Minimum exhalation is 6 seconds for patients 10 years or older and 3 seconds for children 10 years or younger. Older or obstructed patients commonly need more than 6 seconds.

## Topic 11: Start-of-Test Errors

Sub-maximal blast is identified by low peaks on the flow-volume curve. Coach the patient to blast harder. The implication is falsely reduced FEV1 and ratio.

Excessive extrapolated volume is identified by hesitation before the initial blast. Coach the patient to blast faster and not wait to blast after the deep breath. The implication is reduced FEV1 and reduced ratio.

Back extrapolation determines and compensates for hesitation at test start. Extrapolated volume must be less than 100 mL when FVC is less than 2 L, and less than 5% when FVC is greater than 2 L.

## Topic 12: FVC

Forced vital capacity is the volume that can be expired as forcefully as possible after maximum inhalation. It is recorded in liters, BTPS.

Healthy patients have FVC equal to slow vital capacity. Obstructive disease can have FVC less than SVC due to airway collapse and air trapping. Restrictive disease has reduced FVC due to loss of lung volume.

FVC percent predicted:

- Greater than 80% is normal
- 66-79% is mild
- 50-65% is moderate
- Less than 50% is severe

## Topic 13: FEVt and FEV1

Forced expiratory volume-time is the volume of gas expired during a given interval from the beginning of the FVC maneuver. Examples are 0.5, 1.0, and 3.0 seconds. It is recorded in liters, BTPS.

FEV1 is the most commonly used timed interval and the best value to grade severity of airway obstruction.

FEV1 percent predicted:

- Greater than 80% is normal
- 70-79% is mild
- 60-69% is moderate
- 50-59% is moderately severe
- 35-49% is severe
- Less than 35% is very severe

## Topic 14: FEVt/FVC Ratio

The FEVt/FVC ratio is the ratio of FEVt to FVC, expressed as a percentage. It is commonly called the ratio.

From the source, FEV1/FVC interpretation is:

- Greater than 100% or greater than ULN suggests restrictive diseases
- About 70% is a normal patient
- 65-70% may be healthy but older patients
- Less than 70% suggests obstructive diseases

Actual timed values:

- FEV0.5 is 50-60% of FVC
- FEV1 is 75-85% of FVC
- FEV2 is 90-95% of FVC
- FEV3 is 95-98% of FVC
- FEV6 is 98-100% of FVC

## Topic 15: FEF25%-75%

FEF25%-75% is the average flow rate during the middle half of an FVC maneuver. It is recorded in L/sec, BTPS.

It is a good indicator of flow characteristics in medium and small airways. Normal is 4-5 L/sec. Values as low as 50% predicted may be considered normal. Decreased values are common in early obstructive disease and are seen in asthma and bronchitis.

## Topic 16: PEFR

Peak expiratory flow rate is the maximum flow rate attained during the FVC maneuver. It is the peak of the flow-volume loop.

It is recorded in L/sec or L/min, BTPS. It may be displayed as forced expiratory flow max, or FEF Max.

PEFR is effort dependent. Decreased values should be evaluated for reproducibility before diagnostic interpretation. It is a good indicator of patient effort.

Small-airway obstructive disease may show relatively normal PEFR. Uniform decrease in PEFR is commonly associated with non-specific upper or large airway obstruction.

## Topic 17: Graphic Displays

Flow-volume loop displays flow generated during an FVC maneuver plotted against volume, usually followed by an FIV maneuver.

Flow-volume loop:

- X-axis is volume in L, BTPS
- Y-axis is flow in L/sec
- Values include FVC, PIFR, PEFR, and FEF25%-75%

Volume-time curve:

- X-axis is time in seconds
- Y-axis is volume in L
- Values include VT, FVC/SVC, and FEVt

## Topic 18: Spirometry Interpretation

Interpretation begins by checking patient demographics, acceptability, and reproducibility.

Then evaluate:

1. FEV1/FVC measured value
2. FEV1
3. FVC percent predicted
4. FEF25%-75% percent predicted
5. Flow-volume loop

Evaluate the flow-volume loop for disease pattern shape, best loop, PEFR, and total expired time.

## Topic 19: Post-Bronchodilator Study

The objective is to perform a lung post-bronchodilator study.

Indications are determining reversibility of airway obstruction, assessing need for additional medication, and diagnosing asthma.

Contraindications and hazards are the same as spirometry.

Bronchodilator-associated hazards include tachycardia, HTN, tremors, and nausea.

Equipment includes a bronchodilator, beta-adrenergic medication, and anticholinergic medication. Beta-adrenergic medication is most commonly used.

## Topic 20: Medication Withholding Times

Inhaled bronchodilators:

- Metaproterenol (Alupent): 4 hours
- Albuterol (Proventil, Ventolin): 4 hours
- Levalbuterol (Xopenex): 8 hours
- Ipratropium (Atrovent): 4 hours
- Salmeterol (Serevent): 12 hours
- Tiotropium (Spiriva): 24 hours

Mediator modifiers:

- Cromolyn sodium (Intal): 8 hours
- Nedocromil (Tilade): 48 hours

## Topic 21: Post-Bronchodilator Procedure and Interpretation

Administer medications per local policy. The source protocol lists 4 puffs MDI per ATS. Wait 15 minutes, then repeat spirometry per ATS guidelines.

Calculate percent change:

```text
% change = ((post - pre) / pre) x 100
```

Significant airway reversibility is FEV1 or FVC increase of greater than 12% and 200 mL.

FEV1 and FEF25%-75% usually show the greatest change, although lung volumes and DLCO may also respond to bronchodilator therapy.

Failure to show significant response does not exclude a response. Reasons include moderate to severe COPD due to patient fatigue and inadequate disposition of medication.
