This lesson introduces the foundations of anatomy and physiology. Anatomy is the science of body structures and the relationships among them. Physiology is the science of body functions and how the body parts work. One describes structure, the other describes function, and the two are always connected.

The body is organized into levels, from simplest to most complex. The chemical level includes atoms, the smallest units of matter, and molecules, which are two or more atoms joined together. The cellular level is made of cells, the smallest living units in the human body. The tissue level is groups of cells and the materials surrounding them that work together to perform a particular function. An organ is two or more different types of tissues that serve particular functions. A system is a group of related organs with common functions. And the organismal level is any living individual.

There are several types of tissue. Epithelial tissue covers body surfaces, lines hollow organs and cavities, and forms glands. Connective tissue connects, supports, and protects body organs. Muscular tissue contracts to make body parts move and generates heat. Nervous tissue carries information from one part of the body to another through nerve impulses. Adipose tissue maintains insulation, stores triglycerides, and acts as a shock absorber for muscle tissue and movement. Glands secrete substances into ducts, onto surfaces, or into the blood. Endocrine glands secrete hormones, while exocrine glands secrete sweat, mucus, and digestive fluids.

The living human organism has several characteristics. Metabolism is the sum of all chemical processes in the body. Within metabolism, catabolism is the breakdown of complex substances into smaller components and is generally exergonic, while anabolism is the building up of complex substances from simpler components and is generally endergonic. The other characteristics are responsiveness, which is the ability to detect and respond to change; movement; growth; differentiation, which is the development of a cell from unspecialized to specialized; and reproduction.

Homeostasis is the maintenance of relatively stable conditions in the body's internal environment. It is regulated by the nervous system and the endocrine system. A negative feedback system reverses a change in a controlled condition, while a positive feedback system strengthens or reinforces a change. When homeostasis fails, a disorder is an abnormality of structure or function, and a disease is an illness with a recognizable set of signs and symptoms. Related sciences include epidemiology, which deals with why, when, and where diseases occur and how they spread; pharmacology, which deals with the effects and uses of a drug in treatment; and diagnosis, which is distinguishing one disorder or disease from another.

Anatomical terminology gives us a shared language. In anatomical position, the subject stands erect facing the observer, head level and eyes forward, lower limbs parallel with feet flat and directed forward, and upper limbs at the sides with palms turned forward. Prone means lying face down, and supine means lying face up. Body regions include the head, neck, trunk, upper limbs, and lower limbs.

Directional terms come in pairs. Lateral is farther from the midline; medial is nearer to the midline. Proximal is nearer to the attachment or origination of a structure; distal is farther from it. Superficial is toward or on the surface; deep is away from the surface. Superior is toward the head or upper part; inferior is away from the head or lower part. Anterior is toward the front; posterior is toward the back. The horizontal or transverse plane divides the body. Important surface lines include the vertebral line, scapular line, mid-sternal line, mid-clavicular line, anterior axillary line, and mid-axillary line.

The body has several cavities. The cranial cavity holds the brain, and the vertebral canal holds the spinal cord. The thoracic cavity contains the pericardial cavity, which holds the heart, and the pleural cavity, which holds the lungs. The diaphragm separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity. The abdominal cavity holds the stomach, spleen, liver, and intestines. The pelvic cavity holds the bladder and genital systems. The organs inside the thoracic and abdominal or pelvic cavities are called the viscera.

Basic medical terminology is built from prefixes, roots, and suffixes. For example, brady- means slow, as in bradycardia and bradypnea; tachy- means fast, as in tachypnea; dys- means difficult or abnormal, as in dyspnea; and eu- means normal or good, as in eupnea. The suffix -pnea refers to breathing, -itis means inflammation, -ectomy means removal, -emia means a blood condition, -osis means a condition or increase, and -ostomy means a surgical opening. Roots like bronch refer to the bronchus, pneum and pulmon refer to lung, trache to the trachea, cardi to the heart, nephr and ren to the kidney, and hepat to the liver. Learning these building blocks lets you decode unfamiliar terms.

The lesson closes with medical imaging. Radiography sends a single barrage of x-rays through the body to produce an image; it is inexpensive and quick, bones appear white while air and hollow structures appear black, and contrast can be added. A chest x-ray is the most rapid and utilized study to identify lung issues. Magnetic resonance imaging uses a high-energy magnetic field and radio waves; it is relatively safe and gives fine detail of soft tissue and distinguishes abnormal from normal tissue, but it cannot be used on patients with metal in the body and gives no fine detail for bones. It is used to detect tumors, artery-clogging fatty plaques, and brain abnormalities. Computed tomography uses x-rays to form transverse sectional images, visualizes soft tissue and organs in detail, can be assembled into a 3D image, and is useful for screening lung cancers, coronary artery disease, and kidney cancers. Ultrasound uses high-frequency sound waves, is safe, noninvasive, painless, and uses no dyes; it is most commonly used to visualize a fetus and to assess organ size, location, and actions and blood flow through vessels with Doppler. Coronary CT angiography injects an iodine-containing medium into a vein and uses x-ray beams to create a 3D image of the heart and vessels, mainly to identify coronary artery blockage. Positron emission tomography injects a substance that is absorbed by tissues to study physiology such as metabolism in the brain or heart. Endoscopy is a visual exam of the inside of body organs or cavities for therapeutic and diagnostic purposes, such as bronchoscopy.
