# NotebookLM Source: Cellular Level of Organization

This lesson looks at the cell, starting with the plasma membrane. The plasma membrane is a flexible outer surface made of a lipid bilayer. It separates the internal environment from the external environment and has selective permeability to nutrients and electrically charged ions. The membrane contains several kinds of proteins. Enzymes help catabolize reactions. Ion channels allow certain ions to move into and out of the cell. Identity markers determine blood type and allow the body to recognize self from non-self, which is the role of MHC. Carrier proteins allow the transit of certain substances across the membrane.

Next we cover transport across the plasma membrane. Diffusion is the mixing of particles in a solution that happens because of the particles' kinetic energy, and particles always move from areas of higher concentration to areas of lower concentration. Transport is either passive or active. Passive transport requires no cellular energy and moves substances down a concentration or electrical gradient. Active transport uses cellular energy in the form of ATP to drive substances against a concentration or electrical gradient. A related idea is electroneutrality, where the cell maintains an electrically neutral charge to keep homeostasis.

Several factors affect the diffusion rate. A steeper concentration gradient gives a greater rate. A higher temperature gives a faster rate. A larger mass of the diffusing substance gives a slower rate. A larger surface area gives a faster rate. And a greater diffusion distance gives a slower rate.

There are two passive processes to know. Simple diffusion does not require the assistance of membrane proteins. Osmosis is a form of simple diffusion and is the net movement of a solvent, which is water, through a selectively permeable membrane, moving from areas of high pressure to low pressure; the solute in the solution also exerts a force called osmotic pressure. Facilitated diffusion is diffusion that occurs through ion channels or carrier-mediated channels.

The clinical application here is tonicity. A hypotonic solution has a lower concentration of solute than is found inside normal cells, so it has less solute and more solvent. An isotonic solution has the same solute and water concentration compared to body fluids, so solute equals solvent. A hypertonic solution has a higher concentration of solute than is found inside normal cells, so it has more solute and less solvent. Three quick tips make this easier: the solute is typically salt, the solvent is typically water, and water follows salt.

Inside the cell is the cytoplasm, which is all of the cellular contents between the plasma membrane and the nucleus. The fluid portion of the cytoplasm is the cytosol, and the cytoplasm also contains organelles, which means little organs. Cilia and flagella allow movement and attachment of organisms. Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis. The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membranes shaped like flattened sacs or tubules. Rough ER is lined with ribosomes and is responsible for protein synthesis, while smooth ER handles steroid and fatty acid synthesis and detoxifies potentially harmful substances. The Golgi body complex packages and modifies proteins and other molecules, attaching or breaking apart molecules based on the cell's needs. Lysosomes are responsible for breaking down other molecules. Mitochondria are the powerhouse of the cell and generate the majority of the cell's ATP; the cristae are the ridges within the mitochondria and are the site of cellular respiration and the Krebs cycle.

Finally, the nucleus houses most of the cell's DNA.
