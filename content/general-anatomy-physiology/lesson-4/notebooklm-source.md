# NotebookLM Source: Organ Systems I — Integumentary, Skeletal, Muscular, Nervous & Endocrine

This lesson is the first of two on the body's organ systems, and it covers the integumentary, skeletal, muscular, nervous, and endocrine systems.

We start with the skin, the integumentary system. The skin has three layers. The epidermis is the superficial, thinner portion made of epithelial tissue; it is avascular and is about 90 percent keratinocytes. The dermis contains blood vessels, nerve endings, hair follicles, and oil-producing glands. The subcutaneous layer, also called the hypodermis, contains adipose tissue for insulation along with large veins and arteries. The skin performs six functions: thermoregulation, acting as a blood reservoir, protection, cutaneous sensations, excretion and absorption, and synthesis of vitamin D. The skin is also a diagnostic clue. Cyanosis is a blue coloration in the nail beds, lips, and skin that means the blood is not carrying adequate oxygen, which is hypoxemia. Jaundice is a yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes from a buildup of bilirubin and generally indicates liver failure. Erythema is a reddening of the skin from engorgement of capillaries due to injury, heat, infection, inflammation, or an allergic reaction. Pallor is paleness of the skin and indicates shock and anemia.

Next is the skeletal system. The functions of bone and the skeletal system are support, protection, movement, mineral homeostasis through storage and release, blood cell production called hemopoiesis, and triglyceride storage. Hemopoiesis is the process by which the formed elements of blood develop. The skeleton has two divisions. The axial skeleton is the bones along the vertical axis, including the skull, rib cage, and vertebrae. The appendicular skeleton includes the limbs and supporting structures. Major bones to know include the hyoid bone, which supports the muscles in the tongue, neck, and pharynx; the ribs; the spine; the clavicle and scapula; and the sternum. The sternum has three parts: the manubrium is the superior portion above the suprasternal notch, the body is the flat surface used during CPR, and the xiphoid process is the inferior portion. A joint is a point of contact between two bones. Fibrous joints are held together by fibrous connective tissue and allow little to no movement. Cartilaginous joints have no synovial cavity and are held together by cartilage with little to no movement. Synovial joints are movable. Ligaments hold bones together and are made of parallel dense bundles of connective tissue. A fracture is any break in a bone and is named by severity or by the shape and position of the fracture line. An open or compound fracture has broken ends that protrude through the skin. A comminuted fracture is splintered, crushed, or broken into pieces. A greenstick fracture has one side broken while the other bends and occurs only in children whose bones have not fully ossified. An impacted fracture is one in which one end of the bone is forcefully driven into the interior of another.

The third system is muscular tissue. There are three types of muscle. Skeletal muscle is striated and primarily voluntary. Cardiac muscle is striated, involuntary, and autorhythmic. Smooth muscle is nonstriated and generally involuntary. Auto-rhythmicity is the ability to repeatedly generate spontaneous nerve impulses, and it controls the heart rhythm, making it the pacemaker of the heart. The functions of muscular tissue are movement, stabilization, thermogenesis (which is heat generated as muscles contract to maintain body temperature), electrical excitability (the ability to respond to electrical stimuli), contractility (the ability of fibers to shorten), extensibility (the ability to stretch), and elasticity (the ability to return to original shape). Muscle proteins come in three categories. Contractile proteins, actin and myosin, generate force. Regulatory proteins, tropomyosin and troponin, turn contraction on and off; tropomyosin prevents myosin from binding to actin, and troponin is regulated by calcium and controls the attachment of tropomyosin. Structural proteins, titin, myomesin, nebulin, and dystrophin, keep proper structure, elasticity, and extensibility. The sliding filament mechanism explains that skeletal muscle shortens during contraction because the thick myosin filaments and thin actin filaments slide past one another. The contraction cycle has four steps. In ATP hydrolysis, the phosphate bond on ATP is broken by water, energy is released, the products are ADP and a phosphate group, and the energy is stored in the myosin head. In attachment, the energized myosin head attaches to the binding site and the phosphate group is released. In the power stroke, the myosin head pivots, the thin filament is pulled past the thick filament, and ADP is released. In detachment, regulatory proteins turn contraction off, ATP binds to the myosin head, and myosin releases from actin.

The fourth system is the nervous system. It is organized into the central nervous system, which is the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system, which is all nervous tissue outside the CNS. The peripheral nervous system has a somatic division, which conveys output to skeletal muscles for voluntary functions like walking, talking, and writing, and an autonomic division, which has sympathetic and parasympathetic parts that control involuntary functions like heart rate. The nervous system has three functions: sensory, which detects internal stimuli like a rise in blood pressure and external stimuli like touching a hot surface; integrative, which processes sensory information and makes decisions; and motor, which activates effectors so muscles contract and glands secrete. Communication happens at a synapse, a region where two neurons or a neuron and an effector cell communicate. Electrical synapses use gap junctions, tube-like structures that connect two cells directly for rapid communication and synchronization, and they occur in the heart and smooth muscle. Chemical synapses occur between presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons across the synaptic cleft; the presynaptic neuron releases neurotransmitters that diffuse across the cleft and bind to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron; this takes longer, and chemical synapses never close and are usually filled with interstitial fluid. A neurotransmitter is the body's chemical messenger, and a receptor is the receiver of the stimulus. In the cholinergic, parasympathetic pathway, the receptors are muscarinic and the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine. In the adrenergic, sympathetic pathway, the receptors are alpha and beta and the neurotransmitters are epinephrine and norepinephrine.

The last system is the endocrine system. Hormones are molecules released in one part of the body that regulate the activity of cells in other parts; they act slower than nerve impulses but last longer, and they are released by endocrine glands. The responsiveness to a hormone depends on the concentration of the hormone, the abundance of receptors, and the influences of other hormones. Hormones can interact in three ways: the permissive effect, where a hormone cannot exert its full effects without another hormone present; the synergistic effect, where two or more hormones together produce a greater effect than each alone; and the antagonistic effect, where one hormone opposes another. Hormone secretion is controlled by signals from the nervous system, chemical changes in the blood, and other hormones. The stress response is triggered by a stressor, any disturbance of the body such as heat, cold, toxins, or emotional reactions, and it is controlled mostly by the hypothalamus. There are three stages. Fight or flight mobilizes the body's resources for immediate physical activity by increasing glucose and oxygen to organs and blood to the brain and muscles while inhibiting nonessential functions; its effects are short term. The resistance reaction releases hormones for a longer-lasting response as the body begins returning to homeostasis. Exhaustion results from prolonged exposure to high cortisol and causes a weakened immune system, deterioration of muscles and organs, and chronic diseases.
