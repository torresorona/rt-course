# NotebookLM Source: Organ Systems II — Cardiovascular, Lymphatic, Respiratory, Digestive & Urinary

This lesson is the second of two on the body's organ systems, covering the cardiovascular, lymphoid, respiratory, digestive, and urinary systems.

We begin with the cardiovascular system and blood. Blood has two parts: plasma and formed elements. Plasma is a watery extracellular matrix, the straw-colored liquid that remains after the formed elements are separated. It is 91.5 percent water and 8.5 percent solutes, and it contains hepatocytes, albumins, globulins, fibrinogen, and antibodies; plasma is often donated for clotting factors. The formed elements are red blood cells, also called erythrocytes; white blood cells, also called leukocytes; and platelets. Blood has three functions. Transportation carries oxygen and carbon dioxide for exchange, nutrients from the digestive canal, hormones, and waste products to eliminating organs. Regulation controls pH through buffers and adjusts body temperature through water. Protection comes from coagulation properties, antibodies, and white blood cells or phagocytes. Red blood cells contain hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein, which reversibly binds oxygen and carbon dioxide. White blood cells perform phagocytosis and the immune response; the normal count is 5,000 to 10,000 per microliter and rises in times of stress such as infection, surgery, or illness. Hemostasis is the sequence of events that stops bleeding and prevents hemorrhage, and it has three steps: vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and blood clotting. For basic anatomy, arteries carry blood away from the heart and are generally oxygenated, while veins carry blood back to the heart and are generally deoxygenated. There are two circulation pathways. Systemic circulation sends oxygenated blood from the left ventricle through the aorta to all organs, and deoxygenated blood returns to the right atrium. Pulmonic circulation sends deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs through the pulmonary artery and returns oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium through the pulmonary veins.

Next is the lymphoid system. The lymphatic system consists of lymph fluid and vessels along with major organs, and it allows immune responses to occur. The spleen is the largest mass of lymph tissue in the body, and other components include red bone marrow and lymph nodules such as the tonsils. Immunity is the ability to ward off damage or disease through defenses. The functions of the lymphatic system are to drain excess interstitial fluid from tissue spaces and return it to the blood, to transport nutrients absorbed by the digestive canal, and to carry out immune responses. Innate immunity is present at birth, while adaptive immunity involves specific recognition of a microbe once it has breached the innate defenses.

The third system is the respiratory system. Respiration has three steps. Pulmonary ventilation is the act of breathing, air going in and out. External respiration is gas exchange between the alveoli and the pulmonary capillaries. Internal respiration is gas exchange between blood in the systemic capillaries and the tissue cells, which is perfusion. The upper respiratory system includes the nose, which provides humidity and olfactory senses; the nasal cavity, a large space lined with mucous membranes that produces mucus and traps debris; the oral cavity; and the pharynx or throat, which houses the tonsils. The lower respiratory tract includes the larynx or voice box, the trachea, the lungs, and the bronchi.

The fourth system is the digestive system. Its functions are to break food into smaller molecules that cells can use and to use muscular contractions that break down food, move it throughout the body, and mix it with digestive enzymes. The notable organs are the liver and the pancreas. The liver is composed of hepatocytes and handles nutrient metabolism, detoxification, and phagocytosis of bacteria in the blood through Kupffer cells. It is the first organ to absorb and metabolize nutrients from the GI tract: carbohydrates as glycogen to glucose, lipids to triglycerides and fatty acids, and proteins to amino acids used for ATP production. The pancreas also handles carbohydrates, converting glycogen to glucose; low blood glucose stimulates secretion of glucagon, while high blood glucose stimulates insulin.

The final system is the urinary system. The kidneys have seven functions: excretion of waste as urine, regulation of blood ionic composition such as sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, and phosphate, regulation of blood pH, regulation of blood volume, regulation of blood pressure, production of hormones, and regulation of blood glucose level. To regulate blood pH, the kidneys excrete excess hydrogen ions and conserve bicarbonate for buffering. To regulate blood pressure, anti-diuretic hormone increases when blood pressure is low and decreases when blood pressure is high, and renin is also involved. For hormone production, calcitriol is the active form of vitamin D and regulates calcium homeostasis, while erythropoietin stimulates production of red blood cells and becomes reduced during kidney failure. The functional unit of the kidney is the nephron. The renal corpuscle filters blood plasma and contains the glomerulus, a capillary network, and the glomerular or Bowman's capsule, a double-walled epithelium. The renal tubule is the site of major buffer action for hydrogen and bicarbonate and includes the proximal convoluted tubule, the nephron loop or loop of Henle, and the distal convoluted tubule. Nephron physiology has three steps. Glomerular filtration moves water and solutes across the glomerular capillaries and filters nutrients. Tubular reabsorption reabsorbs 99 percent of filtered water and other solutes and returns them to the blood. Tubular secretion moves waste, drugs, and excess ions out of the body through the renal tubule and duct cells.
