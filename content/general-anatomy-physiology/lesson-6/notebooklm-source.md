# NotebookLM Source: Acid-Base Homeostasis

This lesson covers how the body keeps its pH in balance. Normal blood pH is 7.35 to 7.45. An increase in hydrogen ions lowers the pH. The body removes hydrogen ions in three ways: through buffer systems, by exhaling carbon dioxide, and by kidney excretion of hydrogen ions in the urine.

Buffer systems prevent drastic changes in the pH of body fluids by converting strong acids and bases into weak acids and bases. There are three buffer systems. The protein buffer system uses albumin and hemoglobin. The carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer system relies on excess carbon dioxide being exhaled from the lungs. The phosphate buffer system enables excess hydrogen ions to be excreted through the urine.

The lungs contribute through the exhalation of carbon dioxide. Two factors affect carbon dioxide elimination: the rate of breathing, which is the respiratory rate, and the depth of breathing, which is the tidal volume. Chemoreceptors respond to changes in pH and help bring the body back to homeostasis.

The kidneys contribute by excreting hydrogen ions. Intercalated cells are epithelial cells in the distal segments of the kidney tubule that regulate acid-base homeostasis. When the pH is too low, they excrete excess hydrogen ions, and when the pH is too high, they excrete excess bicarbonate. Collecting duct buffers filter any other hydrogen ions. Monohydrogen phosphate binds to a hydrogen ion to form dihydrogen phosphate, and ammonia binds to a hydrogen ion to form ammonium. Both ammonium and dihydrogen phosphate are excreted in the urine.

Finally, there are four key acid-base imbalances to recognize: respiratory acidosis, respiratory alkalosis, metabolic acidosis, and metabolic alkalosis. The respiratory imbalances originate with the lungs and carbon dioxide, while the metabolic imbalances originate from other causes.
