# NotebookLM Source: Chemical Level of Organization

This lesson covers the chemical foundation of the body. An element is a building block of matter that cannot be broken down into simpler forms, and each element is given a chemical symbol of one or two letters. The four major elements of the human body's mass are oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, and nitrogen.

An atom is the smallest unit of matter that retains the properties of the element. Inside the nucleus are protons, which are positively charged, and neutrons, which are uncharged. Electrons, which are negatively charged, orbit outside the nucleus. The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus. The mass number, also called atomic weight or atomic mass, is the sum of protons and neutrons and reflects the average mass of all occurring isotopes, measured in Daltons. An isotope is an atom of an element with a different number of neutrons but the same number of protons. A Dalton is the standard unit for measuring the mass of atoms and subatomic particles.

When atoms gain or lose electrons, they become ions. An ion is a particle with a positive or negative charge because it has unequal numbers of protons and electrons. A cation has a positive charge, and an anion has a negative charge. Ionization is the process of giving up or gaining electrons. A molecule is two or more atoms sharing electrons, and a molecular formula tells you the elements and the number of atoms of each. A compound is a substance that contains atoms of two or more different elements. Remember that all compounds are molecules, but not all molecules are compounds.

A free radical is an atom or group of atoms with an unpaired electron in the outermost shell. This causes instability, makes the radical highly reactive, and makes it destructive to nearby molecules. Free radicals come from exposure to excessive UV radiation, x-rays, cigarettes, air pollutants, and superoxides, and they can contribute to cancers, atherosclerosis, and Alzheimer's. Antioxidants can slow the pace of damage from free radicals.

Chemical bonds are the forces that hold the atoms of a molecule together. The octet rule explains that elements bind in an attempt to fill the valence, or outer, shell for stability, which makes reactions predictable. There are three bonds to know. Ionic bonds form when positive and negative ions are attracted to each other, and you can think of the ionic bond as taking the electron. An electrolyte is an ionic compound that breaks into positive and negative ions in solution and can conduct an electric current. Covalent bonds form when two or more atoms share electrons; polar covalent sharing is unequal and creates a partial charge, while nonpolar covalent sharing is completely equal. Hydrogen bonds form when a partially positive hydrogen atom attracts a partially negative atom. The cohesion of water molecules creates surface tension, because water is more attracted to itself than to air. Surface tension is the difficulty of stretching or breaking the surface of a liquid, and this matters in the lungs: a thin watery fluid coats the air sacs, and each inhalation must have enough force to overcome surface tension as the air sacs stretch and enlarge, which relates to compliance.

Chemical reactions occur when new bonds form or old bonds break, based on the interactions of valence electrons. The starting substances are reactants and the ending substances are products. Metabolism is all of the chemical reactions occurring in the body. In a decomposition reaction, a large molecule breaks down into smaller ones, such as water breaking into hydrogen and a hydroxyl group. In a synthesis reaction, smaller molecules combine into a larger one, such as hydrogen and hydroxyl forming water. In an exchange reaction, two molecules swap atoms, such as hydrochloric acid plus sodium hydroxide forming salt and water. A reversible reaction can go in either direction and is governed by the law of mass action, which says that if one side has a greater quantity of molecules, the reaction proceeds toward the other side. Dehydration synthesis removes a hydroxyl group and a hydrogen ion, releasing water, so water comes out. Hydrolysis, or hydration synthesis, splits a bond when water ionizes into a hydroxyl group and a hydrogen ion that attach to the monomers, so water goes in. The rate of reactions depends on the concentration of reactants, temperature, and catalysts. An exergonic reaction releases energy, like glucose, while an endergonic reaction absorbs energy, and catalysts such as enzymes speed reactions by lowering activation energy.

Carbon is the building block of the body's macromolecules. A monomer is a small building block, a polymer is two or more monomers, an isomer has the same molecular formula but a different structure, and macromolecules are generally polymers, including carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Carbohydrates are the primary source of cellular energy and include sugars, glycogen, starches, and cellulose; glycolysis is the splitting of sugars. Lipids are a secondary energy source used in times of stress and are not broken down as readily as carbohydrates; lipolysis reverses lipid structures back to fatty acids and glycerol to form glucose. Lipids are hydrophobic, meaning insoluble in water. Fatty acids are the simplest lipids and are used to make triglycerides and phospholipids. A triglyceride is three fatty acid chains bonded to glycerol; the solid room-temperature form is fat and the liquid form is oil. Eicosanoids are signaling molecules in the inflammatory response, such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes, and steroids can block the effects of inflammatory responses.

Proteins are responsible for the structure of body tissues and are made of amino acids. Enzymes are proteins that speed up biochemical reactions as catalysts. The substrate is the reactant on which the enzyme acts, and the active site is the area of the enzyme that catalyzes the reaction. The lock-and-key model says a specific substrate fits a specific active site, while the induced-fit model says the active site molds around the substrate.

Nucleic acids store genetic information. DNA forms inherited genetic information inside human cells, is double stranded as a double helix, and replicates by splitting and transcribing the corresponding nucleotide. A mutation is any change in the base sequence of DNA and can cause cell death, cancer, or genetic defects in future generations. A chromosome is a thin, threadlike structure made of protein and DNA that carries hereditary information from cell to cell and contains genes, the basic units of heredity. RNA carries single-stranded genetic information and includes messenger RNA, ribosomal RNA, and transfer RNA.

Finally, ATP is the energy currency of living organisms, produced by cellular respiration, the oxidation of glucose. In phase one, glycolysis oxidizes one glucose molecule into two molecules of pyruvic acid in the cytoplasm; this is anaerobic, meaning it occurs without oxygen, and produces four ATP. In phase two, the Krebs cycle uses Acetyl-CoA to break pyruvic acid into NADH, hydrogen ions, and FADH2, with carbon dioxide and water as byproducts; this is aerobic, meaning it requires oxygen, produces about 36 to 38 ATP, and occurs in the cristae of the mitochondria.
