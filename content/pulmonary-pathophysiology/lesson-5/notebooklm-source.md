Pulmonary vascular disease in this lesson includes pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension, and idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension as listed in the source overview.

Pulmonary embolism is a sudden blockage of a pulmonary artery, most often from a venous blood clot or embolus. The source states that 80 percent originate from deep veins of the lower extremities. Pulmonary emboli occur more often in the right lung and more frequently in the lower lobes. They cause ischemia and infarction of lung tissue distal to the embolism. A large enough embolus can result in death.

Embolus origins listed in the material include deep vein thrombosis, fat from the marrow of a broken long bone such as femur or pelvis, collagen or other tissue, part of a tumor, and air bubbles.

Pulmonary embolism signs and symptoms include severe dyspnea with rapid onset over seconds to minutes, pleuritic chest pain, cough, hemoptysis, tachycardia, diaphoresis, and anxiety. Risk factors include advanced age, post-operative state, previous DVT, trauma, oral contraceptive use, thrombocytosis, prolonged bed rest, long periods of travel, cancer, obesity, smoking, and CVA.

Diagnosis includes patient history and physical, chest x-ray, ABG, D-dimer ELISA to detect emboli, V/Q scan, CT, and pulmonary angiography. Treatment includes thrombolytics, anticoagulants, and surgical or percutaneous pulmonary embolectomy. Thrombolytics listed in the source include Eminase or anistreplase, Retavase or reteplase, Streptase or streptokinase or kabikinase, t-PA class drugs including Activase, and TNKase or tenecteplase. Anticoagulants include heparin, Eliquis, and others.

Pulmonary hypertension is defined as mean pulmonary artery pressure greater than 25 mmHg at rest. Normal mean pulmonary artery pressure is 8 to 20 mmHg. Classifications listed in the source include pulmonary arterial hypertension, congenital heart defect, liver cirrhosis, viral infections, drugs and toxins, pulmonary hypertension from left heart disease, pulmonary hypertension from lung disease or hypoxemia, chronic thromboembolism, and multifactorial mechanisms.

Pulmonary hypertension signs and symptoms include dyspnea, angina, syncope, cough, hemoptysis, loud second heart sound, and right-sided third or fourth heart sound. Diagnosis can include CBC, HIV testing, rheumatologic panel, liver function panel, EKG, PFT, echocardiogram, pulmonary artery catheterization, V/Q scan, CT angiography, and Swan-Ganz catheter or pulmonary artery catheter.

Treatment focuses on treating the cause. Options listed in the source include oral anticoagulation with INR around 2, oxygen therapy to prevent pulmonary vasoconstriction, diuretics, calcium channel blockers, prostanoids such as Flolan, and lung transplant.
