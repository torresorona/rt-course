Acute respiratory distress syndrome, or ARDS, is defined in the source using the Berlin definition language as acute diffuse inflammatory lung injury leading to increased pulmonary vascular permeability, increased lung weight, and loss of aerated lung tissue. It includes hypoxemia and bilateral radiographic opacities, increased venous admixture, increased physiologic dead space, and decreased lung compliance.

A syndrome is a combination of symptoms resulting from a single cause or so commonly occurring together that it forms a distinct clinical picture. ARDS key components include acute onset within 1 week or less, bilateral opacities on chest x-ray or CT consistent with pulmonary edema, PaO2/FiO2 ratio less than 300 mmHg with 5 cmH2O PEEP or greater, and a condition not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload. When there is no clear cause such as trauma or sepsis, an objective assessment such as echocardiogram should be performed in most cases.

The P/F ratio is a quick formula used to identify acute hypoxemic respiratory failure when supplemental oxygen has already been given and no room air ABG is available. Acute hypoxemic respiratory failure can be defined by PaO2 less than 60 mmHg on room air, SpO2 less than 91 percent on room air, or P/F ratio less than 300 on oxygen. Normal PaO2 is 80 to 100 mmHg and normal FiO2 is 0.21. A PaO2 of 80 on 0.21 gives a P/F ratio of 380. A PaO2 of 100 on 0.21 gives a P/F ratio of 476. The source lists normal P/F ratio as greater than 400. Abnormal examples include 60 divided by 0.21 equals 285, 80 divided by 0.40 equals 200, and 60 divided by 0.80 equals 75.

ARDS can begin from direct or indirect pulmonary insult causing an inflammatory response that promotes neutrophil accumulation in the lung microcirculation. These neutrophils migrate across vascular endothelial and alveolar epithelial surfaces, releasing proteases, cytokines, and reactive oxygen species. This leads to vascular permeability, gaps in the alveolar epithelial barrier, and necrosis of type I and type II alveolar cells. The result is pulmonary edema, hyaline membrane formation, surfactant loss, decreased pulmonary compliance, and difficult gas exchange. Later fibroblast infiltration can lead to collagen deposition, fibrosis, and worsening disease.

Direct ARDS injuries listed in the source include pneumonia, gastric aspiration, toxic inhalation, near drowning, and lung contusion from blunt trauma. Signs and symptoms shared by ARDS and CHF include anxiety, dyspnea, tachypnea, reduced lung volumes, decreased lung compliance, respiratory alkalosis and hypoxemia, and x-ray with diffuse alveolar and interstitial infiltrates. The source contrasts ARDS with CHF by noting ARDS patient history, peripheral x-ray infiltrates, PCWP less than 18 mmHg, and proteinaceous and inflammatory BAL. Treatment and management include mechanical ventilation, lung protective strategies, high-frequency ventilation, inverse ratio ventilation, pressure control ventilation, and airway pressure release ventilation.

Lung cancer is introduced by defining cancer as a collection of related diseases in which cells divide without stopping and spread into surrounding tissues. Cancer spreads through tissue into nearby areas, through the lymphatic system to other parts of the body, and through blood vessels to other parts of the body.

Lung cancer risk factors listed in the source include tobacco smoke exposure, active or passive, genetic factors, sex, diet, COPD, and pollution. Lung cancer has two major groups: small cell carcinoma at 20 percent and non-small cell carcinoma at 80 percent. Non-small cell carcinoma includes adenocarcinoma at 40 percent, squamous cell carcinoma at 30 percent, and large cell carcinoma at 10 percent.

The source describes lung cancer pathophysiology as genetic material damage in lung cells from carcinogen exposure. Damaged cells divide out of control. Cancer cells block apoptosis, recruiting macrophages. Macrophages trigger inflammation and angiogenesis, or new blood vessel formation. Cancer cells form a tumor using the new vessels and then move to secondary sites through metastasis.

Signs and symptoms include cough, dyspnea, hemoptysis, blood or rusty-colored secretions, fatigue, unexplained weight loss, recurrent respiratory infections, hoarseness, and new wheezing. Diagnosis includes history and physical, chest x-ray, CT scan, PET scan, and bronchoscopy. Staging uses Roman numerals from 0 to IV. Lower stages indicate cancer limited to the lung. Stage IV is advanced and has spread to other areas.

Treatment for non-small cell lung cancer includes surgical resection for local control, radiation therapy for local control, and chemotherapy for systemic control. Surgical options include wedge resection, segmental resection, lobectomy, and pneumonectomy. Wedge resection removes a small tumor-containing section plus margin of healthy tissue. Segmental resection removes a larger portion but not an entire lobe. Lobectomy removes an entire lobe. Pneumonectomy removes an entire lung. Small cell lung cancer treatment includes chemotherapy and radiation therapy.
