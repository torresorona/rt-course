This lesson covers obstructive pulmonary diseases using the source mnemonic CBABE: cystic fibrosis, bronchitis, asthma, bronchiectasis, and emphysema. The source defines COPD using the American Thoracic Society description: a preventable and treatable disease characterized by airflow limitation that is not fully reversible. The limitation is usually progressive and associated with chronic inflammatory response of the lungs to noxious particles or gases.

COPD is a nonspecific term for conditions that develop progressively from several disease processes, most commonly chronic bronchitis, emphysema, and asthma. Diagnosis of obstructive lung disease uses spirometry as the primary test because it measures flows and volumes. Lung volumes measure RV, FRC, and TLC. Diffusion testing measures how well oxygen moves across the alveolar-capillary membrane. In obstructive disease, airway resistance is high and compliance is normal or high. PaO2 and SaO2 reflect oxygenation, while PaCO2 reflects ventilation.

Cystic fibrosis is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the gene for the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator protein, or CFTR. CFTR controls movement of chloride ions through cell membranes and is involved in sweat, digestive fluid, and mucus production. When CFTR does not function, secretions become thick and cause persistent lung infections.

Pulmonary manifestations of cystic fibrosis include copious thick mucoid secretions that clog airways and trap bacteria. Recurring infections include Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Other pulmonary findings include atelectasis, bronchiectasis, hyperinflation, and severe hypoxemia. Pancreatic mucus prevents digestive enzyme release, causing malnutrition and deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K. Sweat gland dysfunction causes very salty skin and easy dehydration.

Cystic fibrosis diagnosis is based on clinical manifestations such as recurrent pulmonary infections and failure to grow, plus family history. Both parents must be carriers. The source lists a 25 percent chance the child will have CF, a 50 percent chance the child will be a carrier without CF, and a 25 percent chance the child will not be a carrier and will not have CF. Diagnosis is confirmed by sweat chloride testing. Less than 40 mEq/L is normal, 40 to 60 mEq/L is borderline and should be repeated, and greater than 60 mEq/L is positive. Monitoring includes spirometry, chest x-ray, and sputum cultures. Treatment includes airway clearance therapy, exercise, inhaled bronchodilators, DNase or Pulmozyme, 7 percent hypertonic saline, antibiotics such as tobramycin, and lung transplantation.

Chronic bronchitis is inflammation of the bronchial airways that causes productive cough. Acute bronchitis is usually viral. Chronic bronchitis is a chronic productive cough present for at least 3 months per year for two consecutive years, after other causes such as GERD, asthma, and postnasal drip have been ruled out. Causes include cigarette smoke, air pollution or dust, and toxic gas, fumes, or chemicals. Pathologic changes include mucus hypersecretion, ciliary dysfunction, mucosal inflammation and edema, mucus plugging, hyperinflation of alveoli, and bronchoconstriction.

Chronic bronchitis signs include productive cough with clear, white, yellowish, green, or rarely blood-streaked sputum, low-grade fever, and the blue bloater pattern. The source describes the patient as generally overweight and cyanotic. Symptoms include shortness of breath, chest discomfort, and fatigue. Diagnosis includes history and physical, PFT, ABG, and x-ray. Spirometry is decreased, lung volumes are normal, DLCO is normal, PaO2 is normal or decreased, and PaCO2 is normal or increased. Treatment includes monitoring PFT, ABG, and x-ray, smoking cessation, bronchodilator therapy, oxygen therapy, and pulmonary rehab.

Asthma is a syndrome with airway obstruction due to airway inflammation and/or airway hyper-responsiveness or bronchospasm from various stimuli. It is partially or completely reversible spontaneously or with treatment. Triggers include allergens, respiratory infections, occupational or environmental exposures, exercise or hyperventilation, cold dry air, cigarette smoke, inhaled irritants, and stress. Inhaled allergen causes IgE molecules to attach to mast cells in the bronchial mucosa, leading to release of histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, and platelet-activating factor. Symptoms include episodic wheezing, bronchospasm, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough. Diagnosis includes history, physical, wheezing, rhinitis, cough, PFT using FEV1 and FEV1/FVC, bronchial provocation testing, and allergy testing. Management includes monitoring lung function, pharmacologic therapy, environmental control, and patient education.

Bronchiectasis is abnormal permanent dilation and thickening of the airways caused by inflammation from recurring lung infections. Causes include pulmonary infections, foreign body aspiration, primary ciliary dyskinesia or Kartagener syndrome, and cystic fibrosis. Signs and symptoms include classic cough and mucopurulent sputum production lasting months to years, blood-streaked sputum or hemoptysis, dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, wheezing, fever, weakness, fatigue, and weight loss. Diagnosis uses chronic respiratory history, obstructive PFT, sputum cultures, chest x-ray, and high-resolution CT as the standard test. Treatment includes antibiotics, airway clearance, bronchodilators, corticosteroids, dietary supplementation, oxygen for hypoxemic patients, hospitalization for severe exacerbations, and surgery for localized disease only.

Emphysema is abnormal permanent enlargement of airspaces beyond the terminal bronchioles with destruction of alveolar walls. Causes include cigarette smoke, air pollution or dust, and toxic gas, fumes, or chemicals. The protease-antiprotease theory explains acinar wall destruction when proteases such as elastase overpower antiproteases. Smoking increases lung proteases and impairs antiproteases. Alpha1-antitrypsin deficiency accounts for 5 percent in the source and prevents normal deactivation of elastase. Emphysema changes include permanent airspace enlargement and destruction, pulmonary capillary destruction, weakened distal airways, bronchospasm, and air trapping. Signs include slight cough, labored breathing with prolonged expiratory time, increased AP chest, barrel chest, orthopnea, pink puffer pattern, pursed-lip breathing, and pink skin color rather than cyanosis. Diagnosis includes history and physical, ABG, PFT, and x-ray. Treatment includes monitoring, smoking cessation, bronchodilator therapy, oxygen therapy, pulmonary rehab, and lung reduction surgery.
