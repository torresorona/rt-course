This lesson covers pneumonia and tuberculosis. Pneumonia is a lower respiratory tract infection that inflames the alveoli in one or both lungs. The alveoli may fill with fluid or pus. Lobar pneumonia is acute inflammation of an entire lobe or lung. Bronchopneumonia is patchy foci of consolidation scattered in one or more lobes of one or both lungs.

The source lists pneumonia as the 8th leading cause of death in the U.S., with 5 million cases annually, 1 million hospitalizations, and 50,622 deaths. Risk factors include infants and young children, adults over 65, smoking, immunocompromised status, and recurring respiratory infections.

Pneumonia causes include bacteria, viruses, and fungi. Bacterial examples are Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Legionella pneumonia, and Staphylococcus aureus. Viral examples are influenza A and B, corona, respiratory syncytial virus, and adenoviruses. Fungal examples are Coccidioides causing valley fever and Aspergillus causing aspergillosis.

Pneumonia is classified by where it was acquired because that helps predict the likely cause and guide empiric antimicrobial therapy. Community-acquired pneumonia can be acute or chronic. Acute typical CAP includes pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae, and Staphylococcus aureus. Acute atypical CAP includes Legionella pneumophila, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and viruses. Chronic CAP develops over weeks to months and includes Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Histoplasma capsulatum.

Health care-associated pneumonia develops in patients hospitalized in acute care for 2 or more days within the last 90 days or in a long-term care facility in the last 30 days. Hospital-acquired pneumonia develops more than 48 hours after admission after CAP is ruled out. Common HAP pathogens include Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus including MRSA and MSSA, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and E. coli. Ventilator-associated pneumonia develops more than 48 to 72 hours after intubation. The endotracheal tube impairs cough, allows microaspiration around the cuff, permits biofilm formation, pools secretions around the cuff, and impairs mucociliary clearance.

Pneumonia pathogenesis includes inhalation of aerosolized particles, aspiration from the oropharynx, direct introduction into the lower airway, spread from adjacent organs, spread through the blood, and reactivation of latent infection in immunosuppressed patients. Clinical signs include pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, sputum production, fever, chills, muscle aches, stiffness, cough, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea.

Pneumonia diagnosis includes chest x-ray, CT, cultures, lab tests, bronchoscopy, and mini BAL. Lab tests may include CBC, Chem 7, ABG, HIV testing, AFB if TB is suspected, blood culture, influenza testing, and RSV testing. Sputum Gram stain and culture identify bacterial growth. Sensitivity testing determines whether a particular antibiotic or antifungal is likely to stop organism growth. Bronchoscopy can obtain lower respiratory tract samples when expectorated samples cannot be obtained or are nondiagnostic. Treatment can include antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals, oxygen, fluids, and airway clearance therapy.

Tuberculosis is an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It may affect almost any tissue, especially the lungs, and is characterized by tubercles. Primary active TB is symptomatic shortly after exposure. Reactivation latent TB becomes symptomatic months to years after exposure. Endobronchial TB involves the airways and can result from primary or reactivation TB. Extrapulmonary TB spreads beyond the lung and may reach any organ through the lymphatic system.

Active TB is symptomatic, infectious, PPD positive, IGRA positive, AFB negative or positive, and has abnormal imaging. Latent TB has no symptoms, is not infectious, is PPD positive and IGRA positive, is AFB negative, and has normal imaging or granulomas.

TB is acquired by inhalation of airborne droplets containing Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Most individuals, 85 to 95 percent, contain the infection and remain asymptomatic but remain susceptible to reactivation later in life or if immunosuppressed. The immune response forms a tubercle or caseous granuloma. If the immune system cannot stop the infection, the organism multiplies, the granuloma center liquefies through caseation, and organisms can escape to infect other lung areas.

TB symptoms include cough for at least 3 weeks, barking cough, hemoptysis, fever, weight loss, night sweats, fatigue, and chest pain. Diagnosis includes history and assessment, chest x-ray, PPD skin test, AFB smear or sputum culture, NAAT, and IGRA blood testing. Morning sputum samples are collected on 3 consecutive days. Treatment lasts 6 to 12 months and includes pyrazinamide, ethambutol, rifampin, isoniazide, and streptomycin. BCG vaccination is not used in the U.S. and causes a false positive PPD.

Hospitalized patients with suspected or confirmed TB require respiratory isolation and airborne precautions. Use a private negative-pressure room. Staff enter with N95 masks. An N95 blocks at least 95 percent of 0.3 micron test particles if properly fitted. Infected patients can be transported through the hospital with simple surgical masks.
