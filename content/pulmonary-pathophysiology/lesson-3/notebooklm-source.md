Restrictive lung disease is a group of respiratory diseases that restrict lung expansion. This results in decreased lung volume, increased work of breathing, and inadequate ventilation and/or oxygenation. The source organizes restriction with PAINT: pleural, alveolar, interstitial, neuromuscular, and thoracic or extrathoracic disease. Pleural examples include pleural effusions, empyema, and pneumothorax. Alveolar examples include edema and hemorrhage. Interstitial examples include IPF and pneumonitis. Neuromuscular examples include myasthenia gravis, ALS, and Guillain-Barre. Thoracic or extrathoracic examples include obesity, kyphoscoliosis, and ascites.

Restrictive diagnosis includes spirometry, lung volumes, and diffusion. Lung volumes are the primary PFT test to diagnose restrictive disease because they measure RV, FRC, and TLC. Diffusion measures how well oxygen moves across the alveolar-capillary membrane. Airway resistance is normal and compliance is normal or low.

Pleural diseases include pleural effusion, pneumothorax, hemothorax, and bronchopleural fistula. Pleural effusion is any abnormal excessive amount of fluid in the pleural space. Transudative effusions are caused by hydrostatic pressure and include CHF, liver failure or cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome. Transudative fluid has decreased proteins. Exudative effusions are inflammatory and may be caused by pneumonia, cancer, TB, PE, or viral infections. Exudative fluid has increased proteins. Symptoms include sharp chest pain worse with cough or deep breaths, cough, tachypnea, and shortness of breath. Diagnosis includes physical exam, diminished breath sounds, dull percussion, asymmetrical chest expansion, tracheal deviation away from the affected lung, chest x-ray with blunting of the costophrenic angle, CT, and ultrasound. Treatment includes thoracentesis and treating the underlying cause. Recurring effusions may require a pleural catheter, pleurodesis with chemical or talc, or surgical pleural removal.

Pneumothorax occurs when rupture of the lung or airway and visceral pleura allows air into the pleural space. Non-traumatic spontaneous or closed pneumothorax can be primary or secondary. Primary spontaneous pneumothorax occurs in patients without underlying lung disease and is associated in the source with late teens to early twenties, tall, thin, male. Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax occurs with underlying lung disease such as COPD, especially emphysema, asthma, cystic fibrosis, or interstitial lung disease. Traumatic or open pneumothorax includes blunt trauma, penetrating injury, or medical procedure. Open pneumothorax is a sucking chest wound that allows atmospheric air into the pleural space through the chest wall.

Signs and symptoms of pneumothorax depend on size and type and include dyspnea, cyanosis, restlessness, agitation, chest pain, tachypnea, tachycardia initially, bradycardia as the leak worsens, hypertension initially, and hypotension as the leak worsens. Treatment includes observation, needle aspiration, and small pigtail catheter. Diagnosis includes history and physical, chest x-ray, breath sounds, and digital percussion.

Tension pneumothorax occurs when air enters the pleural space and has no vent to atmosphere. It is especially dangerous during positive pressure ventilation. Mediastinal shift can quickly lead to cardiovascular collapse because the vena cava and right heart cannot accept venous return. No venous return means no cardiac output, and no cardiac output means death. Signs include tracheal deviation away from the affected side, decreased breath sounds, hypertympanic percussion, jugular venous distension, unequal chest expansion, pulsus paradoxus, subcutaneous emphysema, and difficulty ventilating with high airway pressures.

Hemothorax is associated with traumatic injuries and thoracic surgery. The lung collapses to some degree depending on the blood volume. The source notes less risk of mediastinal shift because enough blood to shift the mediastinum would also cause life-threatening blood volume loss. Pneumothorax and hemothorax treatment can include oxygen, observation, percutaneous needle aspiration for air only, and chest tube for air or fluid.

Bronchopleural fistula is a pneumothorax involving a large airway such as a mainstem, lobar, or segmental airway and causes a large air leak. It is common after lung resections and may require multiple chest tubes. Monitor the chest drainage air leak and ventilator values including tidal volume, airway pressure, and PEEP.

Interstitial lung disease is a broad group of more than 200 diseases affecting lung parenchyma, characterized by fibrosis or inflammation. The interstitium is the area between capillaries and alveoli. Lung parenchyma includes alveoli, alveolar ducts, and respiratory bronchioles. ILD begins with inflammation such as bronchiolitis, alveolitis, or vasculitis, and can lead to scarring in the interstitium. Known causes include tobacco exposure, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, drug-related causes, radiation therapy, occupational exposure, and systemic disease. Tobacco-related examples include RB-ILD, DIP, and PLCH. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis is a cell-mediated immune reaction to inhaled antigens such as bacteria, fungi, humidification systems, indoor hot tubs, and animal proteins. Occupational examples include asbestosis, silicosis, and coal workers pneumoconiosis.

Sarcoidosis is an idiopathic multisystem disorder in which inflammatory cells collect in granulomas. It commonly affects lungs and lymph nodes but can also affect eyes, skin, heart, and other organs. It is the most common ILD in the U.S. in the source. Symptoms include fatigue, swollen mediastinal lymph nodes, weight loss, joint pain and swelling, persistent dry cough, shortness of breath, wheezing, chest pain, and pulmonary nodules on x-ray. Unknown-cause ILDs include IPF, NSIP, COP, and LIP. ILD signs include dyspnea on exertion, nonproductive cough, fine inspiratory crackles, hemoptysis, and pneumothorax. Diagnosis includes restrictive PFT, decreased DLCO, decreased compliance, ABG, exercise testing, imaging, bronchoscopy, BAL, and biopsy. Treatment includes oxygen, vaccination, infection avoidance, pulmonary rehab, exercise, and transplant.

Neuromuscular disorders in the source include myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barre, phrenic nerve damage, spinal cord trauma, and stroke. Myasthenia gravis is an incurable autoimmune neuromuscular disease causing weakness, ptosis, dysphagia, hoarseness, diplopia, difficulty walking, and possible respiratory failure in myasthenic crisis. It is caused by thymus abnormalities releasing antibodies that block or destroy nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. Diagnosis includes AChR antibodies, CT or MRI of thymus, and Tensilon testing. Monitoring includes VC less than 20 mL/kg or 30 percent of baseline, MIP less than -30 cmH2O, and MEP less than +40 cmH2O. Treatment includes thymectomy, pyridostigmine, IV immune globulin, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, plasmapheresis, and mechanical ventilation.

Guillain-Barre is acute demyelinating polyneuropathy. The immune system attacks the myelin sheath of peripheral nerves, preventing signals from nerves to muscles. It is often preceded by respiratory or GI infection 1 to 4 weeks before symptoms. About 20 percent develop respiratory failure. Diagnosis includes history, spinal tap, electromyography, and nerve conduction studies. Monitoring uses the same VC, MIP, and MEP thresholds. Treatment includes IV immune globulin, plasmapheresis, and mechanical ventilation.

Phrenic nerve damage can be caused by high cervical spine injury or cardiac surgery. It may cause no symptoms, though athletes and musicians may notice decreased ventilatory capacity. Diagnosis includes reduced VC and TLC, worse values supine, chest x-ray, and fluoroscopic sniff test showing paradoxical upward movement of the affected side during vigorous inspiration. Treatment is surgical plication of the affected side.

Stroke is acute interruption in blood flow to an area of the brain. It may be ischemic, hemorrhagic, or TIA. Respiratory complications include central or obstructive sleep apnea, venous thromboembolism, swallowing abnormalities, aspiration, and pneumonia. Chest wall or thoracic diseases include kyphosis, scoliosis, kyphoscoliosis, and flail chest. Flail chest occurs when multiple adjacent ribs fracture in two or more places and part of the thoracic cage becomes free-floating.
