# NotebookLM Source: Infection, Disease & Epidemiology

This lesson covers infection, disease, and defense. It begins with the host-microbe relationship. Symbiosis is a close relationship between two different types of organisms in a community. Mutualism means both members benefit. Commensalism means one organism benefits while the other is neither harmed nor helped. Parasitism means one organism benefits while the other is harmed. A pathogen is a parasite capable of causing disease.

Normal flora are microorganisms that are always present on or in a person and usually do not cause disease. Normal flora are found on the skin, mucous membranes, eyes, mouth, digestive tract, and respiratory tract. They help impede colonization by other bacteria, deplete nutrients so other microbes struggle to survive, and can make an environment too acidic for colonization.

Opportunistic pathogens become pathogenic when they take advantage of opportunities that are not normally available in a host. Pseudomonas aeruginosa in cystic fibrosis patients is one example. Risk situations include acute and chronic disease, malnutrition, emotional or physical stress, age extremes, radiation or chemotherapy, and immunosuppressive drugs used in transplant patients.

Opportunistic pathogens can cause infection when normal flora changes. Candida albicans can cause yeast infection after prolonged antibiotic use. Hormonal change, stress, diet change, or exposure to excess organisms can allow other bacteria to thrive. A member of normal flora can also cause infection when it gains access to a body area it does not normally inhabit, such as E. coli from the GI tract entering the urethra and causing a UTI. Injury, burns, and surgery can also create opportunities for infection.

The stages of infection begin with a portal of entry. A portal of entry is the site where the pathogen enters the body, such as skin, mucous membranes, or placenta. Parenteral entry introduces pathogens directly into subcutaneous tissue. Virulence is the degree of disease-evoking power of a microbe based on virulence factors. Pathogenicity is the ability to cause disease. Virulence factors enhance the potential to cause disease.

Invasion is the ability to break down primary and secondary defensive barriers of the host. Adhesion is the first step in infection that allows colonization. Fimbriae, pili, and receptor sites enable adhesion. Colonization occurs when the pathogen begins to replicate once a foothold is established. Common sites include the urogenital tract, digestive tract, respiratory tract, and conjunctiva of the eye.

Evasion is the ability to avoid the host response. A slime layer or capsule can prevent phagocytosis. Some organisms produce proteins that bind to host cell antibodies. Mutation of the organism can also help evasion.

Exotoxins are proteins secreted by bacterial cells. They are specific to the microbe and often specific to the substrate where they bind. Clostridium tetani produces tetanus. Neurotoxins damage nervous tissue. Endotoxins are released from cell walls during lysis. Release can be initiated by antibiotics or host defense. Endotoxins are less specific to the site of infection and can work at remote sites. Infectious dose is the amount of cells needed to infect the host and contract disease. Portals of exit are where the pathogen leaves the infected patient, including excretion or secretion, sneeze or cough, and blood.

Patterns of infection include local, focal, systemic, and septicemia or sepsis. Local infection stays confined to a specific tissue, such as warts or fungal skin infections. Focal infection spreads from a local infection to another tissue. Systemic infection spreads to several sites and tissue fluids, usually through the circulatory system. Septicemia or sepsis is infection of the blood. Other types include mixed infection, chronic infection, acute infection, primary infection, and secondary infection.

A reservoir is a source of infection. Animal reservoirs transmit disease through contact with the animal, animal waste, or waste-contaminated food or water. Vectors are animals or insects that carry disease-causing pathogens. Human reservoirs occur when the human body carries a pathogen; the person may show signs or be asymptomatic. Carriers are infected but asymptomatic and can infect others without knowing. Nonliving reservoirs include soil, water, and food.

Direct contact transmission involves direct physical contact with the infectious agent and no intermediate object. Examples include kissing, touching, and intercourse. Indirect contact transmission occurs when a pathogen moves from reservoir to host by a fomite, which is an inanimate object such as a stethoscope, towel, or shared item. Droplet transmission occurs through respiratory droplets traveling less than 1 meter or about 3 feet. Examples include coughing, laughing, sneezing, and talking. Diseases include common cold, flu, and pertussis. For droplet precautions, place the patient in a private room if possible, cohort if necessary, perform hand hygiene, and wear a surgical mask before entering the room.

Vehicle transmission includes airborne, waterborne, foodborne, and bodily fluid transmission. Airborne transmission spreads through droplet nuclei, aerosols, and dust that can travel more than 1 meter. It uses a negative pressure room, HEPA filtration with 6 to 12 air changes per hour, a closed room door, and keeping the patient in the room. Waterborne transmission is associated with untreated or poorly treated sewage. Foodborne transmission is associated with incompletely cooked, poorly processed, or poorly refrigerated foods. Bodily fluid transmission may involve blood, saliva, urine, and other body fluids and includes diseases such as AIDS, hepatitis, and herpes.

Vector transmission comes from animals. Biological vectors transmit pathogens through bites, such as mosquitoes or lice. Mechanical vectors carry pathogens passively by feet or body parts but do not necessarily host the pathogen.

Healthcare-associated infections, or HAIs, are also called nosocomial infections. They are acquired during treatment in a hospital-like setting but are secondary to the patient's original condition. Exogenous infections are shed from other sick individuals. Endogenous infections are caused by the patient's normal flora. Iatrogenic infections result from medical procedures such as catheters and surgery.

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when mutations allow pathogens to resist treatments that once worked. Control and prevention include disinfection, proper hand hygiene, surgical asepsis, isolation, cohorting, standard precautions, and proper transmission-based precautions. Hand hygiene should happen before and after patient contact, immediately after removing gloves, when avoiding cross-contamination, after touching body fluids or contaminated items, and between tasks on the same patient when contamination between body sites is possible. Use an approved alcohol-based product unless hands are visibly soiled; when visibly soiled, use soap and water.
