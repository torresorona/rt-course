# NotebookLM Source: Basic Science, Microbial Control & Lab Safety

This lesson begins with basic cell classification. Eukaryotic cells have a membrane-bound nucleus and divide by mitosis. Prokaryotic cells do not have a membrane-bound nucleus and divide by binary fission.

Bacteria can be classified by shape. Cocci are round, bacilli are rod shaped, coccobacilli are oval, vibrio are comma shaped, and spirochetes are spiral. Bacteria can also be classified by spatial arrangement. Diplo means division in pairs, strepto means chains of three or more, and staphylo means grapelike clusters.

Bacterial structure includes the cellular envelope, cytoplasm, and appendages. The cellular envelope includes the cytoplasmic membrane, periplasmic space, cell wall or peptidoglycan, outer membrane, and sometimes a cell capsule. The cytoplasmic membrane is present in Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria and provides selective permeability. The periplasmic space sits between peptidoglycan and the cytoplasmic membrane, contains detoxifying enzymes, secures nutrients, and is larger in Gram-negative bacteria. The cell wall gives the bacterium its shape and is thicker in Gram-positive bacteria. The outer membrane is found in Gram-negative bacteria and gives another selective barrier through porins. A capsule or slime layer may give protection, resist phagocytosis, and allow attachment to surfaces.

The cytoplasm is a gel-like substance containing organelles and DNA/RNA. Appendages include fimbria or pili, which attach bacteria to a host and help establish infection, and flagella, which provide mobility.

Viruses are submicroscopic infectious agents that replicate only inside living cells and cannot reproduce genetic material on their own. A capsid is the shell around viral genetic material. Spikes aid attachment to host cell membrane receptors. A virion is the complete extracellular infectious virus particle.

Bacteriophage multiplication includes adsorption or attachment, penetration, assembly, maturation, and lysis. Viral tropism means the virus can attach only to specific binding sites. During penetration, the virus injects nucleic acid through the bacterial cell wall into the cytoplasm. Some viruses use a tubelike projection and some use digestive enzymes. Syncytia can form when multiple cytoplasms fuse after viral infection. Animal virus replication includes adsorption, penetration by endocytosis, uncoating, replication or macromolecular synthesis, assembly, and release by lysis or budding.

Microbial control requires asking whether the item can be treated without damage, whether heat can be used, whether biofilms are present, whether cleaning is needed before disinfection, and how susceptible or resistant the microbe is. Endospores are dormant, highly resistant structures formed by vegetative bacterial cells in unfavorable environments.

Cleaning removes organic material but does not rid a surface of bacteria. Sterilization eliminates all microbial life, including endospores. Disinfection destroys vegetative life but does not kill endospores. Degermination mechanically removes most microbes from a limited area. Sanitization lowers microbial counts to safe public health levels.

Physical control methods include temperature, osmotic pressure, and radiation. Dry heat exposes an object to direct flame and may require high temperatures and long exposure times. Moist heat uses lower temperatures and shorter exposure times. An autoclave uses steam under pressure and can inactivate fungi, bacteria, viruses, and some spores after equipment has been cleaned. Refrigeration and freezing halt growth of many pathogens and slow metabolism. Osmotic pressure uses a hypertonic solution to dehydrate cells. Radiation damages DNA, causing mutation and cell death, and is mainly used on surfaces, air, and fluids.

Chemical control methods include disinfectants for nonliving surfaces and antiseptics for living tissues. Effectiveness depends on population size, population composition, contact time, concentration, temperature, and organic matter. Phenols and phenolics denature proteins and may irritate skin or airways. Halogens denature enzymes and may be sporicidal with prolonged exposure. Alcohols lack sporicidal activity and denature proteins and lipid membranes. Alkylating agents inactivate nucleic acids and proteins. Glutaraldehyde is used to clean bronchoscopes and requires a 20-minute contact time. Ethylene oxide is a gas sterilizing agent for delicate heat-sensitive instruments, but it has toxic effects and equipment must be properly aerated before patient use.

Gram staining is a common staining technique that identifies Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Cells are stained with crystal violet, decolorized with alcohol, and counterstained with safranin. Blue or purple cells are Gram-positive; pink or red cells are Gram-negative. Acid-fast staining uses carbol fuchsin, decolorization, and methylene blue counterstain. Cells that retain the red stain are acid-fast and this is useful for identifying tuberculosis. Agar differentiation promotes growth of specific organisms while killing others. Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion uses antibiotic or disinfectant disks on agar; a halo is the zone of inhibition. The minimal inhibitory concentration method identifies the lowest drug concentration that inhibits visible growth.

Lab and clinical safety include biosafety, handwashing, eyewash and safety showers, refrigerator/freezer safety, hazardous waste disposal, sharps safety, protective gear, patient care, equipment care, and specimen handling. Proper handwashing with approved soap and warm running water for at least 20 seconds is the simplest and most effective control method. Eyewash and safety showers must be accessible within 10 seconds after hazardous exposure. Lab refrigerators and freezers must be labeled for lab use, not food storage, and contents must be sealed and labeled.

Broken glass goes into a heavy-duty plastic liner in a cardboard box. Biohazardous waste goes into autoclave bags. Sharps go into puncture-resistant biohazard sharps containers. Needles should never be recapped, pointed toward the body, removed by hand from disposable syringes, bent, broken, or manipulated by hand.

Gloves are used for all patients in all settings all of the time. Surgical masks filter large droplets. N95 masks use HEPA filtration for small airborne particles. Clothing should be changed if visibly soiled, and closed-toe puncture-resistant shoes are required. Eye protection is used when splashing of blood, body fluids, secretions, or excretions is possible.

Private rooms are used for patients who contaminate the environment or cannot assist with hygiene. Reusable equipment must not be used for another patient unless properly cleaned. Vent circuits should be clean and free from rainout. HEPA filters protect the ventilator from spreading pathogens. Circuits should be broken as little as possible and exchanged only if visibly soiled or damaged. Covered suction catheters reduce ETT contamination. HMEs should be changed every 4 days or when visibly soiled. Humidifiers should be filled with sterile water and cleaned before refilling. Bronchoscopes require meticulous cleaning because improper cleaning can transmit TB and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Bag-mask ventilation should use a one-way valve or disposable mask and be disposed of after single-patient use.

Specimen handling requires preventing contamination of the outside of the container, disinfecting the external surface, placing the container in an impervious bag, preventing leaks, and labeling all specimens with patient information, collection time, and collection site when appropriate.
