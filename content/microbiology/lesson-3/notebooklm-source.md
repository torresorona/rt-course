# NotebookLM Source: The Immune Response & Lymphatic System

This lesson covers the immune response and lymphatic system. Immunology is the study of the genetic, biological, chemical, and physical characteristics of the immune system. Immunity is the body's ability to respond to the presence of any foreign substance. Innate immunity is nonspecific. Adaptive immunity is specific. Antigens are agents capable of binding specifically to components of the immune system and triggering an immune response.

The major histocompatibility complex, or MHC, recognizes and tolerates self-antigens and rejects foreign antigens. MHC I encodes self-antigen characteristics and aids in recognition of self. It allows natural killer cells and T cells to target infected cells. MHC II receptors recognize foreign antigens and are located on macrophages and B cells. MHC II is involved in presenting exogenous antigens to T cells during the immune response.

MHC matters in transplant rejection because the body may reject transplanted tissue when it does not recognize the MHC as self. Transplant patients may be placed on immunosuppressing medications. Blood types are dictated by which antigens are present on red blood cells.

Antibodies are immunoglobulins. They are Y-shaped, made of constant and variable regions, and contain an antigen-binding site and phagocyte-binding site. Antibodies are produced by B lymphocytes to recognize and bind to foreign antigens. They neutralize antigen, inhibit movement, and cause agglutination, which is bacterial clumping as antibodies bind to antigen. Antibodies do not directly kill antigens. They mark antigen for destruction and signal phagocytes, resulting in phagocytosis.

Major tissues and organs of the immune system include lymphatic tissue, lymphatic vessels, lymphatic nodes, lymphatic nodules, tonsils, spleen, thymus gland, and red bone marrow. The spleen is the largest organ of the lymphatic system.

Leukocytes are white blood cells. Granulocytes include neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, and mast cells. Neutrophils are the most abundant and motile phagocytes, produced in bone marrow, short lived, and generally first to respond to bacterial infection. They migrate to the infection site, engulf invading organisms, and release digestive enzymes from lysosomes to dissolve cellular debris and antigens. Eosinophils increase during allergic disorders, asthma, skin conditions, fungal infections, some cancers, and bone marrow disorders. Basophils are the least common leukocytes and promote inflammation by releasing histamine, heparin, and serotonin. Mast cells are found under skin, near blood vessels and lymph vessels, in lungs, and in intestines. They are produced in bone marrow, long lived, respond to antigens, and contain histamine, heparin, and cytokines.

Agranulocytes include monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes. Monocytes are large circulating phagocytic cells that develop into macrophages when they leave blood vessels during inflammatory reaction. A macrophage is a matured monocyte. Macrophages engulf and destroy bacteria and cellular debris, present digested antigens to lymphocytes using MHC II, and may be wandering or fixed. Fixed macrophages include Kupffer cells in the liver, mesangial cells in the kidneys, and microglial cells in the brain.

Dendritic cells have long extensions, break down foreign matter, and are concentrated where portals of entry are common. Examples include thymus cells that help mature T cells, Langerhans cells in the epidermis, interstitial cells in the GI tract, and interdigitating cells in lymph nodes.

Lymphocytes include B cells, T cells, and natural killer cells. B cells produce antibodies. When antigen binds to antibody on a B cell, the B cell rapidly divides and forms plasma cells and memory B cells. B cells are responsible for antibody-mediated immunity. T cells mature in the thymus gland and are responsible for cellular immunity. Natural killer cells kill infected cells and tumor cells by recognizing MHC I. They help activate macrophages to kill bacteria, attack by recognizing cell dysfunction, release cytotoxins that cause apoptosis, and act similarly to cytotoxic T cells.

Host defense includes defenses present from birth, nonspecific defenses, learned responses, and antigen-specific responses. Innate defense slows replication and spread of infectious agents while adaptive immunity begins.

The first line of defense includes physical barriers, chemical barriers, and mechanical defenses. The skin uses keratinocytes that constantly slough off and are replaced, sweat that mechanically flushes organisms from pores, and skin colonizers. Mucous membranes trap pathogens in mucus, and the mucus is swept away by the ciliary escalator. Tears, saliva, and urine flow help flush bacteria. At the mucocellular level, cells are tightly packed, consistently slough off, and include cells that protect against foreign antigens.

Chemical barriers include skin acidity, oil from hair follicles, lysozymes in tears, saliva, nasal secretions, and sweat, stomach acidity, digestive enzymes, proteolytic enzymes such as pepsin, intestinal alkalinity, and bile salts. Lysozymes break down organism cell walls. Mechanical defenses include crying or tear production, blowing the nose, and movement of intestinal contents.

The second line of defense includes skin-associated lymphoid tissue, mucous-associated lymphoid tissue, phagocytosis, inflammation, fever, interferons, cytokines, and complement. SALT is found within the dermal layer of the skin. MALT is deep to skin and mucous membranes and mediates specific and nonspecific responses. The complement system includes proteins activated by the immune system or by the presence of foreign organisms.

Phagocytosis begins when phagocytes extend pseudopods and surround an organism or debris. This forms a phagosome, which protects the cell from toxic material. The phagosome fuses with a lysosome, and the lysosome releases bactericidal chemicals to break down the organism. The phagocyte then presents antigen protein on the cell surface for lymphocyte recognition through MHC.

The inflammatory response begins when injury to capillaries and tissue cells releases bradykinin. Bradykinin activates sensory nerves and initiates pain. Pain stimulates mast cells and basophils. Histamine and leukotrienes are released. Bradykinin and histamine cause capillary dilation. The coagulation system helps isolate antigen to one area and wall off infection. Neutrophils and monocytes migrate to the injury site and phagocytize microbes.

Signs of inflammation include redness, swelling, heat, and pain. Redness is caused by capillary dilation and increased blood flow. Swelling is caused by capillary dilation allowing increased blood in the area. Heat is caused by increased blood flow and helps increase metabolic rate and healing rate. Pain is caused by prostaglandins. Prostaglandins are lipids secreted by damaged cells that intensify the effects of histamines and kinins.

Fever is a systemic response to extensive inflammation or microbial invasion. It helps slow bacterial growth and infection spread. It is regulated by the hypothalamus in response to pyrogens released from white blood cells. Interferons are glycoproteins produced by cells infected with a virus. They interfere with viral replication and impede pathogen spread. Cytokines are small signaling proteins that act as chemical mediators. The complement system is a series of proteins that function together to rid the body of infection or colonization by the microbe to which it was exposed.

The third line of defense includes cell-mediated immunity and antibody-mediated immunity. Cell-mediated immunity requires activated T cells. Cytotoxic T cells cannot respond to free-floating antigens; they respond only to antigen-presenting cells, recognize MHC I, and release perforin into infected cells. Helper T cells respond to MHC II-presenting cells, release cytokines that stimulate growth and division of cytotoxic T cells and B cells, and attract more macrophages to the area. Suppressor or regulatory T cells release chemicals to suppress further helper T-cell development and plasma B-cell antibody production. Memory T cells persist after suppression of the immune response, recognize a pathogen that previously invaded the body, and secrete cytokines to stimulate macrophages and B cells during repeat invasion.

Antibody-mediated immunity is also called humoral immunity and is the action of B cells. Specific antibodies are produced in response to specific antigen. Specific B cells and memory B cells are produced through clonal selection. Plasma B cells are generally short lived. Memory B cells are long lived and stimulate reproduction of more specific B cells if the same antigen appears again.

Active immunity occurs when antigen enters the body and the body learns how to respond. Specific B cells and memory cells form. Examples include vaccines and natural exposure. Repeat exposure results in faster response because memory cells have already formed. This is the idea behind booster shots. Passive immunity occurs when antibodies are exogenous, meaning from an outside source. Passive immunity lasts only as long as the antibody remains in the bloodstream, but it can help hold off severe infection while the body makes its own antibodies.

Addressing emerging and reemerging diseases requires infection prevention programs with investigation, prevention, control, reporting, and surveillance. Surveillance occurs at state and federal levels as infectious disease cases reported by healthcare providers are collected and analyzed. Information is reported to the CDC. Surveillance determines whether clinical symptoms associated with an incident warrant public attention. Epidemics are often detected through routine surveillance or unusual clusters recognized by healthcare providers, such as rising West Nile cases in an area. Investigation requires quick and precise identification of the pathogen, its transmission, and the appropriate response. Response may include immunization programs, vector control, retraining or education, food recall, isolation, and quarantine.
