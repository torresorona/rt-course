# NotebookLM Source: Fundamentals of Respiratory Care Research

Use only the material in this source. Do not add outside terminology, examples, journal names, software, statistics, or guidelines that are not stated here. Keep the tone conversational and approachable for respiratory therapy students, and quiz the listener naturally as you go. Cover the units below in order, giving the most airtime to reviewing the literature, the steps in research, and the structure of a research article.

This lesson is an introduction to respiratory care research. The big idea is that you may never conduct a study yourself, but you must be familiar with the concepts of research and become an educated consumer of medical research. If you do want to become a respiratory care scientist, the most important first move is to find a mentor, someone who has experience conducting scientific studies and publishing the results. A mentor can help you turn your ideas into practical realities.

Start with why research matters in health care. Academic medicine has three basic missions: heal, teach, and discover. There are four kinds of stakeholders, and you can remember them with the word RACE: researchers, administrators, clinicians, and educators. Researchers need to generate new ideas that inform or benefit the other stakeholders. Administrators need to evaluate the quality of services and the validity of policies and procedures. Clinicians need to assess the usefulness of new procedures, equipment, and treatments. Educators need to find, summarize, and present evidence for clinical activities. The common skill all of these stakeholders must share is the ability to read and critically evaluate published scientific reports. Without research, you cannot have meaningful evaluations of current practices and you cannot plan research.

Next, talk through how to review the literature. Bibliographic databases contain books, book chapters, reports, citations, abstracts, and either the full text of the indexed articles or links to the full text. They provide search filters, automatic e-mailing of search updates, the ability to save records, and result filters using "My NCBI," which stands for the National Center for Biotechnology Information. For search engines, PubMed is the most popular; it is a service of the U.S. National Library of Medicine and includes over 18 million citations from MEDLINE and other life science journals for biomedical articles, dating back to 1948. SearchMedica is another search engine that delivers only the most clinically reputable content intended for practicing medical clinicians. Synthesized databases are the next category, and the Cochrane Collaboration is the leading one. The pros of synthesized databases are pre-filtered records for particular topics and the ability to provide the "best" evidence without extensive searches of standard bibliographic databases; the con is that they are subscription based with relatively large fees. Portals are web pages that act as a starting point for using the Web or web-based services, providing access or links to other sites; examples are MD Consult, which links to books, journals, Clinics in Medicine, patient education resources, and images, and Ovid, which links to books, journals, evidence-based medicine databases, and CINAHL, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature. We are rapidly reaching the point where all medical journals are available online, and some are available only online due to cost restraints; electronic textbooks are also available, and subscription services include Oxford Reference Online, STAT!Ref, Safari Books Online, and the Respiratory Care Journal from the AARC. For general internet resources, Google is the most popular general search engine. Two practical suggestions for conducting searches: talk to a professional librarian, and use bibliographic software, also called reference management software, which is a database of the sources you use when researching your assignments. Examples are EndNote, RefWorks, and Zotero.

Then cover how to start RT research. A good entry point is device evaluation, particularly of a new device. It is usually very inexpensive because vendors often donate or loan equipment and supplies, and it does not require approval by the Institutional Review Board, or IRB. Remember the key distinction: the IRB is required for studies of human subjects.

Next, walk through the factors affecting the feasibility of research, which you can remember with MADSAK: measurability, availability of research subjects, duration and timing of the study, significance or potential benefits to the results, availability of equipment and funds, and knowledge and experience of the investigators.

Now the steps in research. They are: find your motivation, find a mentor, find a good textbook on respiratory care research, develop a study idea, write a study protocol, analyze the data, and publish a scientific paper. To develop a study idea, you choose and define a research topic, which implies concern or doubt about some concept or observation and is usually based on the observer's experience from clinical practice. You then clarify your study purpose through a literature review and develop a formal problem statement. From the problem statement comes either a brief statement of the study purpose or a hypothesis statement, and a hypothesis is a supposition or proposed explanation for an observation. Writing the study protocol helps you clarify the goals of the study and the methods of investigation; permission may need to be sought from a funding source, the IRB, a department manager, or a student advisor before a study may begin, and the protocol provides an operational guide for the entire research team.

For analyzing the data, be familiar with the basics of statistics. Know the mean and standard deviation of a set of numbers, and the p-value, which stands for probability-value. A t-test is used to compare two mean values. Analysis of variance, or ANOVA, is used to compare more than two mean values. A chi-square or Fisher Exact test is used to compare proportions. Make sure the listener hears the distinction: t-test for two means, ANOVA for more than two means, chi-square or Fisher Exact for proportions.

To get published, you communicate results in one of three formal ways: the abstract, the poster, and the paper. The abstract is a condensed version of a research paper that appears at the beginning of the publication; most journals restrict its length, and it should include an introduction, background, study purpose, methods, results, and discussion. A good tip is to start with a longer abstract and then edit, decreasing the word count while increasing clarity. The poster allows a bit more freedom in space than an abstract; it is generally 4 feet tall by 6 feet long, graphics should be at least 300 dots per inch, and posters are usually presented in groups of 10 to 15 in small rooms where visitors walk around reading them and discussing them with the authors. The research paper takes anywhere from 20 to 100 hours, has the same basic outline as an abstract or poster but in much greater detail, and includes a references section; there is no word limit for the Introduction, but most journals cap the full text, usually in the range of 2000 to 3000 words.

Spend good time on the basic structure of a research article, which is abstract or summary, introduction, materials and methods, results, discussion, conclusion, and references. The introduction provides a brief background explaining why the study was conducted and why it is of interest, and includes the research problem or hypothesis. The materials and methods section explains exactly what was done to answer the research question and test the hypotheses described in the introduction. The results section presents the data gathered from the experiments, the "bare facts," with little or no explanation of their significance. The discussion shows how the results answered the research question first described in the introduction and interprets the results in the face of earlier studies' conclusions. The conclusion is briefly explained and includes reasons for rejecting alternate interpretations.

Cover peer review next. A paper is usually reviewed by two or three peer reviewers who read and critique everything about your paper, including what words you use, what measurements you made, and what statistical procedures you used. They make a recommendation to the journal editor, and there are three possible outcomes: the paper should not be considered for publication, it is rejected with an opportunity to revise and resubmit, or it should be accepted as is.

Finish with the types of research. Quantitative research collects data for numerical analysis; an example is "How many men got their physical this year at our hospital?" Qualitative research increases our understanding of why people act the way they do; an example is "Tell me why men aren't getting their physicals this year." Descriptive research observes and describes associations between exposures and outcomes, shows patterns of disease occurrence, records events, and helps to generate hypotheses. Analytic research analyzes associations, investigates relationships, and tests hypotheses, and it is also known as explanatory research. Reinforce the two key contrasts: quantitative is about how many and qualitative is about why, while descriptive research generates hypotheses and analytic research tests them.

To wrap up, remind the listener that everyone practicing respiratory care has the responsibility to intelligently evaluate what they are doing in light of scientific evidence, and that a great way to begin is to find a mentor and start creating basic study ideas and conducting experiments.
