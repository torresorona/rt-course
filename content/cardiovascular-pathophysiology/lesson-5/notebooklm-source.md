# NotebookLM Source: Coronary Artery Disease & Atherosclerosis

This lesson covers development of artery disease. The source objectives are to understand plaque development, understand the normal structure of an artery, and identify risk factors for atherothrombotic diseases.

The source states that when heart disease or coronary artery disease is discussed, atherosclerosis of the arteries is identified as a common cause. Atherogenesis tends to promote hardening of the arteries due to fatty plaque deposits. It is mainly caused by sedentary lifestyle with increased consumption of a high-fat diet. Atherosclerosis is hardening and narrowing of the arteries due to fatty plaques in the arteries. Atheroma is degeneration of arterial walls caused by accumulated fatty deposits and scar tissue. It restricts circulation and increases risk of thrombosis or complete artery blockage.

The process from atherogenesis to atherosclerosis can take a long time and usually occurs naturally as a person ages. In a normal artery, endothelial cells are naturally resistant to plaque buildup. They have protective methods that keep thrombin and blood cells from binding to the wall. The source says this is usually done with a heparin-like substance that helps prevent clots and thrombosis. Smooth muscle in larger arteries contracts and relaxes in a normal arterial wall, helping regulate blood flow through arterial beds.

Three primary structures are affected by plaque buildup within the artery. The tunica intima is the inner lining and is generally thinner than the rest. It includes endothelial cells that are in contact with blood. The tunica media is thicker and includes the smooth muscle that lets arteries contract and relax. The tunica adventitia is the external covering of the artery and contains collagen fibrils in a loose array.

The first steps toward lipid accumulation begin with a diet rich in cholesterol and saturated fat. Small lipoproteins accumulate in the intima of the artery. As lipoproteins attach and enter the tunica intima, a complicated immune response releases cytokines and other materials that attract more cells to the local area. This causes buildup of tissue and low-density lipids.

More cells then attach and bind to endothelial cells that are normally resistant to these adhesions. The source links most lipoprotein binding to the body's immune response, including leukocytes, cytokines, and chemokines that attract more cells to the local area. Complications of blockage can cause turbulent flow and increased likelihood of clot formation, which further complicates the plaque inside the artery. As plaque grows, cell death of the inner arterial portions and hardening of the tunica media and adventitia reduce normal function. As the individual ages and plaque grows, mineralization can occur. Mineralization often occurs in a way similar to normal bone calcification and is usually related to the body's immune response.

Progression of atherosclerotic plaque varies and depends heavily on genetics, lifestyle choices, socioeconomic factors, and age. In most cases, atherosclerosis is discussed based on how plaque affects the heart. Stenosis is abnormal narrowing in a blood vessel or other organ or structure. In coronary disease, stenosis from plaque can impair heart function. Stenosis greater than 60% can cause flow limitations during increased demand. This typically produces chronic stable angina pectoris or intermittent claudication during increased demand.

Plaque disruption causes most coronary events. Plaque can fracture away and block a coronary artery. Plaque can also erode the intima enough to cause thrombosis that blocks the artery. This often leads to an immediate cardiac event and increased likelihood of death.

Although atherosclerosis can occur anywhere in the body, the source says the primary focus of a cardiopulmonary technician is the heart. Diagnosis of infarction in suspected acute ischemic heart disease requires evidence of myocardial necrosis. Ischemia is insufficient blood supply to an organ, usually due to a blocked artery. Infarction is an area of tissue necrosis caused by impairment of arterial or venous blood supply due to mechanical factors such as emboli, thrombi, or plaque.

CAD identification is typically done through EKG, biomarkers, or cardiac catheterization. The source notes that EKG is discussed in Cardiac Diagnostics I and cardiac catheterization is discussed in Cardiac Diagnostics II. Acute myocardial infarction occurs during the period when circulation to a heart region is obstructed and necrosis is occurring.

Troponin is the primary indicator of an acute coronary event or acute myocardial infarction. Troponin is typically elevated 4 to 6 hours after onset of AMI and remains elevated for 8 to 12 days. It should not be the only indicator because other muscular damage or insults can result in false positives. Troponin is done in conjunction with an EKG.

CK2, or creatine kinase, is another molecular indicator. CK2 is typically elevated within 6 to 12 hours after infarction onset. Peak levels occur at 18 to 24 hours and return to baseline after 48 hours. Insults, injuries, and high levels of endurance training can cause false positives. The source states CK2 is a better molecular indicator for acute MIs because its half-life is shorter than troponin.

Risk factors for atherothrombotic diseases include smoking, hypertension, low-density lipoproteins and cholesterol, diabetes, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndromes. Other than advanced age, smoking remains the single most important risk factor for coronary artery disease. Ischemic heart disease underlies 35% to 40% of smoking-related deaths. Smoking effects on the heart are mostly caused by unfavorable effects on blood pressure and sympathetic tone and reduced myocardial oxygen supply. Smoking is also associated with spontaneous platelet aggregation. It complicates adhesion of foreign particles to endothelial cells, increasing the likelihood of plaque attachment and proliferation.

Hypertension is chronic elevated pressure of blood against arterial walls. It is determined by the volume of blood the heart pumps and resistance to blood flow in systemic arteries. The more blood the heart pumps, and the greater the systemic vascular resistance, the higher the blood pressure. Hypertension is a major risk factor for coronary heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease, renal failure, atrial fibrillation, total mortality, loss of cognitive function, and increased incidence of dementia. Observational data indicate that death from coronary heart disease and stroke increases progressively from blood pressure levels as low as 115 mm Hg systolic and 75 mm Hg diastolic. For patients 40 to 70 years old, each increase of 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic doubles cardiovascular disease risk. Prehypertension, defined as systolic blood pressure 120 to 139 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure 80 to 89 mm Hg, is associated with nearly twice the risk of MI and stroke in women compared with normal blood pressure.

Hypertension risk factors include age, ethnicity, family history of hypertension, genetic factors, lower education and socioeconomic status, greater weight, lower physical activity, tobacco use, psychosocial stressors, sleep apnea, and diet.

Of plasma-based atherothrombotic risk factors, LDL cholesterol is the best-established risk factor causally linked to incident MI and cardiovascular death. High LDL cholesterol levels consistently predict risk of future cardiovascular events. Human mutations in the LDL receptor that produce hypercholesterolemia lead to accelerated atherosclerosis. Increased LDL exposure increases the risk of clinical events in older patients. Cholesterol levels measured early in life influence long-term cardiovascular risk. Risk factors for atherosclerosis, including hypercholesterolemia, correlate with autopsy-proven fatty streak and raised lesion formation in the arterial tree. The source notes that LDL can be moved away from the tunica interna by HDL. HDL takes LDL from the blood and plasma environment and moves it to the liver for breakdown, preventing LDL from depositing on vascular walls.

Insulin resistance and diabetes rank among the major cardiovascular risk factors. Diabetes increases metabolic age. Cardiovascular disease risk increases long before clinical diabetes begins. Insulin resistance itself promotes atherosclerosis before frank diabetes. Most metabolic syndromes and diabetic-related disease increase inflammatory markers from the immune system, increasing the likelihood of plaque development.
