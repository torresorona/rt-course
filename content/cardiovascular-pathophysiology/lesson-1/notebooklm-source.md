# NotebookLM Source: Congenital Heart Defects

This lesson covers congenital heart defects. The source defines congenital heart disease as an abnormality in cardiocirculatory structure or function that is present at birth, even if it is discovered much later. Congenital heart malformations usually result from altered embryonic development of a normal structure, or failure of a structure to progress beyond an early stage of embryonic or fetal development. Congenital malformations and congenital diseases are both congenital heart defects, and they may significantly affect heart function throughout life.

Congenital heart defects may occur through genetic disorders or abnormalities at birth, including trisomy or Down syndrome. Environmental toxins, maternal diabetes, alcohol or drug use, and viral infections during pregnancy are also listed causes. Detection in neonates and infants may involve scalp edema, ascites, pericardial effusion, decreased fetal movements, and low birth weight. Preterm infants under 1500 grams, or about 3 pounds, have the highest incidence of patent ductus arteriosus.

The source identifies fetal echocardiography as the diagnostic requirement for congenital heart defects. Fetal echocardiography is ultrasound of the fetal heart and is described as the new standard of care for mothers with increased risk for fetal cardiac anomalies. Other diagnostic tools include transthoracic echocardiography, ECG, auscultation for heart murmur, MRI, CT, and chest radiography. Chest radiography can identify cardiomegaly from atrial and ventricular enlargement.

Acyanotic congenital heart diseases include atrial septal defect, patent foramen ovale, atrioventricular defect, ventricular septal defect, and patent ductus arteriosus. Atrial septal defect is a hole in the septum that separates the right and left atria. Small defects may be found by chance and may never cause symptoms. Most close during infancy or early childhood. ASD pathophysiology includes left-to-right shunt, right-sided heart failure, supraventricular arrhythmias, and possible transient right-to-left shunt. A transient right-to-left shunt may decrease oxygen saturation, cause dyspnea, and allow blood clots to lead to strokes or heart attacks.

The source lists ASD types as ostium secundum, ostium primum, sinus venosus, and coronary sinus ASD. Ostium secundum is the most common and occurs in the middle of the atrial septum. Ostium primum occurs in the lower atrial septum and may occur with other congenital heart problems. Sinus venosus is rare and occurs in the upper atrial septum. Coronary sinus ASD is rare and occurs when part of the wall between the coronary sinus and left atrium fails to develop.

Patent foramen ovale is a tunnel-like space between the overlying septum secundum and septum primum. The foramen ovale is part of normal fetal circulation in utero and lets blood flow from the right atrium to the left atrium because lung function is unnecessary before birth. Most close at birth by fusion of the septum primum and septum secundum. PFO has the same pathology as ASD. It is primarily a left-to-right shunt, but transient right-to-left shunt is possible and can cause decreased oxygen saturation and paradoxical embolism, including stroke and heart attacks.

Ventricular septal defect is a hole in the ventricular septum. It can be located in different areas of the septum, with outlet VSDs listed as the most common. Small VSDs may cause no symptoms and may close on their own. Large VSDs may need surgical repair. VSD allows blood to pass from the left to the right side of the heart, so oxygenated blood returns to the lungs instead of going out to the body. This causes the heart to work harder. Clinical manifestations include pulmonary hypertension, increased right ventricular workload, and right ventricular hypertrophy. Adults may show cyanosis and clubbing from chronic low PaO2. Infants may show decreased oxygen saturation and pulmonary hypertension.

Atrioventricular septal defect is a combination of defects affecting the center of the heart. It involves the valves between the atria and ventricles and the septal walls between the chambers. Defects may involve the atria, ventricles, and malformed mitral and tricuspid valves. There may be a common valve for both the right and left side. Clinical manifestations include decreased oxygen saturation from left-to-right shunting, pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular dilation, and heart failure.

The ductus arteriosus is part of normal fetal circulation. A right-to-left shunt is needed to bypass the lungs in utero. The ductus arteriosus closes after birth because of pressure changes in the heart and lungs, pulmonary vascular resistance becoming less than systemic vascular resistance, increased blood oxygen levels, and decreased prostaglandin levels. After birth, it normally closes within two or three days. In premature infants it may take longer. If it remains open, it is called patent ductus arteriosus.

PDA risk factors include premature birth, family history of heart defects or genetic conditions such as Down syndrome, infections during pregnancy, and high-altitude births. Clinical manifestations include systolic murmur, bounding peripheral pulses with wide pulse pressure, respiratory distress, and poor weight gain. PDA pathophysiology includes left-to-right shunt, pulmonary hypertension, congestive heart failure, left or right or both ventricular hypertrophy, and cardiomegaly.

Some PDAs are duct dependent and are necessary for survivability. PDA or ASD can prevent complete cyanosis. PDA-dependent pulmonary circulation examples include pulmonary atresia or stenosis, atrioventricular defect, and tetralogy of Fallot. PDA-dependent systemic circulation examples include coarctation of the aorta, atrioventricular defect, and hypoplastic left heart.

Treatment or correction for acyanotic defects may include closure device by cardiac catheterization, interventional catheterization with coil placement, surgical closure in severe cases, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs to block prostaglandin effects.

Cyanotic heart diseases include tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, tricuspid atresia, truncus arteriosus, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, coarctation of the aorta, pulmonary atresia, and Eisenmenger syndrome.

Tetralogy of Fallot has four defects: pulmonary artery stenosis or right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, right ventricular hypertrophy, outlet VSD, and overriding aorta greater than 50%. The initial defect is narrowing of the right ventricular outflow tract into the pulmonary artery. This impedes blood flow to the pulmonary arteries and lungs. Right ventricular outflow tract obstruction causes right ventricular hypertrophy because the ventricle must contract forcefully enough to push blood past the stenosis. Initially the VSD can produce a left-to-right shunt, but severe obstruction can force blood from the right ventricle into the left ventricle, creating right-to-left shunt and cyanosis. The overriding aorta straddles the VSD and lets deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle mix with blood from the left ventricle and go to the body.

TOF risk factors include viral illness during pregnancy such as rubella, alcohol use during pregnancy, poor nutrition during pregnancy, a parent with tetralogy of Fallot, and genetic disorders. Symptoms include low blood oxygen, shortness of breath and rapid breathing during feeding or exercise, poor weight gain, tiring easily, irritability, prolonged crying, heart murmur, fainting, and digital clubbing from chronic hypoxemia. Diagnosis includes echocardiography and x-ray showing enlarged right ventricle, described as boot sign. Treatment includes prostaglandins and surgical correction, usually during the first year of life.

Hypoplastic left heart syndrome means all structures on the left side of the heart are severely underdeveloped. It is characterized by severely underdeveloped left ventricle, atresia or stenosis of the aortic or mitral orifices, hypoplasia of the aorta, ASD, and PDA. It has duct-dependent systemic blood flow and severe symptoms during the first week of life. PDA and ASD prevent infant death. Correction includes prostaglandin to prevent closure of the PDA, staged surgeries, and possible heart transplant. The source lists Norwood-Sano in the first two weeks, Glenn at 3 to 6 months, and Fontan at 18 months to 4 years.

Coarctation of the aorta is a narrowing or constriction in the aorta. It is often near the ductus arteriosus, may be pre-ductal or post-ductal, and may occur with other heart defects, bicuspid aortic valves, or genetic disorders. Post-ductal coarctation is listed as 30% and adult. The PDA is closed, upper extremity blood pressure is high, and lower extremity blood pressure is low. Pre-ductal coarctation is listed as 70% and pediatric. The PDA is open, and lower extremity cyanosis may occur. Diagnosis can include echocardiography, cardiac catheterization, upper and lower body blood pressure differences, weak or delayed femoral pulses, upper and lower extremity SpO2 differences, murmur, and chest x-ray. Correction includes surgery, balloon dilation, and balloon dilation with stent.

Tricuspid atresia usually has right ventricular hypoplasia and a non-existent tricuspid valve. Blood circulates through an ASD or VSD. It resembles hypoplastic left heart syndrome, but the right chambers are the main issue. Constant mixing causes cyanosis and low blood oxygen. Patients typically receive a shunt through systemic-to-pulmonary shunting or staged procedures that avoid use of the right side of the heart.

Truncus arteriosus is an anomaly where a single vessel forms the outlet of both ventricles and gives rise to the systemic, pulmonary, and coronary arteries. It is always accompanied by a VSD. Pulmonary blood flow is governed by the size of the pulmonary arteries and pulmonary vascular resistance. After birth, cyanosis of the extremities and lips may be noticed. Clinical manifestation includes cyanosis from right-to-left shunt and excessive pulmonary blood flow due to lower pulmonary vascular resistance than systemic vascular resistance. Echocardiography can reveal cardiomegaly. Correction includes VSD patch, creating a new aorta using the truncus, and placing a shunt and artificial valve to create a new pulmonary artery and valve.

Pulmonary atresia is closely related to TOF. It involves an underdeveloped right outflow tract or absent pulmonary valve, underdeveloped right ventricle, right-to-left shunt, ASD, PDA, and sometimes VSD. Reduced pulmonary outflow decreases blood flow to the lungs and causes persistent pulmonary artery hypertension. Pulmonary vascular resistance can remain greater than systemic vascular resistance, causing persistent right-to-left shunt and persistent fetal circulation. This results in cyanosis, low saturations, and low PaO2. Correction is surgery.

Transposition of the great arteries means the aorta and pulmonary artery are switched. The source describes two closed circuits. Blood returning from the body flows through the right heart and is pumped through the aorta back to the body. Blood returning from the lungs flows through the left heart and is pumped back to the lungs. VSD or ASD usually accompanies the disease and allows some oxygenation. This is a cyanotic heart defect with severe oxygenation deficits. Severity depends on the size of the VSD and ASD and shunting within the heart. Correction is surgery.

Eisenmenger syndrome is a group of symptoms caused by congenital heart defects with large septal defects that create a left-to-right shunt. Left-to-right shunting leads to severe pulmonary arterial hypertension and elevated vascular resistance. With increased pulmonary vascular resistance, the shunt becomes right-to-left, causing significant hypoxemia and cyanosis. Uncorrected defects produce increased right ventricular pressure, increased pulmonary artery pressure, and right-to-left shunt. Causes include atrioventricular canal defect, ASD, VSD, and PDA, with VSD listed as the most common cause. Symptoms include cyanosis, digital clubbing, polycythemia, arrhythmias, fatigue, dyspnea, and shortness of breath at rest. Treatment includes antiarrhythmics, blood thinners, and drugs to reduce pulmonary hypertension such as bosentan, nitric oxide, and sildenafil.
