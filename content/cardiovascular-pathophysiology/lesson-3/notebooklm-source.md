# NotebookLM Source: Cardiomyopathies

This lesson covers cardiomyopathies. The source objective is to differentiate dilated, restrictive, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathies. Cardiomyopathies are defined as a heterogeneous group of diseases of the myocardium associated with mechanical and/or electrical dysfunction. They usually, but not always, show inappropriate ventricular hypertrophy or dilation and are due to a variety of causes that are frequently genetic.

The source describes dilated cardiomyopathy as too thin, restrictive cardiomyopathy as stiffened, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy as thickened. Cardiomyopathies can lead to dysrhythmias, backup of blood into the lungs or the rest of the body, and heart failure.

Causes can be genetic or acquired and secondary to another disease or condition. Listed causes include chronic hypertension, myocardial damage from a heart attack, chronic tachycardia, heart valve problems, infections that cause inflammation of the heart, metabolic disorders, obesity, thyroid disease, diabetes, granulomas from sarcoidosis, iron buildup in the myocardium from hemochromatosis, buildup of abnormal proteins in the organs from amyloidosis, and pregnancy complications.

Dilated cardiomyopathy is defined as a dilated chamber with systolic dysfunction that is not caused by ischemic or valvular heart disease. It often begins in the left ventricle, causing it to dilate and stretch. This thins the muscle fibers in the walls. As the left ventricle fails, the rest of the heart tries to compensate for the left-sided failure. This leads to further dilation and weakening of the other chambers and outflow tracts.

Symptoms of dilated cardiomyopathy include dyspnea, fatigue, edema of the feet, legs, abdomen, and jugular veins, weight gain, cough and congestion related to fluid retention, heart arrhythmias, dizziness, and syncope. Diagnosis can include ECG, chest x-ray, echocardiography or TEE, and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.

Restrictive cardiomyopathy is the rarest form of cardiomyopathy. It is a condition in which the walls of the ventricles are abnormally rigid and lack flexibility to expand as the ventricles fill. Systolic function may be normal, but diastolic function is abnormal. The ventricles have more difficulty filling with blood. Over time, the heart loses the ability to pump properly, leading to heart failure.

Restrictive cardiomyopathy is not usually inherited, and the cause is often unknown. Known causes include scar tissue buildup, listed as idiopathic and most common; abnormal proteins in the heart muscle from amyloidosis; chemotherapy or chest exposure to radiation; excess iron in the heart from hemochromatosis; and other systemic diseases such as sarcoidosis.

Infiltrative cardiomyopathy is characterized by deposition of abnormal substances that cause ventricular walls to become progressively rigid, impeding ventricular filling. It is caused by infiltration of an abnormal substance. The source lists pathogens, identifies infiltrative cardiomyopathy as a type of restrictive cardiomyopathy, and says it can be characterized by thickened walls and occasional dilation.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy involves hypertrophy or thickening of the ventricles. The source lists left ventricular stiffness, mitral valve changes, myocardial cellular changes, and the phrase "athletes' heart." Thickening of the myocardium occurs most commonly at the septum. The thickened septum causes narrowing that reduces blood flow from the left ventricle to the aorta. This is called outflow tract obstruction. The ventricles must pump harder to overcome the narrowing, and the source terms this hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy.

Left ventricular stiffness results from cellular changes in the myocardium due to hypertrophy. The left ventricle is unable to relax and fill with blood. Because there is less blood at end diastole, less oxygenated blood is pumped to organs and muscles.

Mitral valve changes occur because narrowing of the left ventricular outflow tract disrupts proper mitral valve function. Outflow obstruction and increased left ventricular pressure occur. The obstruction results from the mitral valve striking the septum. This can make the mitral valve leak, causing blood to go back into the left atrium.

Cellular changes in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy include myocytes that appear disorganized and irregular instead of organized and parallel. This can change electrical signals traveling through the lower chambers of the heart and can lead to ventricular arrhythmia. Diagnostics include echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. Symptoms of heart failure may develop unpredictably at any age, with functional limitation due to exertional dyspnea or fatigue. Orthopnea or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea may also occur.
