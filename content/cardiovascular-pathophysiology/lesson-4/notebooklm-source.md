# NotebookLM Source: Inflammatory Heart Diseases

This lesson covers inflammatory heart diseases. The source objective is to differentiate endocarditis, myocarditis, and pericarditis. Endocarditis is inflammation of the inner lining of the heart chambers, called the endocardium. Myocarditis is inflammation of the heart muscle, called the myocardium. Pericarditis is inflammation of the outer lining of the heart, called the pericardium.

Infective endocarditis is a heterogeneous disease process that typically includes several different disease processes leading to heart dysfunction. Patients often develop several bacterial infections, described as polybacterial, which can lead to progressive heart dysfunction. Biofilm produced by bacterial species allows attachment to the wall, eventually creating vegetation and large growths within the heart. Vegetation may eventually lead to tissue breakdown, valve obstruction, turbulent flow, and higher incidence of thrombosis.

Patients with infective endocarditis typically have a history of previous infective endocarditis, chronic intravenous access, intravenous drug abuse, indwelling endocavitary devices, diabetes mellitus type 2, underlying malignancy, renal failure requiring hemodialysis, and chronic immunosuppressive therapy.

Symptoms of infective endocarditis are varied, and some patients show no symptoms. This makes identification difficult, especially because non-specific symptoms such as chills, sweats, cough, and headache are common in other disease processes. Chest angina may occur. Arm pain, back pain, and abdominal pain are known symptoms when a patient is having a heart attack and may occur during infective endocarditis. Some patients present with a murmur. An audible murmur is heard in most patients on presentation, especially with left-sided infective endocarditis.

Echocardiography is first-line detection for endocarditis. Transthoracic echocardiogram can detect most cases. Transesophageal echocardiogram may be a better choice because it provides a better view of valves and improves detection of lesions and vegetation. Patients suspected of endocarditis, with or without blood cultures, may receive TEE or TTE to detect valvular vegetations. TEE or TTE may also be used for valve lesions, reevaluation with high-risk features such as virulent organisms, clinical deterioration, or new murmur, and prosthetic valves.

Lab values, especially blood samples, may be ineffective in detecting infective endocarditis. White blood cell levels typically stay the same, making infection difficult to detect. Serum creatinine may be elevated. EKG findings are likely nonspecific. The source states most infective endocarditis is identified by cardiac abscesses and blood culturing. Other criteria include vegetation of a valve or implanted material on the heart wall, abscess, and new or partial dehiscence of a prosthetic valve. Treatment includes antibiotics based on the specific infection and surgery on an affected implanted device to remove it or the vegetation.

Myocarditis refers to any inflammation of the myocardium. It is caused primarily by injury or external antigens such as bacteria, viruses, drugs, or toxins. Autoimmune disease can also lead to myocarditis. Myocarditis has a wide range of possible clinical presentations, which makes diagnosis and classification difficult. Presentations may include asymptomatic ECG or echocardiographic abnormalities, chest pain, cardiac dysfunction, arrhythmias, heart failure, and hemodynamic collapse.

Acute myocarditis commonly presents with fatigue, dyspnea on exertion, arrhythmias, palpitations, and chest pain. It is usually idiopathic but may be viral or traumatic. Fulminant myocarditis has abrupt onset within days of viral illness. Patients often have hemodynamic compromise and hypotension, requiring pressors or mechanical support. Echocardiograms show hypofunction with ventricular thickening. Acute myocarditis has indistinct onset over weeks or months and may eventually resolve. Fulminant myocarditis has distinct onset within 1 or 2 days, often after viral illness.

Giant cell myocarditis has a distinctive pattern of giant cells with active inflammation and scar tissue. Early immunosuppressive therapy can prolong life, but giant cell myocarditis is often caused by or occurs with autoimmune disorders such as thymoma and Crohns disease. Cardiac transplantation or mechanical support remain treatments for this disease process.

Chronic active myocarditis includes patients with borderline myocarditis. Borderline means the inflammatory infiltrate is few and injury to the heart myocytes is not demonstrated. The cause is idiopathic but thought to be immune-related. It is usually seen in older populations with ventricular dysfunction, fatigue, and dyspnea. Chronic myopathic changes with fibrosis and myocyte dropout occur, and fibrosis eventually restricts diastolic function.

Eosinophilic myocarditis usually results from a foreign antigen, typically a drug. Treatment focuses on the underlying condition and may resolve once the offending agent is removed or treated. Peripartum myocarditis is characterized by left ventricular dysfunction in the last month of pregnancy or within 5 months after delivery in a patient with no preexisting cardiac dysfunction and no underlying cause of cardiomyopathy.

Diagnosis of myocarditis is difficult because symptoms are broad and non-specific. Histological examination of the heart is often the only definitive method of identifying myocarditis. Three diagnostics may help: unexplained rise in troponin, ECG changes suggestive of acute myocardial injury, and identified heart dysfunction on echocardiogram, TEE, or CMR. Lab testing usually shows cardiac injury biomarkers in patients with acute viral illnesses. Elevated troponin is generally noted in most types of myocarditis. Assessment of left ventricular function is essential in suspected myocarditis and is performed with cardiac imaging. Echocardiography is an excellent choice, although there are no specific echocardiographic features. Some ventricular dilation with reduced systolic function may appear.

Acute pericarditis is symptoms or signs from pericardial inflammation lasting no more than 1 to 2 weeks. It can occur from many diseases but is often considered idiopathic. Idiopathic means no specific cause is found until routine diagnostic testing is done. Most idiopathic cases are presumed viral. Acute pericarditis accounts for some emergency room patients with nonischemic chest pain. It usually presents with acute chest pain that is relieved by sitting forward. Uncomplicated cases may have a rub heard through auscultation during ventricular systole and early diastolic filling. The source says to have the patient lean forward and listen at the lower left sternal border for a sound like crunchy snow. EKG may show ST segment elevation, which must be differentiated from myocardial infarction. Treatment includes NSAIDs and corticosteroids.

Pericardial effusions and tamponade can be caused by idiopathic pericarditis, infection, neoplasm, autoimmune or inflammatory processes, penetrating or blunt trauma, cardiac surgery, and percutaneous cardiac procedures. Tamponade occurs when pressure within the pericardial sac increases enough to affect the heart's ability to work. Clinical features include Beck's triad: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and elevated jugular venous pressure. Patients with pericardial effusions are usually more comfortable sitting forward. Reduced cardiac output, reduced blood pressure, pericardial pain, nonspecific discomfort, and paradoxical pulse may occur. Identification includes echocardiography, ECG, and chest x-ray. Management depends on whether tamponade is present or may occur. Treatment may include pericardiocentesis with corticosteroids. The most common closed pericardiocentesis approach is subxiphoid needle insertion under echocardiographic guidance.

Constrictive pericarditis is the end stage of an inflammatory process involving the pericardium. In the developed world, causes are commonly idiopathic, postsurgical, or radiation injury. Tuberculosis was the most common cause before effective drug therapy and remains important in developing countries. Pericardial disease can eventually cause scarring that impedes the heart's ability to fill the chambers. Anti-inflammatory drugs can relieve some patients. Pathology includes marked restriction of chamber filling, elevated and equal filling pressure of all chambers, and pressure elevation in systemic and pulmonary veins. This can lead to impaired filling, fatigue, muscle wasting, and weight loss. Diagnosis may show an enlarged heart on chest x-ray, but ECG may not detect it. M-mode, two-dimensional transthoracic, and Doppler echocardiographic techniques are key imaging modalities. Effusive-constrictive pericarditis combines elements of effusion or tamponade and constriction, with causes including idiopathic disease, malignancy, radiation, tuberculosis, pericardiotomy, and connective tissue diseases.
