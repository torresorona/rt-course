# NotebookLM Source: Valvular Dysfunctions

This lesson covers valvular dysfunction. The source objective is to differentiate valvular stenosis, regurgitation, and prolapse. Stenosis is defined as abnormal narrowing or contraction of a body passage or opening. It is also called arctation, coarctation, and stricture. Regurgitation is backflow of blood into the previous chamber of the heart caused by valves that have been damaged or are stenotic. Prolapse is when heart valve leaflets slip or are pushed out of place by different pressures on the valve leaflets.

Aortic stenosis occurs when the aortic valve narrows from malformation or calcification. This reduces blood flow from the left ventricle through the aorta to the rest of the body. Without treatment, severe aortic valve stenosis can lead to death. Causes include calcific aortic valve, which is the most common cause in adults, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, rheumatic aortic stenosis from rheumatic fever, and bicuspid aortic valve leading to obstruction and calcification of the outflow tract. Rheumatic fever is a condition caused by Streptococcus bacteria that leads to inflammation in the body, including the heart.

Aortic stenosis pathophysiology includes increased left ventricular filling pressures, left ventricular hypertrophy, increased left atrial pressures, left atrial dilation, decreased oxygen supply to the heart that may worsen ischemia, and progression to congestive heart failure. Symptoms include syncope, angina, dyspnea, weak pulses, decreased pulse pressure, systolic murmur, and arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation.

Mitral stenosis is narrowing of the heart's mitral valve. The valve does not open properly, reducing blood flow into the left ventricle. Causes include rheumatic fever, which the source lists as most common, calcium deposits from aging, radiation therapy for cancer treatment, and rare causes such as congenital defect and autoimmune disease such as lupus. Mitral stenosis pathophysiology includes left atrial enlargement, pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary edema, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and blood clots from atrial fibrillation that can cause stroke. Symptoms include abnormal heart sounds or murmur, shortness of breath with activity or lying down, fatigue with activity, chest discomfort or chest pain, peripheral edema, palpitations, arrhythmias, atrial fibrillation, coughing up blood, dizziness, syncope, pulmonary hypertension, and pulmonary edema.

Tricuspid stenosis is narrowing or calcification of the valve between the right atrium and right ventricle. It reduces blood flow across the valve. The source states that this condition causes decreased pulmonary vascular resistance and increased systemic vascular resistance, leading to symptoms such as ascites and jugular vein distention. Causes include rheumatic heart disease, rheumatic fever, and carcinoid syndrome. The source notes that rheumatic disease also affects the mitral and pulmonic valves. Tricuspid stenosis pathophysiology includes increased right atrial volume and pressure, right atrial dilation, right heart failure, jugular venous distension, ascites, hepatomegaly, pedal edema, atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter, and clotting leading to pulmonary embolism.

Pulmonic stenosis is narrowing, calcification, or malformation of the valve between the right ventricle and pulmonary arteries. It reduces blood flow across the valve. Congenital pulmonic stenosis is listed as the most common form. Examples include tetralogy of Fallot or a bicuspid valve. Other causes include rheumatic fever, rubella, and carcinoid cancer. Carcinoid heart disease is characterized by fibrous plaque deposition on cardiac valves and chambers. It affects right heart valves in 85% and left heart valves in 15%. Pulmonic stenosis pathophysiology includes reduced blood flow to the lungs, right ventricular hypertrophy, right ventricular dilation, and heart failure. Symptoms include abnormal heart sounds or murmur, fatigue, shortness of breath during activity, chest pain, and syncope.

Aortic regurgitation occurs when the valve between the left ventricle and aorta does not close properly, allowing blood to leak backward into the left ventricle. Causes include aortic root calcification, which the source lists as most common, congenital valve defects such as bicuspid valve, infective endocarditis, rheumatic fever, and trauma to the heart. Aortic regurgitation pathophysiology includes backflow into the left ventricle, increased left ventricular end-diastolic volume, left ventricular hypertrophy and enlargement, increased myocardial force and oxygen requirements to maintain stroke volume, and heart failure. Symptoms include fatigue, shortness of breath, syncope, and widened pulse pressure.

Mitral regurgitation occurs when the valve between the left ventricle and left atrium does not close properly, so blood leaks backward into the left atrium. Causes include mitral valve prolapse, rheumatic fever, heart attack, congenital heart defect, cardiomyopathy, damaged chordae tendineae, and endocarditis. Mitral regurgitation pathophysiology includes dilation of the left atrium, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary hypertension, and congestive heart failure in severe cases. The heart works harder to pump blood to the body, which can make the left ventricle hypertrophy. Untreated, the heart muscle becomes weak and heart failure can occur.

Tricuspid regurgitation occurs when the valve between the right ventricle and right atrium does not close properly. Blood leaks backward into the right atrium. Causes include other heart dysfunctions, tricuspid stenosis, pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular dysfunction, myocardial infarction with damaged papillary muscles and chordae tendineae, IV drug abuse with endocarditis from S. aureus infection, and congenital heart defects such as Ebstein anomaly. In Ebstein anomaly, the tricuspid valve sits lower than normal, part of the right ventricle becomes part of the right atrium, and the right atrium enlarges. Tricuspid regurgitation pathophysiology includes increased right atrial volume, right atrial dilation, right heart failure, and arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter. Symptoms are often absent until severe, but can include fatigue, shortness of breath with activity, arrhythmias, ascites, peripheral edema, and jugular venous distension.

Pulmonic regurgitation is found mostly in patients with congenital heart diseases or surgeries. It may also come from congenital malformations such as leaflet anomalies, trauma, and infective endocarditis. Pulmonic regurgitation pathophysiology includes increased right ventricular volume, right ventricular dilation, right heart failure, and arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter.

Mitral valve prolapse is primarily a problem of the valves between the atria and ventricles, most commonly the mitral valve. It is also called Barlow syndrome. Causes include damage to left ventricular anatomical structures, leaflet dysfunction, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, genetic factors, Ebstein anomaly, Marfan syndrome, Graves disease, and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Mitral valve prolapse can eventually lead to regurgitation into the left atrium. Severity depends on the valve dysfunction. Most patients are asymptomatic until they are older.

Diagnostics for valvular dysfunction include transthoracic echocardiogram, transesophageal echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, chest x-ray, cardiac catheterization, EKG, and heart sounds. The source calls transthoracic echocardiogram the best diagnostic tool. Transesophageal echocardiogram gives a better view of the valves and upper chambers. EKG can show abnormal waveforms. Heart sounds may include murmurs, clicks from late closing valves, new heart sounds, and extra rushing.

Management and treatment may include diuretics to reduce edema in the lungs and systemically, anticoagulants to prevent blood clots, heart rate control to allow the heart to fill more effectively, antiarrhythmics to treat atrial fibrillation, and antibiotics to treat rheumatic fever. Treatment options include balloon valvuloplasty, valve replacement, right heart failure treatment with fluid restriction and diuretics, and symptom management for mitral valve prolapse. Mechanical valve replacement is listed for patients under 65, patients with atrial fibrillation, and patients who must stay on anticoagulants for life. Bioprosthetic valves are listed for patients over 65 and may be bovine, porcine, or cadaver valves. Patients may need antibiotics to prevent valve infections.
