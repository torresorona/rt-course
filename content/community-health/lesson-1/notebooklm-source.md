# NotebookLM Source: Alternative Care Settings

This lesson focuses on respiratory care in alternative, non-acute, and post-acute care settings. The Affordable Care Act was implemented in 2010. It expanded health coverage to many uninsured Americans, prohibited exclusion of preexisting conditions, and promoted coordinated care. In these settings, respiratory therapists help reduce cost and achieve better patient outcomes.

Respiratory care in non-acute and post-acute facilities commonly includes continuous O2 therapy, long-term mechanical ventilation, aerosol drug therapy, airway care, sleep apnea treatment and monitoring, and pulmonary rehabilitation. Continuous O2 therapy is the most common. Alternative care settings include home care, subacute facilities, rehabilitation facilities, and skilled nursing facilities. Home care is the most common alternative care setting. Comprehensive inpatient care is used for stable patients who have experienced an acute event from injury or illness, have a determined course of treatment, and require diagnostics or invasive procedures. Patients of all age groups can be found at these sites.

Most post-acute respiratory care is provided in the home. Common clinical conditions treated at home include COPD, cystic fibrosis, chronic neuromuscular diseases, chronic restrictive disease, and carcinoma of the lung.

Standards for respiratory care in subacute and home settings are derived from American Association of Respiratory Care clinical practice guidelines, federal and state laws, and private-sector accreditation standards. Medicare plays a major role in setting standards for care outside the hospital. Institutions undergo certification surveys to determine compliance with standards, and each state has regulations for quality assurance. The Joint Commission is the primary organization responsible for patient care standards in the subacute care setting, and approximately 90% of health care organizations voluntarily subscribe to The Joint Commission accreditation.

Respiratory therapists in alternative care settings have less equipment and fewer resources, work more independently, complete more paperwork, are often on-call, and work as part of a team approach. Team members may include a provider, nurse, respiratory therapist, and durable medical equipment supplier.

Discharge planning is supported by a multidisciplinary team. The team helps with discharge and with achieving the best patient results. The correct discharge site is determined by the patient's needs. If the patient is discharged home, caregivers must be trained and prepared, and a reliable durable medical equipment supplier may need to be involved.

Oxygen therapy in alternative settings requires a prescription that includes diagnosis, flow rate in liters per minute and/or concentration, frequency of use, duration of need, and laboratory evidence such as ABGs. The most common long-term oxygen delivery system is the nasal cannula. Transtracheal O2 therapy is used in select patients and conserves oxygen while offering cosmetic advantages. Demand-flow systems, also called pulsed-dose systems, are also oxygen-conserving devices.

Oxygen supply methods include compressed O2 cylinders, liquid O2 systems, and oxygen concentrators. Small E and D compressed cylinders are used for ambulation and transports. Large H and K cylinders are used as backup to liquid or concentrator systems. Liquid O2 systems provide a large quantity of oxygen in a small space. Liquid O2 is approximately -300 degrees F, and pressures when not in use are between 20 and 25 psi. Oxygen concentrators separate O2 from room air, and the molecular sieve is the most common concentrator in use.

Ventilator support in alternative settings begins with patient selection and choosing the appropriate ventilator. Noninvasive ventilator support is increasing in popularity because it is easier to run, patients are cooperative, and FiO2 is 40% or lower. Ventilator choice depends on the patient's needs. A backup ventilator is required for patients who cannot maintain spontaneous ventilation for more than 4 hours. Home ventilators should be dependable, easy to operate, mobile if the patient is mobile, and electrically powered when dependability is needed.

Most patients, especially those with COPD, prefer pressure-limited ventilation over volume-cycled ventilation. The biggest challenge with noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation is getting a good, comfortable, leak-free interface. Negative-pressure ventilators are rarely used for ventilatory support in post-acute care settings. The iron lung has been replaced with the chest cuirass and wrap or pneumosuit.

Evaluation and follow-up include daily monitoring of vital signs, lung sounds, and sputum production. ABGs and lung compliance are monitored only as needed. Routine respiratory therapist follow-up visits help ensure successful home patient management.

Other post-acute respiratory care modes include bland aerosol therapy, aerosol drug administration, airway care, clearance methods, and nasal CPAP. Bland aerosol therapy is delivered by jet or ultrasonic nebulizers, may be intermittent or continuous, and may help patients with thick secretions as an adjunct to airway clearance procedures. Infection is the primary risk. Aerosol drug administration may include bronchodilators and anti-inflammatory medications delivered by metered-dose inhalers, dry powder inhalers, or small volume nebulizers. Reimbursable expenses for aerosol drug therapy in the home are limited.

Patients with tracheostomies require daily care. Tube changes should be done only by a nurse, respiratory therapist, or physician. Suctioning is provided using a portable electrically powered suction pump. Patients can be taught coughing, forced exhalation, active cycle of breathing, and autogenic drainage methods. Caregivers may apply chest physical therapy manually or with mechanical devices when retained secretions become problematic.

Nasal CPAP is an accepted form of therapy to treat obstructive sleep apnea. Medicare reimbursement requires the diagnosis to be confirmed by a polysomnogram.

Assessment and documentation in subacute or long-term care institutions include screening, treatment planning, ongoing assessment, and discharge. In home care, visit frequency depends on the patient's condition and therapeutic needs, family or caregiver support, equipment complexity, the home environment, and the patient's ability to provide self-care. Respiratory therapist home visits include patient assessment, treatment-plan compliance, equipment assessment, identification of problems, and documentation related to patient goals and the treatment plan.

Palliative care is another home care goal. It maximizes comfort for terminally ill patients by controlling symptoms such as pain and dyspnea while maximizing psychological and spiritual well-being. Respiratory therapists may play a key role when lung disease is present in the terminally ill patient.
